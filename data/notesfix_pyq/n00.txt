 • The oldest of the Deccan ruling dynasties: Satavahanas
    The Satavahanas (about 2nd century BCE-3rd century CE) were the first major Deccan dynasty, before the
    Chalukyas, Rashtrakutas and Cholas.
    Jr. Stenos AP Vaidya Vidhana Parishad 2012 (B2 p.251 Q63)

 • Ptolemy described Maisolia (Machilipatnam) as a great market
    Ptolemy's Geography (2nd century CE) mentions Maisolia at the mouth of the Krishna (Maisolos), usually identified
    with the Machilipatnam region.
    FROs 03.06.2012 (B2 p.251 Q64)

 • Motupalli was not a Satavahana-era port
    Motupalli rose to importance under the Kakatiyas. Satavahana ports included Kalyan, Sopara and Broach in the west
    and Koduru, Ghantasala and Maisolia in the east.
    DL 2012 (B2 p.252 Q75)

 • Yajnasri Satakarni's religious leaning: patron of Buddhism.
    Tradition says Yajnasri built a monastery at Sriparvata for Acharya Nagarjuna. The Satavahanas were mainly Vedic,
    but several rulers supported Buddhist establishments.
    Trap: Jainism, Shaivism and Vaishnavism are the distractor faiths.
    DL 2012 (B2 p.252 Q76)

 • Katantra Vyakarana was composed by Sarvavarma
    Sarvavarma, at the Satavahana court (traditionally of Hala), wrote this simplified Sanskrit grammar. Gunadhya wrote
    Brihatkatha in Paishachi Prakrit and Hala compiled Gathasaptashati.
    Group-1 Prelims 2010 (Oct) (B3 p.137 Q56)

 • The Satavahanas were the first major dynasty to rule Andhra
    Founded by Simuka, they ruled from Pratishthana and Dhanyakataka (Amaravati). Gautamiputra Satakarni was the
    greatest ruler.
    Group-1 Prelims 2010 (Oct) (B3 p.139 Q101)


 Ikshvakus & Post-Satavahana Dynasties (39)
 EARLY PALLAVAS IN ANDHRA

 • Manchikallu and Mayidavolu inscriptions belong to the early Pallavas
    The Mayidavolu copper plates of Sivaskandavarman (in Prakrit) and the Manchikallu inscription of Simhavarman
    show early Pallava rule in the Krishna-Guntur region before they moved to Kanchi.
    Group-2 Mains 2025 Paper-1 (B1 p.22 Q12)

 • The Manchikallu inscription of Simhavarman shows the Pallavas replaced the Ikshvakus
    Early Pallava Prakrit records (Mayidavolu, Hirahadagalli, Manchikallu) show Pallava rule in the Krishna-Guntur area
    after the Ikshvakus fell.
    Group-2 Prelims 2019 (B1 p.72 Q55)

 IKSHVAKUS

 • The incorrect statement about the Ikshvakus of Vijayapuri: 'Puranas mentioned Ikshvakus as Sri
   Parvathiyas'; the Puranas call them Andhra Bhrityas
    The Ikshvakus ruled from Vijayapuri (Nagarjunakonda) after the Satavahanas in the 3rd-4th century CE, and the
    book notes the Puranas refer to them as 'Andhra Bhrityas'. They claimed descent from the Ikshvakus of Ayodhya
    and had marriage ties with the Western Satraps (Sakas) of Ujjain. Virapurushadatta's reign was a golden age of
    Buddhism, with royal women patronising stupas and viharas.
    Trap: The other three statements (Ayodhya descent, Saka marriage ties, Virapurushadatta and Buddhism) are true and act
    as distractors.
    Group-2 Mains 2025 Paper-1 (B1 p.23 Q20)

 • The Ikshvakus of Andhra issued iron coins with a thin coating of copper
    The Ikshvakus ruled the Krishna-Guntur region from Vijayapuri after the Satavahanas. Their distinctive coinage of
    copper-coated iron coins differed from the more common lead and copper coins of the Satavahanas.
    Trap: Satavahanas (lead coins), Salankayanas and Vishnukundins are the distractors.
    Group-2 Prelims 2019 (B1 p.72 Q57)

 • Rudrapurushadatta was the last Ikshvaku ruler. REPEATED
    The Ikshvakus (Vijayapuri/Nagarjunakonda) succeeded the Satavahanas in the Krishna valley. Founder

    Group-2 Mains 2019 Paper-2 (held 19-07-2019) (B1 p.103 Q10); Group-2 (paper not stated) (B2 p.9 Q12)

 • Bodhisiri, a lay woman devotee, rendered great service to Buddhism in the Ikshvaku period.
    Her Nagarjunakonda inscription records building chaityas and a chaitya for Sri Lankan monks. Royal women like
    Chamtisiri (sister of Santamula) also patronised Buddhism.
    Trap: Chamtisiri was a royal patron; the question points to Bodhisiri.
    Group-2 Mains 2019 Paper-2 (held 19-07-2019) (B1 p.103 Q12)

 • Virapurushadatta was described as 'Sri Parvatadhipati' (book key).
    Virapurushadatta's reign was the high point of Buddhism at Nagarjunakonda.
    Group-2 Mains 2017 Paper-2 (held 15-07-2017) (B1 p.168 Q10)

 • Rudrapurushadatta issued the Gurazala inscription.
    Gurazala (Palnadu) inscription shows the Ikshvaku line lasted into his reign.
    Group-2 Mains 2017 Paper-2 (held 15-07-2017) (B1 p.168 Q11)

 • Manchikallu inscription of Pallava Simhavarma records the Pallava conquest of Vijayapuri from the
   Ikshvakus
    The Ikshvakus ruled from Vijayapuri (Nagarjunakonda). The Manchikallu inscription of Simhavarma indicates that
    the Pallavas overran Vijayapuri, ending Ikshvaku rule around the late 3rd/early 4th century. The Mayidavolu plates
    of Sivaskandavarma show the Pallavas then administering the region.
    Trap: Nagarjunakonda inscriptions are Ikshvaku records themselves; Nandampudi grant belongs to Rajaraja Narendra.
    Group-2 Prelims 2019 (B2 p.1 Q5)

 • Upasika Bodhisri was the woman who served Buddhism greatly in the Ikshvaku age
    Bodhisri, a lay devotee in the reign of Virapurushadatta, built a chaitya on Chula Dhammagiri at Nagarjunakonda
    and other structures. Her inscription records the spread of Buddhism to lands like Kashmir, China, Gandhara and Sri
    Lanka.
    Trap: Chamtisiri was an Ikshvaku princess who built the Mahachaitya at Nagarjunakonda; do not confuse the two.
    Group-2 (paper not stated) (B2 p.9 Q3)

 • N. Venkataramanayya identified Sriparvata Swamy as the Buddha
    Inscriptions refer to a deity called Sriparvata Swami. Nelaturi Venkataramanayya held this to be the Buddha, while
    others identified the deity with Kartikeya or with Mallikarjuna of Srisailam (Kielhorn's view).
    Group-2 (paper not stated) (B2 p.10 Q22)

 • Virapurushadatta was described as Sriparvatadhipati
    Virapurushadatta, the most famous Ikshvaku king, is called lord of Sriparvata. Nagarjunakonda Buddhism flourished
    most in his time, and royal women made rich donations.
    Trap: Chamtamula I performed Vedic sacrifices; he is not the Buddhist icon.
    Group-2 Mains 2017 (B2 p.32 Q10)

 • Rudrapurushadatta's inscription was found at Gurazala
    Rudrapurushadatta, the last Ikshvaku ruler, is known from inscriptions at Gurazala (Guntur/Palnadu) and
    Nagarjunakonda.
    Group-2 Mains 2017 (B2 p.32 Q11)

 • Upasika Bodhisri served Buddhism in the reign of Virapurushadatta
    Bodhisri's Nagarjunakonda inscription belongs to Virapurushadatta's reign (6th or 14th regnal year), the golden age
    of Andhra Buddhism.
    Group-2 Paper-2 2012 (B2 p.50 Q6)

 • Hariti was the tutelary deity of the Ikshvakus (key's answer)
    A Hariti shrine exists at Nagarjunakonda, where devotees prayed for children. The later Chalukyas also called
    themselves Haritiputras.
    Trap: An earlier paper's key gave Chalukyas for a similar question; both have links to Hariti, so read the wording carefully.
    Group-2 Paper-2 2012 (B2 p.51 Q9)

 • Chamtamula (Santamula I) promoted agriculture through donations
    Chamtamula I performed Vedic sacrifices and gave away large numbers of ploughs, cows and gold, earning titles like
    Satasahasra-halaka. This involved Brahmins in agricultural expansion.
    Group-2 Paper-2 2012 (B2 p.51 Q25)

 • Vijayapuri was the capital of the Ikshvakus
    The Ikshvakus ruled from Vijayapuri in the Nagarjunakonda valley. The town is thought to be named after
    Satavahana Vijaya Satakarni, and the hill was called Sriparvata.
    Group-2 2011 (B2 p.65 Q4)

 • Caldwell argued that the Ikshvakus were native Telugu people
    Historians debate Ikshvaku origins: Caldwell saw them as local Andhras; Rapson and Buhler linked them to the
    northern Ikshvakus of Ayodhya; Vogel suggested Kannada origins; Gopalachari suggested Tamil links, per the book.
    Group-2 2011 (B2 p.66 Q21)

 • Buddhism received greatest patronage under Virapurushadatta
    Virapurushadatta, a Vedic follower who turned to Buddhism, saw Nagarjunakonda flourish, with the Mahastupa
    repaired and many monasteries built. He is called the Ashoka of the South.
    Group-2 Paper-2 2008 (B2 p.78 Q8)

 • Vijayapuri was the Ikshvaku capital
    Vijayapuri in Nagarjunakonda valley was the Ikshvaku capital.
    Group-2 Paper-2 2008 (B2 p.81 Q46)

 • The Ikshvakus were the Deccan dynasty that promoted Buddhism
    Though the Ikshvaku kings performed Vedic rites, their queens and relatives made huge Buddhist donations at
    Nagarjunakonda. Chamtisiri is the earliest known donor.
    Group-2 2000 (Backlog) (B2 p.110 Q3)

 • Nagarjunakonda flourished as an education centre under the Ikshvakus
    Sriparvata-Vijayapuri attracted monks from Sri Lanka, China and elsewhere. It is called the Bodh Gaya of the South
    for Mahayana Buddhists.
    Group-2 1993 (B2 p.112 Q26)

 • Sriparvata Swamy is linked with the Ikshvakus
    The Puranas call the Ikshvakus Sriparvatiyas or Sriparvatiya Andhras, connecting them with Sriparvata.
    Trap: Others link Sriparvata Swamy with the Vishnukundins.
    Group-2 1993 (B2 p.112 Q28)

 • The Mandhata (Chakravarti) panel with the raised hand is from Jaggayyapeta
    The Jaggayyapeta stupa yielded a famous slab showing the Chakravarti Mandhata with his hand raised and coins
    falling from the sky.
    Group-1 1991 (B2 p.145 Q57)

 • Veerapurushadatta married a princess of Ujjain
    Ikshvaku king Virapurushadatta married Rudradhara Bhattarika, a princess of the Saka (Kshatrapa) line of Ujjain. He
    also married his cross-cousins.
    Group-1 1991 (B2 p.145 Q59)

 • The Ikshvakus were overthrown by the Pallavas
    The Manchikallu inscription suggests the Pallava king Simhavarma ended Ikshvaku rule under Rudrapurushadatta in
    the 4th century.
    Group-2 Limited Recruitment 2012 (B2 p.161 Q48)

 • Capital of the Ikshvakus: Vijayapuri (Nagarjunakonda)
    Vijayapuri in the Nagarjunakonda valley on the Krishna was the Ikshvaku capital. The site is now largely under
    Nagarjuna Sagar reservoir.
    Trap: Nagarjunakonda is the modern name of the same site.
    Group-2 Limited Recruitment 2012 (B2 p.161 Q49)

 • A sculpture showing a trampled Shivalinga is linked to Virapurushadatta
    A Nagarjunakonda panel shows a figure trampling a linga, which is usually read as Virapurushadatta's turn from
    Vedic religion to Buddhism.
    NTR Health University Junior Assistants 2012 (B2 p.164 Q84)

 • The Kondamudi plates of the Brihatphalayanas are in Prakrit
    Jayavarma of the Brihatphalayana dynasty issued the Kondamudi copper plates in Prakrit. They are the main source

    The Salankayanas ruled from Vengi (near Eluru) in the 4th-5th centuries. Their tutelary deity was Chitrarathaswami,

    NTR Health University Junior Assistants 2012 (B2 p.164 Q86)

 • Royal women of the Ikshvakus were the main patrons of Buddhism
    Women of the Ikshvaku royal family, such as Chantisiri and Bhatideva, and the lay devotee Bodhisiri endowed
    stupas and viharas at Nagarjunakonda, even while the kings performed Vedic sacrifices.
    Panchayat Raj Lab Junior Assistants 2012 (B2 p.167 Q132)

 • The first copperplate grant in Andhra belongs to the Brihatphalayanas
    Jayavarma's Kondamudi copper plates (Prakrit) are considered the earliest copperplate grant in Andhra.
    Panchayat Raj Lab Junior Assistants 2012 (B2 p.168 Q149)

 • Jayavarma of the Kondamudi plates was a Brihatphalayana king
    Jayavarma ruled from Kuduru north of the Krishna and granted Panthur village to Brahmins.
    Asst. Social Welfare / BC Welfare / Tribal Welfare Officers (B2 p.214 Q44)

 • The Salankayana king in Samudragupta's Allahabad inscription: Hastivarman
    Samudragupta's southern campaign defeated Hastivarman of Vengi, a Salankayana, before reinstating him.

 • Chantisiri and Harmyasiri were sisters of Vasishthiputra Santamula
    Santamula, founder of the Ikshvaku line, had sisters Chantisiri and Harmyasiri. Chantisiri was a leading donor to the

 • Ikshvaku inscriptions are mainly in Prakrit
    Ikshvaku records are in Prakrit, though Sanskrit begins to appear late in the period. Sanskrit became the main official

 • The Salankayanas worshipped Chitraratha Swami
    The Salankayanas called themselves devotees of Chitrarathaswami (Sun god). The Vishnukundins were devotees of

    MPDO 1997 (B2 p.229 Q3)

 • Vasishthiputra Santamula founded the Ikshvaku dynasty
    Santamula I took over from the declining Satavahanas and ruled from Vijayapuri. He performed Vedic sacrifices like
    Asvamedha, while his family supported Buddhism.
    ASO 2011 (B2 p.234 Q9)

 • The Ikshvaku period was the golden age of Buddhism in Andhra
    Nagarjunakonda (Vijayapuri), with its many stupas and viharas endowed by Ikshvaku queens, marks the high point

 Vishnukundins (11)
 • 'Janasraya' was a title of the Vishnukundin ruler Madhavavarma
    The Sanskrit prosody work 'Janasraya Chandovichiti' is linked with this title. The Vishnukundins (5th-6th c.) ruled
    from the Krishna-Godavari region and patronised rock-cut caves at Undavalli and Mogalrajapuram.
    Group-2 Mains 2025 Paper-1 (B1 p.22 Q8)

 • Vikramendravarma transferred the Vishnukundin capital from Amarapuri to Denduluru
    The Vishnukundins moved their capital several times during their 5th-6th century rule, including Indrapalanagara
    and Amarapura. Vikramendravarma, a later ruler, shifted it to Denduluru near Eluru.
    Trap: Distractors: Madhavavarma, Govindavarma, Manchana Bhattaraka.
    Group-2 Mains 2025 Paper-1 (B1 p.24 Q29)
