Centripetal force is responsible to :
  (1) independent motion of the object in space
  (2) keep the body moving along the circular path
  (3) fly the object along a straight line
  (4) More than one of the above
explanation (OCR): Newton's first law of motion states that an object moving at constant speed will continue that motion unless acted on by an outside force. This means that circular motion can only happen if there is a 'centre seeking' force – otherwise things would just travel in a straight line, rather than the curved line of a circule. Centripetal means 'centre seeking', so centripetal force is used to refer to the force experienced by an object travelling in a circle. For example, when someone spins a ball attached to a rope horizontally above his head, the rope transmits a centripetal force from the muscles of the hand and arm, causing the ball to move in a circular path. In the special case of the Earth's circular motion around the Sun – or any satellite's circular motion around any celestial body – the centripetal force causing the motion is the result of the gravitational attraction between them.

explanation (OCR): Ball bearings are types of rolling-elements bearings that carry loads, reduce friction, and position moving machine parts while facilitating motion. The purpose of a ball bearing is to reduce rotational friction and support radial and axial loads. It achieves this by using at least two races to contain the balls and transmit the loads through the balls. In most applications, one race is stationary and the other is attached to the rotating assembly (e.g., a hub or shaft). As one of the bearing races rotates it causes the balls to rotate as well. Thus, ball bearings are used to convert static friction into rolling friction.

Physical Properties of Matter Notes • Matter around us exists in three different states—(i) solid, (ii) liquid, and (iii) gas. • The three states of matter show characteristic mechanical properties (many properties overlap across states): 1. Solids : show elasticity and plasticity. Solids are not perfectly rigid; a force is required to deform their shape or size. 2. Fluids (liquids and gases) : liquids and gases can flow and are therefore called fluids; fluids exert pressure (normal force per unit area). 3. Liquids (specifically) : show surface tension and capillarity because surface tension is concerned with liquids (gases do not have free surfaces). 4. Buoyancy/floatation (in fluids) : an immersed body experiences upthrust (buoyant force); objects float if their density is less than the liquid and sink if greater. Elasticity : • Generally, the meaning of a rigid substance is a hard solid which has definite shape and size. • But actually these can be stretched, compressed and deformed. • Even though a rigid steel rod can be deformed by applying sufficient external force. • A force is required to deform the shape or size of a body; deformation may be longitudinal, shear, or volumetric.  • After removing the force, the body regains original dimensions only if stress is within the elastic limit; otherwise permanent deformation remains. • The property of any substance by which it tries to gain its normal shape after being stretched or compressed is called elasticity. "Ability of an object or material to resume its normal shape after being stretched or compressed" is elasticity. • On the basis of the property of elasticity, matters are of two types : 1. Perfectly elastic substance - Matter which resumes perfectly its normal state after withdrawing the applied force. 2. Perfectly plastic substance - Matter which does not resume its normal state after withdrawing the external force, but deformed forever. • Really no matter is perfectly elastic nor perfectly plastic but lies between the limit of these two properties. • Materials may behave approximately elastic (e.g., quartz) or approximately plastic (e.g., wax) within a certain range; putty and mud are close to ideal plastics. Stress : • During the application of external force on a matter to change its shape or size, an internal reaction force of equal amount is produced in the opposite direction on every section. • This force tries to resume a normal state of matter. Stress is the restoring force per unit area developed in the body. • If force (F) is applied vertically on a transverse section with the area (A) of matter, then Stress = F/A Its SI unit is newton/metre2 (Nm–2), i.e., pascal (Pa). Strain : • When the shape or size of a matter is changed by applying an external force, the ratio of change in dimension to the original dimension (e.g. ΔL/L) is known as strain. • Since, it is a ratio, hence it is dimensionless or without any unit. Hooke's Law – • For sufficiently low stress/strain (within the linear elastic range/below the proportionality limit) applied stress on the matter is directly proportional to strain developed in it. The ratio of stress and strain is a constant value (proportionality constant), known as elastic modulus; for tensile/compressive stress this is Young's modulus (E). Surface Tension : • Surface Tension is the elastic tendency of a fluid surface which makes it to acquire the least surface area possible. It is measured in force per unit length. • Its SI unit is newton/metre (Nm–1). • The value of surface tension of any liquid depends upon its nature, temperature and the medium present on another side of the liquid surface. • Surface tension decreases with rise in temperature. • It becomes zero at the critical temperature. • When gravity/airflow effects are negligible compared with surface tension, drops/bubbles are approximately spherical. Small raindrops are roughly rounded, but larger raindrops flatten/distort as they fall. • Drops occupy the form in which surface (interfacial) energy is minimum (since creating extra surface area requires energy). • If only surface tension acts on a drop (other effects negligible), it minimizes surface area/surface energy and becomes spherical. Cohesive and Adhesive Force : • According to molecular theory, every matter is made up of small particles, known as atoms; in many substances, atoms combine to form molecules. These particles attract each other. • Cohesive force is the force of attraction between molecules of the same matter. • Adhesive force is the force of attraction between molecules of different matter. • The adhesive force between oil and water is less than the cohesive force of water but higher than cohesive force of oil, so when the water dropped on oil surface, its drops occupy spherical shape by shrinking, while when oil dropped on the water surface, it spreads widely in form of the thin film generally. • For two immiscible liquids A and B, whether A spreads on B depends on surface and interfacial tensions. The spreading parameter is S = γB − (γA + γAB). If S > 0, A spreads completely on B; if S < 0, it does not (forms droplets/lens instead of a thin film). Capillarity : • A capillary tube is a narrow tube (commonly glass) of very small internal diameter, open at both ends. • The rise or fall of a liquid in a narrow tube due to surface tension (with wetting/non-wetting effects) is called capillarity. • Liquids that wet glass (acute contact angle) show capillary rise, while liquids that do not wet glass (e.g., mercury in glass) show capillary depression. • Example: When a capillary tube is kept standing in mercury, the mercury level in the tube is depressed compared with the level of mercury outside.  Examples of capillarity / capillary effects : • In a fountain pen, ink is drawn toward the nib through narrow channels/slits by capillary action. • Fuel/oil rises in candle and lamp wicks by capillary action. • Blotting paper absorbs spilled liquid due to capillary action in its pores. • In plants, water moves upward through xylem mainly due to cohesion–adhesion and transpiration pull; capillarity alone is not sufficient to explain rise to great heights. Viscosity : • A liquid is flowing over a rigid horizontal surface AB as shown in the figure. The liquid layer which is in contact with the bottom remains at rest/has zero velocity relative to the surface, but the velocity of the upper layers is increasing. The uppermost layer flows with maximum velocity. • In three horizontal layers a, b and c, a is with minimum velocity, b has more than a and c is with maximum velocity. • The consecutive layers, from bottom to surface try to reduce the relative velocity between adjacent layers. • An internal force acts between layers, try to slow down the velocity of the layer which is just above it. • An internal force acts on different layers which tries to oppose their relative velocity. • This force is known as the viscous force. • To maintain the flow of liquid, the external force is applied against viscous force. • The property of fluids (liquids and gases), which opposes the relative movement among different layers of fluid, is known as viscosity. • Highly viscous liquids like honey and glycerine have greater resistance to flow, so they flow less readily/slower, than low-viscosity fluids. • With increase in temperature, viscosity of liquids / generally decreases. • The viscosity of gases generally increases with increase in temperature. • Viscosity is primarily a property of fluids; solids generally lack viscosity and mainly resist shear elastically, though some solid materials show viscoelastic/creep behaviour. This force is known as the upthrust or buoyant force. • The centre of buoyancy (upthrust) is the centre of mass/ centroid of the displaced fluid volume. Archimedes' Principle : • It states that the upward (buoyant) force that is exerted on a body immersed in a liquid, whether fully or partially submerged, is equal to the weight of the liquid that the body displaces and it acts in the upward direction at the centre of mass of the displaced liquid. Law of Floatation : • When a body is in liquid, the following two main forces act on it – 1. The weight (W) of the body acts vertically downward. 2.
