=== Devaluation, Depreciation, Appreciation and Revaluation
- **Devaluation:** an **official reduction in the value of a currency vis-a-vis major internationally traded currencies**, made by the government / monetary authority under a **fixed** exchange-rate system (more rupees per dollar) – not a general "decline in values" or a rise in inflation.
| Term | System | Meaning | Done by |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Devaluation** | Fixed | Official **reduction** of the currency's value | Government / monetary authority |
| **Revaluation** | Fixed | Official **rise** in the currency's value | Government / monetary authority |
| **Appreciation** | Flexible | Market-driven **rise** in value – fewer rupees per $ | Demand and supply |
| **Depreciation** | Flexible | Market-driven **fall** in value – more rupees per $ | Demand and supply |
# Reading a rate change
- **$1 = ₹60 → ₹58:** the **rupee appreciated** and the **dollar depreciated**.
- **$1 = ₹60 → ₹65 (or ₹70) by market forces:** the **rupee depreciated** (not "devalued" – that word is for an official change). ₹96 → ₹94 per $ = rupee appreciated.
- **"Rate of exchange falls"** (foreign currency per rupee falls = rupee cheaper), ceteris paribus: **exports rise and imports fall**.
> Exam angle: official change → devaluation / revaluation; market change → depreciation / appreciation. Wrong-pair MCQs swap these.

=== Effects of Devaluation
- Devaluation makes **exports cheaper abroad** (their foreign-currency price falls) and **imports dearer at home** → it **promotes exports**; it is an **expenditure-switching** policy (spending switches to home-produced goods) used to **correct the balance of trade / BoP**.
- **A-R pattern:** "Devaluation may promote exports" (A) is explained by "the price of the country's products in the international market falls" (R) – **A true, R true, R explains A**. A reason saying devaluation **raises** export prices is **false**.
- Devaluation does **not necessarily** improve the trade balance or raise the currency's foreign value: it **necessarily** only improves export competitiveness (UPSC 2021: "1 only"). Limits: **imported inflation** (it **raises domestic prices**), inelastic oil imports, costlier ECB repayment.
- It **raises the cost of imports** and does **not curb inflation** – so "to fix the balance of trade" is the purpose, not "reduce import costs" or "curb inflation".
- **When does it work?** When exports actually **become cheaper to importers** abroad, i.e. export prices fall in proportion to the devaluation and domestic prices do not rise to cancel it (CDS 2017; APPSC 2011 key: "exports change proportionately").
- **1991:** the rupee was devalued to **make exports cheaper**; export growth rose after 1991 and the devaluation is credited for it (UPSC 2000 key: A and R true, R explains A).
- **Trade imbalance and controls:** import duties, exchange controls and devaluation are used to **wipe out** the trade imbalance and keep the rate stable (APPSC 2011 key).
- BoP correction methods (NextGen): monetary policy, **exchange depreciation, devaluation, exchange control**, import duties and quotas, export promotion, foreign loans and investment.

=== Rupee Devaluation Episodes – 1949, 1966, 1991
| Year | Extent / steps |
|---|---|
| **Sept 1949** | **First** devaluation, with sterling; about **30.5%** (₹3.30 → ₹4.76 per US$) |
| **June 1966** | ₹4.76 → **₹7.50** per US$ = rupee down **36.5%** (dollar's rupee price up 57.5%); wars, drought, 36% inflation; "short-lived liberalisation" |
| **July 1991** | **Two steps, 1 and 3 July**; about **18%** (17.38% against sterling, 18.7% against the US$; Ramesh Singh says 22%); BoP crisis; last formal devaluation |
- **Count:** 1949 (1st), 1966 (2nd), July 1991 two steps (3rd and **4th**) – so the fourth devaluation fell in **1991**.
> Trap: the 1991 steps were on **1 and 3 July**, not 1 and 2 July. 1966's "57.5%" is the rise in the dollar's price; the rupee itself lost 36.5%.

=== Exchange Rate and Forex Market Terms
- **Exchange rate:** the price / value of one currency in terms of another (₹50 for $1 = ₹50 per dollar). **Forex market** participants: commercial banks, brokers, authorised dealers, monetary authorities.
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Direct quote** | Units of **domestic** currency per one unit of foreign currency (₹95 = $1) |
| **Indirect quote** | Units of **foreign** currency per one unit of domestic currency |
| **Spot rate** | Immediate delivery – settled within **two days** |
| **Forward rate** | Agreed now for future delivery; an importer's forward contract **covers exchange-rate risk** |
| **Hedging / speculation** | Covering exchange risk (forwards) / taking an open position on expected moves |
| **Arbitrage** | **Buying a currency where it is cheaper and simultaneously selling it where it is dearer** |
| **Hot currency (money)** | Foreign currency that **migrates quickly** between markets for the highest short-term return |
| **Tobin tax** | Tax on **foreign exchange transactions** (James Tobin) |
| **Currency war** | Countries **competitively devaluing** their currencies to gain export advantage |
| **Hawala** | **Illegal transactions of foreign exchange** (informal transfer outside banking channels) |
- **Money laundering** turns the **proceeds of crime** (drugs, smuggling, corruption) into apparently legal money – hence "drugs and crime"; hawala is a common channel.
- **Import cover:** the **number of months of imports that could be paid for by the country's reserves** – about **11 months** with $701.4 bn (16 Jan 2026, Economic Survey 2025-26).
- **Most valuable currency (in ₹): Kuwaiti dinar**; of pound, euro and US dollar the costliest is the **pound sterling** (₹125.18 vs euro ₹109.05, $ ₹93.00 on 17 Apr 2026).
- **International liquidity problem** = shortage of **dollars and other hard currencies** (internationally accepted reserve assets).
- **RBI** (through authorised dealers, under FEMA) sanctions foreign exchange for imports.

=== Exchange Rate Regimes and Determinants
| Regime | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Fixed / pegged** | Government fixes the rate (to gold, a currency or a basket); the central bank buys / sells forex to hold it, so private demand and supply **need not come into balance** |
| **Exchange control** | Rate fixed and held by **rationing out the available foreign exchange** |
| **Flexible / floating** | Set by **demand and supply** of foreign currency in the market – not by the central bank |
| **Managed float (dirty float)** | Market-set, with central-bank intervention to curb volatility – **India's market-determined system** |
- **Post-Bretton Woods classes:** **fixed** – currency unions, dollarised regimes, **currency boards (Argentina 1991 – Jan 2002)**, pegs; **intermediate** – bands, crawling pegs; **flexible** – managed and independent floats.
- **Floating rates:** traders face the risk of an **exchange-rate loss**; price differences between markets give scope for **arbitrage** (APPSC 2019 key).
- **Determinants:** demand for the country's **goods and services**, **stability of its government**, economic potential – the **World Bank does not decide a currency's price** (UPSC 2012 key: II and III).
- **Raghuram Rajan:** **high inflation, high fiscal deficit and high crude oil prices** all weaken the rupee's exchange value.
- **PPP theory:** equality of purchasing power (the **inflation differential**) decides the long-run **rate of exchange**. Long-run anchors (ES 2025-26): PPP, savings-investment balance, current account.
- **Capital flows:** large inflows → **appreciation** (Dutch disease); higher imports can coexist with a stable rupee if backed by **net investment inflows**; a repo hike attracts **carry-trade** capital; deliberate undervaluation (alleged of China) = **currency manipulation**.
- **Impossible trinity (Mundell-Fleming trilemma):** no country can have a **fixed exchange rate, free capital flows and an independent monetary policy** at once.

=== Bretton Woods, IMF and India's Exchange-Rate History
- **Gold standard (1870-1914):** the epitome of the fixed-rate system.
- **Bretton Woods Conference** = **United Nations Monetary and Financial Conference**, **1-22 July 1944**, **New Hampshire (USA)**, **44 nations**: set up the **IMF and IBRD** (not the UN or WTO) and fixed rates centred on the **dollar** (two-tier convertibility – the origin of **currency convertibility**).
- **IMF:** formally created **27 December 1945**; **India a founding member (27 Dec 1945)**; **191 members** (Liechtenstein 191st, Oct 2024); India's quota **2.75%** of SDRs, votes 2.63% (March 2026). Its purposes are monetary cooperation, trade growth, exchange **stability** and multilateral payments – **not** promoting flexible exchange rates.
| Year | India's exchange-rate step |
|---|---|
| **1947-1971** | **Par value system** under the IMF (rupee linked to sterling) |
| **1971** | **Smithsonian Agreement** – ±2.25% band (NextGen: 2.5%), lasted 14 months |
| **Sept 1975** | Rupee **delinked from pound sterling**, pegged to a **basket of currencies** – the end of the strict fixed system (Ramesh Singh: "between fixed and floating") |
| **1992-93** | **LERMS** (Budget 1992-93): dual rate – **40% official, 60% market**; partial convertibility |
| **1 March 1993** | Rates **unified**; market-determined |
| **August 1994** | **IMF Article VIII** – current account convertibility |
- **Official exchange rate of the rupee:** associated with a **basket of selected currencies** (NextGen; the 1975 basket peg); since 1993 it is market-determined, with the US dollar as the RBI's reference and intervention currency.
- **India's current policy:** **market-determined exchange rate** (managed float).

=== FERA to FEMA and Rupee Convertibility
- **FERA 1973** (restrictive, to prevent outflow of currency) → replaced by **FEMA 1999**: proposed in the **1997-98 Budget**; **in force 1 June 2000**, with a **two-year sunset clause** for FERA cases (ended 31 May 2002 – "finally implemented 2002" in the UPPSC key). **FEMA regulates the flow of foreign exchange**; no "Foreign Exchange Control Act" ever replaced FERA.
- **Convertibility:** **freely permitting the conversion of the rupee to other currencies and vice versa** – not conversion into gold, nor fixing by market forces.
- **Full convertibility** may mean the rupee's **free float**, its **direct exchange anywhere** inside and outside India, and its acting **like any other international currency** (UPSC 2002: 1, 2 and 3).
- India: **full current account** convertibility (Aug 1994), only **partial capital account** convertibility.
| Committee | Facts |
|---|---|
| **Tarapore I** | 1997 – roadmap to **capital account convertibility** |
| **Tarapore II** | Appointed **2006** (report 31 July 2006) – **fuller** capital account convertibility |

=== SDR, Reserve Tranche and Paper Gold
- **SDR (Special Drawing Rights)** – relates to the **IMF**; an **artificial currency / international reserve asset** created in **1969** (agreed 1967) to **supplement members' official reserves**; called **"paper gold"**; exists only as **book-keeping entries**. ADR / GDR are depository receipts, not currency.
- **Basket (since 1 Oct 2016):** US dollar, euro, **renminbi** (the currency proposed / added in 2015-16), yen, pound sterling – **no rupee**. Largest allocation **about $650 bn, August 2021**.
- **Reserve (gold) tranche:** the part of a member's quota paid in reserve assets (historically gold); a **credit facility of the IMF to its members** drawable **without conditions**; the **smallest** component of India's reserves (FCA > gold > SDR > RTP).
- **Reserves:** **RBI is the custodian**; reserves rose to **$785.71 bn on 15 Sep 2026 – India 4th**, overtaking Russia; rising **gold share** = diversification; **India-Japan bilateral swap** renewed from 28 Feb 2026 (two-way, local currencies against US dollars).
~~ Sources: NextGen Indian Economy Part 1 - APPSC 04 p.21; app notes 3:76 sub 10 (Carpediem CA, Sep 2026)

=== NEER and REER
- **NEER:** weighted average of the rupee's bilateral **nominal** rates against a **trade-weighted basket**; RBI indices of **40 and 6 currencies**, **base 2015-16 = 100**. **A rise in NEER = rupee appreciation.**
- **REER** = NEER **adjusted for relative inflation** differentials – the better competitiveness measure. A **rise in REER = loss of competitiveness** (overvalued rupee), not an improvement.
- Higher domestic inflation than abroad **widens the divergence between NEER and REER** (UPSC 2022: statements A and C correct).
~~ Sources: NextGen Rocket Sheets Indian Economy p.69; vajiramandravi.com UPSC 2022 solution page

=== Rupee Movement and RBI Tools
- **RBI aim:** curb **excessive volatility**, not defend a fixed level. If the rupee falls fast, the RBI **sells dollars**; if US / EU rates fall and inflows surge, it **buys dollars**; to fight high inflation it **sells** (not buys) government securities (UPSC 2022: B and C).
| Tool | Example |
|---|---|
| Spot intervention | Selling dollars from reserves |
| Forex swap | **Buy/sell swap**: banks sell dollars to the RBI and buy them back later at a premium |
| NDF curbs | Restricting domestic banks in offshore Non-Deliverable Forward markets; Net Open Position limits |
| FCNR(B) support | RBI bore the full hedging cost on fresh FCNR(B) deposits till 30 Sep 2026 |
- **NOT RBI tools:** **fiscal policy adjustments** (APPSC 2025); **import curbs and export promotion** are Government structural measures.
- **To stop a slide:** curb non-essential imports, promote exports, **masala bonds**, ease **ECB** norms – but **not an expansionary monetary policy** (UPSC 2019).
- **Recent:** FY 2025-26 (to Jan 2026) rupee **depreciated about 5.4%**; crossed **₹96.2/$ on 19 May 2026** (Brent near $110, FPI outflows over ₹2 lakh crore). In 2002-07 the rupee **gained against the US dollar** (₹48.4 → ₹44.3).

=== Rupee Internationalisation, Bonds and World Currencies
- **INR trade settlement:** RBI framework of **July 2022**, built into the **Foreign Trade Policy 2023** – it **encourages the use of the rupee in cross-border trade**. **Special Rupee Vostro Accounts** = foreign banks' rupee accounts with Indian banks; surplus may go into **corporate bonds and CPs** (2025). **Vostro** = foreign bank's account with a domestic bank; **Nostro** = ours abroad.
- **SAARC Currency Swap Framework:** RBI, **2012**; 3-month tenor, rollable twice; 2024-27 **₹250 bn INR window**; first INR drawdown **Maldives, April 2026**.
- **SWIFT:** member-owned **cooperative** founded **1973**, headquartered in **Belgium**; a **messaging** network – not a payment network and no power to impose sanctions (APPSC G1 2023: none correct).

| **Resurgent India Bonds** | **SBI, 5 Aug 1998**, in **US dollar, pound sterling and Deutsche Mark** |
