## SUB 0 | Kesavananda Bharati (1973) – Where the Doctrine Was Propounded
[0:0] - The **basic structure doctrine** was **first propounded** in **Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973)** – not in Golaknath (1967), Sajjan Singh (1965), Shankari Prasad (1951), Champakam Dorairajan, Gopalan, Maneka Gandhi, Minerva Mills, Bommai or Indra Sawhney. Where Kesavananda is not among the options, the answer is "**None of the above**".
[0:1] - Decided **24 April 1973** by **13 judges** (the largest bench ever), majority **7:6**, headed by **CJI S.M. Sikri**.
[0:2] - Held: Parliament under **Art. 368** may amend **any part** of the Constitution, **including Fundamental Rights**, but **cannot alter, damage or destroy its basic structure**.
[0:3] - **Overruled Golaknath** and **upheld the 24th Amendment** (1971), which had amended Arts. 13 and 368.
[0:4] - Held the **Preamble** to be **part of the Constitution** and amendable, but its objectives embody the basic structure – the Preamble is regarded as **part of the basic structure** (Kesavananda, not Berubari, Gopalan or the Privy Purse case).
[0:5] - Struck down the part of **Art. 31C** (25th Amendment) that barred courts from asking whether a law really gives effect to the Directive Principles – so **judicial review** of amendments stays.
[0:6] - Basic features named by the judges included **supremacy of the Constitution, republican and democratic government, secularism, separation of powers, federalism, unity and sovereignty, parliamentary system, welfare state, dignity of the individual**.
[0:7] - Landmark for "**the basic structure of the Constitution, as defined in the Preamble, cannot be changed**" (CDS 2010) – not for religion in politics, untouchability or the non-suspension of Art. 21.
[0:8] TABLE head: Person | Contribution
  [0:8.0] Dietrich Conrad | German jurist; argued in India (1965) that the amending power has implied limits, drawing on Germany's Basic Law
  [0:8.1] Justice Mudholkar | Dissent in Sajjan Singh (1965) – first judicial reference to "basic features"
  [0:8.2] CJI S.M. Sikri | Headed the 13-judge Kesavananda bench
[0:9] - Country the idea is traced to: **Germany**.
[0:10] > Exam angle: "Which case propounded the basic structure doctrine?" is asked again and again (×18 PYQs) – Kesavananda Bharati, 1973.

## SUB 1 | What the Basic Structure Means
[1:0] - Basic structure = the **core features** of the Constitution that **cannot be amended or altered by Parliament** in exercise of the power under **Art. 368** (APPSC G1 2018) – "certain features are so essential that they **cannot be abrogated**" (IAS 1994).
[1:1] - It is **not** "what can be amended by simple majority", **not** "Directive Principles only" or "Fundamental Rights only", **not** "the order of Articles cannot be altered" and **not** "amendments only on procedural matters".
[1:2] - Source of the theory = **judicial interpretation** – **judge-made**, nowhere defined in the text; **not** inserted by any **Act of Parliament** or amendment (APPSC G1 2024 statement I false).
[1:3] - **Not exhaustive**: the Supreme Court has never fully listed it; the list grows **case by case**.
[1:4] - What limits Parliament's power to amend the Constitution = the **basic structure** (not FRs as such, customs or distribution of powers).
[1:5] - Present position: Parliament can amend **any part** of the Constitution, **including FRs**, **without affecting the basic structure** (APPSC G1 2024 statement II true); FRs can still be amended after Kesavananda.
[1:6] - **Parliament's limited amending power** is itself a basic feature (**Minerva Mills, 1980**; statement III true).
[1:7] - As a principle of judicial review, the doctrine **accepts the supremacy of the Constitution and of the courts** (the American principle of judicial supremacy, accepted "to a limited extent"). It goes beyond "**procedure established by law**", under which a court only checks whether a law is within the authority's powers.
[1:8] TABLE head: Statement (APPSC G1 2024) | Verdict
  [1:8.0] Doctrine incorporated by an Act of Parliament | False – judge-made
  [1:8.1] Parliament may amend any part without affecting the basic structure | True
  [1:8.2] Limited amending power is a basic feature | True – Minerva Mills
  [1:8.3] Kumar Padma Prasad (1992) declared the welfare state basic | False – welfare state: Kesavananda / Bhim Singhji (1981); Kumar Padma Prasad was about judicial independence in appointing judges

## SUB 2 | Evolution – Shankari Prasad to the NJAC Case
[2:0] TABLE head: Year | Case / event | Contribution
  [2:0.0] 1951 | Shankari Prasad | Art. 368 includes power to amend FRs; an amendment is not "law" under Art. 13
  [2:0.1] 1965 | Sajjan Singh | Upheld the 17th Amendment; reaffirmed Shankari Prasad; Mudholkar dissent
  [2:0.2] 1967 | Golaknath | Challenged the 17th (not the 24th); 11 judges, 6:5; FRs "transcendental and immutable"; amendment is "law"; Parliament cannot amend (abridge) FRs; first use of prospective overruling
  [2:0.3] 1971 | 24th Amendment | Parliament's reply – Art. 13(4), Art. 368: Parliament can abridge FRs
  [2:0.4] 1973 | Kesavananda Bharati | Basic structure; Golaknath overruled; 24th upheld
  [2:0.5] 1975 | Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain | First application; struck Art. 329A(4) (39th Amendment) putting the PM's and Speaker's election beyond courts; free and fair elections, rule of law, democracy basic
  [2:0.6] 1976 | 42nd Amendment | Art. 368(4), (5): no limit on the amending power, no court review
  [2:0.7] 1980 | Minerva Mills | Struck 368(4), (5) and the widened 31C; judicial review, limited amending power, FR–DPSP harmony basic
  [2:0.8] 1981 | Waman Rao | Doctrine applies to amendments after 24 April 1973
  [2:0.9] 1994 | S.R. Bommai | Nine judges; secularism, federalism basic
  [2:0.10] 2007 | I.R. Coelho | Nine judges; post-1973 Ninth Schedule laws reviewable
  [2:0.11] 2015 | NJAC case (SC Advocates-on-Record Assn.) | 99th Amendment (2014) and NJAC Act struck, 4:1 – judicial independence
[2:1] - **1967** key judgment on FRs = **Golak Nath v. State of Punjab** – the case that said **Fundamental Rights cannot be amended**.
[2:2] - Case by which Parliament got (back) the right to amend FRs = **Kesavananda Bharati** – it upheld the 24th Amendment after Golaknath had denied the power (Shankari Prasad and Sajjan Singh had also allowed it before 1967).
[2:3] - **Judicial review** was **explicitly held a basic feature in Minerva Mills (1980)** – the 42nd Amendment's bar on review was struck "as it excludes judicial review, which is a basic feature"; reaffirmed in S.P. Sampath Kumar (1987) and **L. Chandra Kumar (1997)**.
[2:4] - The term "**judicial review**" is **nowhere defined or used** in the Constitution; Arts. 13, 32, 226 confer the power.
[2:5] - **Election bar** for the PM was the **39th Amendment** (not the 42nd) – struck in Raj Narain; the **99th (NJAC)** was struck for violating **judicial independence** (UPSC 2019: only II correct).
[2:6] - Benches: Golaknath **11, 6:5** | Kesavananda **13, 7:6** | NJAC **4:1** | Bommai and Coelho **9**.

[3:3] - Basic features **not amendable** under Art. 368 include **sovereignty, territorial integrity, federal system, judicial review and parliamentary system** – all five (SSC 2007).
[3:4] - **Judicial activism** is a judicial practice, **not a basic feature**; federation, secularism and judicial review are.

## SUB 4 | Ninth Schedule, Articles 31A–31C and the Coelho Case
[4:0] - Created by the **1st Amendment (1951)** with **Art. 31B** (and 31A); it also added **Art. 15(4)** and new **Art. 19(2)** grounds (public order, friendly relations with foreign States, incitement to an offence). Purpose: protect **land-reform** laws.
[4:1] - Covers **Central and State** laws; entries made **only by Parliament through a constitutional amendment** (UPSC 2018 statement I true) – not by ordinary law, a State Legislature or a Presidential order.
[4:2] - Example: **76th Amendment, 1994** placed **Tamil Nadu's 69% reservation** Act. Entries: **13** at the start → **282** now (more than 280).
[4:3] TABLE head: Case | Year | Rule
  [4:3.0] Kesavananda Bharati | 1973 | Amendments, including inclusions, cannot destroy the basic structure
  [4:3.1] Waman Rao | 1981 | Cut-off 24 April 1973; later entries open to challenge
  [4:3.2] I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu | 2007 | Nine judges; no blanket immunity; "rights test / direct impact test" on the essence of Arts. 14, 19, 21
[4:4] - So "the validity of a law placed in the Ninth Schedule cannot be examined by any court" is **false** – UPSC 2018 answer **"1 only"**.
[4:5] - **Coelho** = the "**Ninth Schedule case**" (not the Seventh, Eighth or Tenth).
[4:6] - Sequence: **1st Amendment (1951) → Kesavananda (1973) → Waman Rao (1981) → Coelho (2007)**.
[4:7] TABLE head: Article | Saves | Inserted by
  [4:7.0] 31A | Estate acquisition, agrarian reform laws from Arts. 14, 19 | 1st Amendment, 1951
  [4:7.1] 31B | Ninth Schedule laws | 1st Amendment, 1951
  [4:7.2] 31C | Laws implementing Art. 39(b), (c) from Arts. 14, 19 | 25th Amendment, 1971 (not the First); widened by the 42nd

## SUB 5 | Supreme Court vs Parliament – Doctrines, Supremacy, Criticism
[5:0] TABLE head: Doctrine / rule | Origin
  [5:0.0] Basic structure | Supreme Court – Kesavananda (1973)
  [5:0.1] Eclipse, severability, pith and substance, colourable legislation, harmonious construction, territorial nexus | Supreme Court (interpretive doctrines)
  [5:0.2] Prospective overruling | Supreme Court – Golaknath (1967)
  [5:0.3] DPSP primacy over Arts. 14, 19 (Art. 31C) | Parliament – 25th Amendment (1971)
  [5:0.4] Art. 13(4) | Parliament – 24th Amendment
  [5:0.5] Unlimited amending power (Art. 368(4), (5)) | Parliament – 42nd; struck in Minerva Mills
[5:1] - The doctrine rests on **supremacy of the Constitution and judicial review by the courts**.
[5:2] - Indian Parliament is **not sovereign in the British sense**: written supreme Constitution, FRs, judicial review, federal division, **limited** amending power. India = a **synthesis** of American judicial supremacy and British parliamentary supremacy.
[5:3] - The **43rd Amendment (1977)** removed the 42nd Amendment's bar on High Courts reviewing central laws.
[5:4] TABLE head: View | Argument
  [5:4.0] Criticism | No textual definition; "judicial rewriting" by unelected judges; open-ended list; "judicial supremacy"
  [5:4.1] Defence | Shield against majorities; checks misuse of Art. 368
[5:5] - **2026**: free and fair elections and democracy as basic features cited in the debate on making **voting** more than a statutory right.
[5:6] - **129th Amendment Bill, 2024** (simultaneous polls), JPC: ex-CJI **B.R. Gavai** – no violation of basic structure; ex-CJIs **U.U. Lalit** and **Sanjiv Khanna** raised doubts (as of 2026).
[5:7] - Cannot be done even by an Art. 368 amendment: **destroying the basic structure** (amending FRs, the Preamble or adding to the Ninth Schedule can be done).
