## SUB 0 | Veto Power of the President – Types and Article 111
[0:0] - **Article 111**: a Bill passed by both Houses is presented to the President, who **declares assent**, **withholds assent** or (for a **non-Money Bill**) **returns** it for reconsideration. So the power to withhold assent is in **Art. 111** (not Arts. 63, 108 or 109).
[0:1] - Does the Indian President have a veto? **Yes** – three of the four kinds: **absolute, suspensive and pocket**; **no qualified veto**.
[0:2] TABLE head: Veto | Meaning | Applies to
  [0:2.0] Absolute | Withholds assent; the Bill ends and never becomes an Act | Private members' Bills; Government Bills when the Cabinet resigns before assent and the new Cabinet advises against it – not "at all times"
  [0:2.1] Suspensive | Returns the Bill once; if re-passed (with or without amendment) he must assent | Ordinary Bills (and Financial Bills) – not Money Bills
  [0:2.2] Pocket | Neither assents, rejects nor returns – keeps the Bill pending indefinitely | Possible because Art. 111 prescribes no time limit
  [0:2.3] Qualified | Can be overridden only by a higher (two-thirds) majority | USA – not India
[0:3] - **Veto by type of Bill:**
[0:4] TABLE head: Bill | President (Union) | President (State Bill reserved, Art. 201)
  [0:4.0] Ordinary | Assent, withhold or return once – must assent if re-passed | Assent, withhold or direct return – not bound to assent even if re-passed
  [0:4.1] Money | Assent or withhold; cannot return (usually assents – introduced on his prior recommendation) | Assent or withhold – so he can veto a State Money Bill
  [0:4.2] Constitutional Amendment | Must assent (24th Amendment, 1971); cannot reject or return | Cannot be introduced in a State Legislature
[0:5] - The one-time return power can apply to **Financial Bills of Categories I and II**; a Money Bill and a Constitutional Amendment Bill cannot be returned.
[0:6] - APPSC G1 2024 statements: "suspensive veto in Money Bills but not Finance Bills" – **false** (the reverse); "absolute veto on Government Bills at all times" – **false**; "**pocket veto indefinitely; the American President has a qualified veto**" – **true**; "objective = checks and balances between the President's office and Parliament" – **false** (the stated objects are to **prevent hasty, ill-considered or unconstitutional legislation**) → **only III**.
[0:7] TABLE head: Feature | India | USA
  [0:7.0] Absolute veto | Yes | No
  [0:7.1] Qualified veto | No | Yes – Congress overrides by two-thirds
  [0:7.2] Pocket veto | Yes – no time limit | Yes – President must act within 10 days (the 10-day rule is American)
[0:8] > Exam angle: Art. 111; Money Bill cannot be returned; amendment Bill must be signed; India has no qualified veto.

## SUB 1 | Vetoes Actually Used – Pocket Veto and the Indian Post Office Bill
[1:0] - **Pocket veto** = the President **keeps a Bill pending with him, without any time limit**, neither approving, rejecting nor returning it – not the Speaker blocking a Bill, the PM dropping ministers or the Rajya Sabha rejecting a Bill.
[1:1] TABLE head: Veto | President | Bill
  [1:1.0] Pocket | Giani Zail Singh | Indian Post Office (Amendment) Bill, 1986 (power to intercept mail)
  [1:1.1] Absolute | Dr. Rajendra Prasad (on the Council of Ministers' advice) | PEPSU Appropriation Bill, 1954
  [1:1.2] Absolute | R. Venkataraman | Salary, Allowances and Pension of MPs (Amendment) Bill, 1991
[1:2] - The Post Office Bill question is asked in every form – "refused to sign / did not assent / did not give assent / first to use the power on it" → **Zail Singh** (not Radhakrishnan, V.V. Giri, Zakir Hussain, R. Venkataraman, K.R. Narayanan, Rajendra Prasad or A.P.J. Abdul Kalam). He **did not return** it; he simply kept it pending, and it was never enacted (later withdrawn).
[1:3] - Pocket veto used **only once** at the Union level; "the only instance of the President's veto" in older keys means this Bill, though the **PEPSU (1954)** and **1991** Bills were absolute vetoes.
[1:4] - **2002**: the **Representation of the People (Amendment) Ordinance** on **electoral reforms** was returned by **President Kalam**; the Union Cabinet sent it back unchanged and he **assented under Art. 123**.

## SUB 2 | Ordinance-making Power – Article 123: Basis, Nature, Scope
[2:0] - **Art. 123** (Part V): the **President** promulgates ordinances during the **recess of Parliament** – not the Speaker, the RS Chairman or the Attorney-General. Governor: **Art. 213** (Part VI). Not Arts. 161, 200, 201, 356 or 360.
[2:1] - Nature: the **most important legislative power of the President** – a **legislative power of the Executive** (President is part of Parliament, Art. 79), not an executive order. Among his legislative powers (summoning, proroguing, assent), the ordinance is the one examiners key; pardon is a judicial power; **adjourning** a sitting is the presiding officer's job.
[2:2] - When: only when **both Houses, or either House, are not in session** and he is **satisfied** that circumstances need **immediate action**; none while both Houses sit. Exercised on the **Council of Ministers' advice**.
[2:3] - Force: the **same force and effect as an Act of Parliament** and the same limits; **co-extensive** with Parliament's legislative power but **not a parallel** power.
[2:4] TABLE head: Can | Cannot
  [2:4.0] Amend or repeal any Central Act (or an earlier ordinance) | Amend the Constitution (Art. 368)
  [2:4.1] Operate retrospectively (from a back date) | Abridge Fundamental Rights – it is "law" under Art. 13(2)
  [2:4.2] Introduce or change tax provisions | Go beyond Parliament's legislative competence
[2:5] - UPSC 2025: "amend any Central Act" and "come into effect from a back date" true; "abridge a FR" false → **I and III only**.
[2:6] - Origin: a **relic of the Government of India Act, 1935** (Governor-General's ordinance power).

## SUB 3 | Ordinance – Life, Lapse, Withdrawal and Judicial Review
[3:0] - 1. Promulgated during recess. 2. **Laid before both Houses** on reassembly. 3. **Ceases six weeks after reassembly** unless approved – if the Houses reassemble on **different dates**, the six weeks run from the **later date**. 4. Ends **earlier** if **both Houses pass resolutions disapproving** it (within those six weeks – not "six months"), or if the President **withdraws** it.
[3:1] - The President **can withdraw an ordinance at any time** – "once promulgated it cannot be withdrawn / the President can never repeal it" is **false**.
[3:2] - **Maximum life = six months + six weeks**: maximum gap between sessions is **six months** (**Art. 85**; States **Art. 174**) + the six-week window – so neither "six months", "three months" nor "indefinitely".
[3:3] TABLE head: Item | Fact
  [3:3.0] Lapse | 6 weeks after reassembly
  [3:3.1] Maximum gap between sessions | 6 months
  [3:3.2] Maximum life | 6 months + 6 weeks
  [3:3.3] Linked limit – Art. 101(4) | MP's unexcused absence for 60 days vacates the seat (prorogation and adjournments of more than four consecutive days excluded)
[3:4] - **Satisfaction and judicial review:**
[3:5] TABLE head: Year | Event | Effect
  [3:5.0] 1975 | 38th Amendment | Satisfaction under Arts. 123, 213, 352 made final
  [3:5.1] 1978 | 44th Amendment | Reversed the 38th – satisfaction justiciable
  [3:5.2] 1987 | D.C. Wadhwa v State of Bihar | Routine re-promulgation without placing before the legislature = fraud on the Constitution
  [3:5.3] 2017 | Krishna Kumar Singh v State of Bihar | Seven judges; re-promulgation may be a fraud on the Constitution; laying is mandatory
[3:6] - Ordinances are **subject to judicial review**; criticism – they **bypass parliamentary deliberation**.
[3:7] - In news (2026): the **Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Amendment Ordinance, 2026** (judges 33 → 37 besides the CJI) revived the debate on **ordinance misuse and executive overreach**.
[3:8] - AP: the State used a Governor's ordinance (**Art. 213**) on **organ-transplant committees**.

## SUB 4 | Pardoning Power – Article 72: Forms and Nature
[4:0] - **Art. 72**: the President may "grant **pardons, reprieves, respites or remissions** of punishment or **suspend, remit or commute** the sentence of any person convicted of any offence" – not Arts. 62, 63, 70, 71, 73, 74 or 75.
[4:1] TABLE head: Form | Meaning
  [4:1.0] Pardon | Removes both sentence and conviction – as if the offence was never committed
  [4:1.1] Commutation | Substitutes a lighter form of punishment (death → rigorous life imprisonment)
  [4:1.2] Remission | Reduces the period without changing its character (2 years RI → 1 year RI)
  [4:1.3] Respite | A lesser sentence for special reasons – pregnancy, physical or mental disability
  [4:1.4] Reprieve | Temporary stay of execution (esp. death) to allow time to seek a pardon
[4:2] - Commutation changes the **form**; remission reduces the **duration**.
[4:3] - Covers: (a) sentence by a **court-martial**; (b) offence against a **Union law**; (c) every **death sentence**. Amnesty is outside Art. 72.
[4:4] - Nature: listed among the President's **judicial powers** (UPPCS key: judicial power); strictly it is an **executive power, independent of the judiciary** – he is not a court of appeal.
[4:5] - Exercised on the **aid and advice of the Council of Ministers** (**Maru Ram, 1980**) – he **cannot** act without the Central Government's advice; subject to **limited judicial review** (mala fide, arbitrary, irrelevant grounds). So UPSC 2025 → **I only**.
[4:6] ~~ Sources: NextGen Polity - APPSC Exams p.383 (src/polity/nextgen-polity-full.txt); Rocket Polity Part 1 p.23, p.99-100 (src/polity/rocket-polity-01.txt); PMF Polity Notes Part 2 p.3-4, p.5 (UPSC 2025 PYQ); Constitution of India Art. 72
## SUB 5 | President (Art. 72) vs Governor (Art. 161) and Clemency Cases
[5:0] - **Both the President (Art. 72) and the Governor (Art. 161)** have pardoning power – not the Chief Justice.
[5:1] TABLE head: Point | President (Art. 72) | Governor (Art. 161)
  [5:1.0] Offences | Against Union laws | Against State laws
  [5:1.1] Death sentence | Can pardon | Cannot pardon; can suspend, remit or commute
  [5:1.2] Court-martial | Yes | No
[5:2] - So "the Governor can pardon a sentence of death" is **false**; the Governor is part of the State Legislature, does not appoint HC judges and has no emergency powers – those statements are true.
[5:3] TABLE head: Case | Holding
  [5:3.0] Maru Ram (1980) | Power exercised on the Council of Ministers' advice
  [5:3.1] Kehar Singh (decided 1988, reported AIR 1989) | President may examine the evidence afresh; no right of oral hearing
  [5:3.2] Epuru Sudhakar v Govt of AP (2006) | Limited judicial review – mala fide, arbitrary or irrelevant grounds

## SUB 6 | State Bills Reserved for the President – Articles 200 and 201
[6:0] - **Art. 200**: the Governor may **assent, withhold, return** (non-Money Bill) or **reserve** a Bill for the President; the **Governor has no pocket veto**.
[6:1] - **Art. 201**: for a reserved Bill the President **declares or withholds assent**, or directs its return (non-Money Bill); no time limit is written in Arts. 200 or 201.
[6:2] - **8 April 2025, State of Tamil Nadu v Governor**: indefinite inaction under **Art. 200** held **justiciable**; **ten** TN Bills deemed assented.
[6:3] - **Nov 2025, Presidential reference (Art. 143, five judges)**: courts **cannot fix timelines** for the Governor or President; **no deemed assent**; the powers cannot be used arbitrarily.
