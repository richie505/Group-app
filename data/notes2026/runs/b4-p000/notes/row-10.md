=== Measurement – Magnitude, Intensity, Scales and Instruments
| Feature | Magnitude | Intensity |
|---|---|---|
| Measures | **Energy released at the focus** (from the **amplitude of seismic waves**) | **Degree of shaking and damage** observed at a place |
| Nature | Quantitative; **one value** per earthquake | Qualitative; **varies from place to place**, falls with distance from the epicentre |
| Scale | **Richter** (Charles F. Richter, **1935**); now **Moment Magnitude (Mw)** | **Modified Mercalli (MMI)**, **MSK-64**, old Rossi-Forel – all **I to XII** |
- **Richter scale:** logarithmic, based on the **amplitude of seismic waves**; each whole number = **10× amplitude** and about **31–32× energy** (two steps ≈ 1,000×) – **not** a hundredfold rise in energy (UPSC 2001).
- **Moment magnitude (Mw):** based on the **rupture area of the fault** and the size of the waves; preferred over Richter for large quakes (Richter works best for regional quakes up to ~M5).
- **"Richter measures intensity"** is strictly wrong (Richter = magnitude, Mercalli = intensity), but when the options offer only Richter as a seismic scale, the keyed answer is Richter (APPSC 2017, UKPSC 2002, WBCS 2020). When both are offered, intensity = **Mercalli** (APPSC 2018).
- **Mercalli scale** → measures **earthquakes** (intensity, from observed effects) – not cyclones, floods or tornadoes. **Mercator** is a map projection, not a seismic scale.
- **MSK-64** (Medvedev–Sponheuer–Karnik) – the intensity scale recommended in **1964** at the UNESCO intergovernmental meeting; used in India (BIS zones are defined in MSK).
| Magnitude | Effect (PMF / USGS) |
|---|---|
| below 3.0 | Micro/minor – recorded, rarely felt, **no damage** |
| 3.0–3.9 | Often felt, very rarely damage |
| 4.0–4.9 | Light – felt by most, none to minimal damage |
| 5.0–5.9 | Moderate – damages poorly built houses |
| 6.0–6.9 | Strong (Latur 1993 6.2–6.4) |
| 7.0–7.9 | Major (Bhuj 2001 Mw 7.7) |
| 8.0 + | Great (Assam–Tibet 1950 8.6; Chile 1960 9.5) |
# Instruments
| Instrument | Use |
|---|---|
| **Seismograph** | **Detects and records seismic waves**; the readings give magnitude (and, in the books, "the device for intensity") |
| Seismometer | The sensor (pendulum / spring mass) inside a seismograph |
| Seismogram | The recording itself |
| Seismoscope | Only shows that a tremor occurred and its time |
| Tiltmeter / Creepmeter | Ground tilt / slow slip (creep) along a fault |
> Most-asked angle: Richter = magnitude, Mercalli/MSK = intensity, seismograph = recording device.

=== Focus, Epicentre and Basic Terms
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Focus / hypocentre** | Point **inside the Earth where the earthquake originates** – energy is released here |
| **Epicentre** | Point on the surface **directly above** the focus; **first surface point to receive the waves**; **intensity and damage are greatest here** |
| **Isoseismal line** | Joins places of **equal intensity** |
| **Homoseismal (coseismal) line** | Joins places where the shock arrives **at the same time** (CDS 2014) |
| **Seismology** | Branch of geology that studies earthquakes; the scientist = **seismologist** |
- **John Milne** = **Father of Modern Seismology** (horizontal seismograph).
- Wrong statement traps: "epicentre is directly **below** the focus"; "the point of origin is the epicentre".
- **Depth of focus:** shallow **0–70 km**, intermediate 70–300 km, deep 300–700 km. **Shallow-focus** quakes are the **most damaging** (energy hits a small area); deep quakes spread energy over a wide area.
- Foreshock (before), aftershocks (after), swarm (many small quakes); **seismic doublet** = two independent quakes of similar magnitude close in time and place.
- Rare continental-mantle quakes start 80+ km below the Moho; **Stanford** made the first global map of them. **26 Jun 2026**: USGS-described doublet at **Caracas, Venezuela** (M 7.1 and 7.5) (app CA).

=== Earthquake as a Disaster – Classification and Prediction
- An earthquake is a **natural**, **geological** disaster – **not** an atmospheric one (blizzards, lightning, hailstorms are atmospheric). War, industrial and train accidents are man-made.
- **High Powered Committee (J.C. Pant, 1999)** – "Geologically related disasters": **landslides and mudflows, earthquakes, dam failures/dam bursts, mine fires**. Cattle epidemics = biological. **Sea surge / storm surge** = hydro-meteorological, **not** geological.
- **Prediction:** earthquakes **cannot yet be predicted accurately** (time, place, magnitude); science only gives **probabilities** of future events from data. Thunderstorms, floods, hailstorms can be forecast to an extent.
- Possible precursors studied: foreshocks, changes in well-water and **oil-well flow** (pore pressure rising as tectonic stress builds – NDA 2013), radon emission, animal behaviour.
- **Tremors** are not caused only by earthquakes – a **meteor impact**, volcanic eruption, explosion or collapse can also shake the ground.

=== Causes and Types of Earthquakes
| Type | Cause | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Tectonic** | Sudden release of stress along a **fault** at plate boundaries – the main cause (**tectonism**) | Himalaya; San Andreas (transform) |
| Volcanic | Magma movement, eruptions; usually milder | Ring of Fire volcanoes |
| **Reservoir-induced** | Water load and seepage reactivate an **old fault** | **Koyna 1967** (M 6.3), Maharashtra |
| **Intraplate** | Hidden old faults in the stable shield | **Latur (Killari) 1993**, Jabalpur 1997, Bhuj (Kutch) |
| Human-induced (other) | Mining, fracking, nuclear tests; low magnitude | – |
- Earthquakes are most likely **near faults**. UPSC 2004: **all four** – development of a fault, movement along a fault, volcanic eruption, folding of rocks – can cause earthquakes.
- **Koyna**: dam on an **old fault plane**, activated by changes in reservoir water level → region likely to become more quake-prone (R explains A).
- **Latur 1993**: far from any plate boundary → quakes can occur in **stable areas along hidden faults**.
- Denudation, rotation and revolution do **not** cause earthquakes. San Andreas movement is tectonic, not induced.
- **AP**: reservoir-triggered tremors possible near **Nagarjuna Sagar, Srisailam, Polavaram** and old Eastern Ghats faults.

=== Seismic Zones of India and Andhra Pradesh
| Zone | Risk | MSK intensity | Zone factor | Areas |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **V** | Very high – **most earthquake-prone** | IX + | 0.36 | Entire North-East (Shillong, Guwahati, Imphal), J&K, Ladakh, Himachal, Uttarakhand, north Bihar (Darbhanga), **Kutch (Bhuj)**, A&N |
| **IV** | High | VIII | 0.24 | **New Delhi**, rest of Bihar, parts of UP, Punjab, Himalayan foothills |
| III | Moderate | VII | 0.16 | Kerala, **Goa**, coastal areas, Chennai, **Vijayawada**, much of coastal AP |
| **II** | Low – **least active** | VI or less | 0.10 | Stable Peninsula: **Visakhapatnam**, Hyderabad |
- India has **four** seismic zones (IS 1893: 2002/2016); **Zone I was merged into Zone II** after **Latur 1993**. About **56–59%** of India's area lies in Zones III–V.
- **Andhra Pradesh** lies in **Zones II and III**; it has **no area in Zone V** (Assam, Bihar, J&K do) (APPSC FRO).
- Most of India's earthquakes occur in the **Himalayan belt** (many longitudinal thrusts – MBT, MCT, HFT); the **Karnataka plateau** is not a high-intensity zone.

=== Effects – Tsunami, Landslides, Liquefaction
- **Tsunami** (Japanese word) = large waves from sudden **displacement of the sea floor**; most commonly caused by **undersea (sea) earthquakes**; also submarine landslides, volcanic eruptions, explosions, meteor impacts – **not** heavy rain, wind storms or deserts.
- Tsunamigenic conditions: magnitude above ~6.5, **shallow focus** (< ~70 km), **vertical** sea-floor movement; over 80% from megathrust subduction quakes. For a tsunami to hit the **Indian coast** the quake must be **above M 7**. **Not all** major earthquakes are tsunamigenic; slow-rupture ("tsunami earthquakes") make **larger** tsunamis than their magnitude suggests.
- India's tsunami warning: **ITEWC at INCOIS, Hyderabad** (set up after the **2004** tsunami).
| Effect | Where / why |
|---|---|
| Ground shaking and rupture | Collapse of buildings, dams, bridges; ~95% of quake deaths in poorly built 1–3 storey houses |
| Landslides | Fragile Himalaya and North-East |
| Liquefaction | Water-saturated soft soils – Indo-Gangetic Plain |
| Fires | Broken gas and power lines (San Francisco 1906, Tokyo 1923) |
| Floods, seiches | Dam damage; standing waves in lakes and reservoirs |
~~ Sources: NextGen Rocket Disaster Management p.10-12 (tsunami, ITEWC); PMF Physical Geography 02 p.102 (book p.189, effects); INCOIS / NDMA tsunami FAQ (M > 7 for the Indian coast); app notes 4:10 sub 6

=== Seismic Waves
| Wave | Arrival | Motion | Medium |
|---|---|---|---|
| **P (primary)** | **First – fastest** (~1.7× S) | Longitudinal, push-pull | Solid, liquid, gas |
| S (secondary) | Second | Transverse | **Solids only** |
| **Surface (L): Love + Rayleigh** | Last – slowest | Love side to side; Rayleigh rolling | Surface only; **most damage** |
- Most surface damage = **R & L (Rayleigh and Love) waves**; P-waves are the least destructive.
- **S-wave shadow zone** beyond ~**103–105°** → **outer core is liquid**; P-wave shadow 103°–142°.
