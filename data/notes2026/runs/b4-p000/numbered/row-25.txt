## SUB 0 | El Nino – Meaning, Mechanism and Effects
[0:0] # What El Nino is (asked 9 times)
[0:1] - **El Nino** = an **abnormally warm ocean current** that appears off the coast of **Peru and Ecuador** in the **eastern (and central) tropical Pacific** and flows south along the west coast of South America, **replacing the cold Peruvian (Humboldt) current**. It is an **ocean** event, not an air mass, a wind or a low-pressure centre (CDS 2011).
[0:2] - **Ocean:** El Nino develops only in the **Pacific Ocean** – not in the Indian Ocean, Bay of Bengal, Atlantic or Mediterranean.
[0:3] - **Name:** Spanish for **"the little boy / Christ child"**, because it appears around **Christmas (December)**.
[0:4] - **Recurrence:** **irregular**, about every **3–7 years** (2–7 in some books); it does **not occur every year** (MPPSC 2024 wrong statement).
[0:5] - The El Nino current carries **warmer, less saline** water (the warm, rain-freshened pool of the western Pacific spreads east) – NDA 2010 "all four" statements.
[0:6] - **Atmospheric counterpart = the Southern Oscillation**; together they are **ENSO**.
[0:7] # Mechanism
[0:8] TABLE head: Step | Normal year | El Nino year
  [0:8.0] Pressure | Low over Indonesia / N Australia, high off Peru | Pressure falls over the central/eastern Pacific; a weak high forms in the west
  [0:8.1] Trade winds | Strong easterlies push warm water west | Weaken or even reverse
  [0:8.2] Peru coast | Cold, nutrient-rich upwelling – rich fishing | Equatorial counter current piles warm water off Peru; thermocline drops; upwelling stops
  [0:8.3] Peru sea temperature | Cold | Warms by up to about 10°C
  [0:8.4] Plankton and fish | Abundant (anchoveta) | Plankton falls; fish catch collapses – the warm water does not increase plankton (UPPSC 2023: statement 2 false)
[0:9] # World effects
[0:10] TABLE head: Region | El Nino effect
  [0:10.0] Peru, Ecuador, coastal Colombia, N Chile | Heavy rain and flooding of the normally arid coast (APPSC FSO "planetary climatic event")
  [0:10.1] Indonesia, northern Australia | Drought, drier
  [0:10.2] Central Pacific | Convective storms, hurricanes
  [0:10.3] India | Delayed, weak monsoon; drought (e.g. 2014 deficit, about 12% below normal; 2014–16 El Nino droughts)
[0:11] - APPSC Group 1: Peru warm current + "Christ child" + 2014 Indian rainfall deficit = **all three correct**.

## SUB 1 | ENSO, La Nina and the Indian Monsoon
[1:0] # Southern Oscillation and ENSO
[1:1] - **Southern Oscillation** = a **pressure seesaw between the Pacific and Indian Oceans** – **Tahiti (French Polynesia) vs Port Darwin (N Australia)**; first described by **Sir Gilbert Walker (1920s)**.
[1:2] - **SOI** = Tahiti minus Darwin pressure. **Positive SOI** (high at Tahiti, low at Darwin) = good Indian monsoon, **La Nina**-like; sustained **negative SOI = El Nino**, bad monsoon.
[1:3] - **ENSO** = El Nino + Southern Oscillation; detected and used for **short-term (seasonal) climate prediction** of **weak Indian monsoon** years (UPSC 2002, asked twice).
[1:4] - **Monsoon and ENSO are inversely related** (APPSC Fisheries ADF).
[1:5] # El Nino vs La Nina
[1:6] TABLE head: Feature | El Nino | La Nina
  [1:6.0] Central/east Pacific sea temperature | Warmer | Cooler (strong upwelling)
  [1:6.1] Trade winds | Weak / reversed | Stronger than usual
  [1:6.2] Walker circulation | Weakens, shifts east | Strengthens
  [1:6.3] Indian monsoon | Weak, below normal – drought | Normal to above normal – good rain
  [1:6.4] North India winter | – | Colder, frequent cold waves (e.g. January 2012)
  [1:6.5] Bay of Bengal | Fewer post-monsoon cyclones (INCOIS study) | More floods, severe cyclones
  [1:6.6] Australia / Indonesia | Drier | Heavy rain, floods (2010–11 Australian floods)
[1:7] - **La Nina** is a cooling of the **central/eastern equatorial Pacific** – **not the Indian Ocean** – and it **does affect** the monsoon (favourably). So both statements of UPSC 2011 are **wrong**.
[1:8] - Over India, El Nino = harbinger of drought; **La Nina brings rain** (APPSC Group 1). A **positive IOD** can also offset El Nino, but the Pacific opposite of El Nino is La Nina.
[1:9] - Factor affecting India's climate among El Nino, Gulf Stream, western intensification, North Atlantic Oscillation = **El Nino**.
[1:10] - Not every El Nino year is a drought year: about **6 of 10** El Nino years brought depressed Indian rainfall; 1997–98 strong El Nino, no drought (positive IOD); moderate 2002 El Nino, severe drought.
[1:11] - El Nino and La Nina are **climate patterns, not storms**. Last global El Nino: **2023–24**; **2025** = warmest La Nina year on record.

## SUB 2 | Walker Circulation, El Nino Types and ENSO Monitoring
[2:0] # Walker circulation (normal year)
[2:1] - East–west (zonal) loop over the tropical Pacific: air **rises over the warm western Pacific / Indonesia**, flows east aloft, **sinks over the cool eastern Pacific**; surface **easterly trade winds** return west. Driven by **high pressure over the eastern Pacific and low over Indonesia**; linked to the Peru upwelling.
[2:2] - El Nino **weakens** (sometimes reverses) the Walker cell and shifts it east; La Nina strengthens it.
[2:3] - **Bjerknes feedback:** weaker trades → warm water shifts east → winds weaken further → air sinks over the Indian Ocean (high pressure) → **late or weak Indian monsoon**.
[2:4] # Types of El Nino
[2:5] TABLE head: Type | Maximum warming
  [2:5.0] Canonical (conventional) | Eastern Pacific, near Peru
  [2:5.1] El Nino Modoki (Japanese: "similar but different") | Central Pacific, with cooler water on both the eastern and western sides (two-cell Walker circulation)
[2:6] # Monitoring and 2026 outlook
[2:7] - **Nino 3.4 region:** **5°N–5°S, 120°W–170°W** (central equatorial Pacific).
[2:8] - **NOAA:** El Nino when the **Oceanic Nino Index** (3-month SST anomaly in Nino 3.4) is **≥ +0.5°C** for **5 consecutive overlapping seasons**. IOD is measured by the **Dipole Mode Index (DMI)**.
[2:9] - **IMD, 13 April 2026:** southwest monsoon forecast **92% of LPA (±5%)** – below normal – as **El Nino** was expected to develop during the season (LPA 87 cm).

## SUB 3 | Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD) and Madden-Julian Oscillation (MJO)
[3:0] # Indian Ocean Dipole ("Indian Nino")
[3:1] - **IOD** = difference in **sea-surface temperature** between **two poles of the tropical Indian Ocean**: a **western pole (Arabian Sea / off Africa)** and an **eastern pole (south of Indonesia)** – **not the eastern Pacific** (UPSC 2017 statement 1 false).
[3:2] - Identified in **1999** (Saji et al., IISc); develops from about April, peaks in October; atmospheric part = **EQUINOO**.
[3:3] TABLE head: IOD phase | West (Arabian Sea) | East (south of Indonesia) | India
  [3:3.0] Positive | Warmer | Cooler, dry | More monsoon rain; can offset El Nino (1983, 1994, 1997); more Arabian Sea cyclones
  [3:3.1] Negative | Cooler | Warmer, rainy | Reduced rainfall; more Bay of Bengal cyclones
[3:4] - **An IOD can influence an El Nino's impact on the monsoon** (UPSC 2017 statement 2 true → answer **2 only**). In **1997** a strong **positive IOD** saved India from drought despite a **Super El Nino**.
[3:5] - The IOD is a separate Indian Ocean mode that **runs independently of ENSO** but interacts with it; positive IOD events often develop in El Nino years. (NextGen's notes pair the positive phase with La Nina – PMF and Rocket give the effect on India as above.)
[3:6] # Madden-Julian Oscillation
[3:7] - Identified by **Roland Madden and Paul Julian (1971–72)**: an **eastward**-moving pulse of clouds and rain that starts over the warm **tropical Indian Ocean** and fades in the eastern Pacific.
[3:8] - Recurs every **30–60 days**; convection up to about 10 km; causes **active and break spells** of the monsoon.
