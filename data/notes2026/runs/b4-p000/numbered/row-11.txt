## SUB 0 | Weathering – Physical, Chemical and Biological
[0:0] - **Weathering:** the natural **breaking down and decay of rocks in situ** (at or near the surface) into fragments, clays and dissolved matter; **no transport** is involved. Erosion then carries the loosened material away.
[0:1] - **Geomorphic processes:** the processes that shape the Earth's surface – weathering, mass wasting, erosion, deposition (endogenic forces build relief, exogenic forces wear it down).
[0:2] TABLE head: Type | Process | What happens / example
  [0:2.0] Physical (mechanical) | Exfoliation, frost wedging (freeze-thaw), salt-crystal growth, block disintegration | Breaks rock without chemical change; driven mainly by temperature variation – typical of hot deserts and cold mountains
  [0:2.1] Chemical | Solution | Soluble minerals (rock salt, gypsum) dissolve
  [0:2.2] Chemical | Carbonation | CO₂ in rain forms carbonic acid, dissolves limestone (karst) and stores carbon as bicarbonate
  [0:2.3] Chemical | Oxidation | Oxygen dissolved in water reacts with iron minerals – rusting → red/yellow soils
  [0:2.4] Chemical | Hydration | Minerals absorb water and swell: anhydrite → gypsum
  [0:2.5] Chemical | Hydrolysis | Water (H⁺/hydronium) reacts with silicates: feldspar → clay; muscovite → vermiculite when interlayer potassium is replaced by hydronium (APPSC FRO)
  [0:2.6] Biological | Living things | Plant roots widen cracks; burrowing animals (earthworms, termites, rodents); lichens release acids
[0:3] - **Climate control:** chemical weathering is **fastest in hot and humid (wet)** climates – heat and abundant water speed up reactions (tropics); it is slowest in cold or dry climates.
[0:4] - **Rain as a weathering agent (UPSC 2024):** rainwater holds **CO₂ in solution** (→ carbonic acid, carbonation) **and atmospheric oxygen** (→ oxidation); **both** are correct and **both explain** why rainfall weathers rocks.
[0:5] - **NDA A–R:** "decay and disintegration of rock in situ is weathering" (true) and "mechanical weathering is mainly caused by temperature variation" (true) – but R does **not** explain A.
[0:6] - Exfoliation is **physical**, not chemical; hydration, oxidation, solution, carbonation, hydrolysis are chemical. Oxidation is weathering, **not mass wasting**.
[0:7] TABLE head: Weathering landform | Meaning
  [0:7.0] Exfoliation dome | Onion-like peeling of granite (unloading or heating-cooling)
  [0:7.1] Tor | Residual granite blocks standing above the weathered surface where joints are widely spaced
  [0:7.2] Block disintegration | Physical break-up of jointed rock into blocks
[0:8] - **AP:** granite/gneiss weathers to **red soil**; basalt to **black soil**. Glacier-fed Himalayan catchments release CO₂ through **pyrite oxidation** (app CA, Sep 2026). Freeze-thaw breaks rocks in the high Himalaya.
[0:9] > Most-asked: hot-humid = fastest chemical weathering; rain = CO₂ + O₂; exfoliation = physical; muscovite → vermiculite = hydrolysis.

## SUB 1 | Soil Texture, Water-holding and Soil Properties
[1:0] TABLE head: Particle | Diameter (USDA)
  [1:0.0] Sand (fine sand) | 0.05–2 mm
  [1:0.1] Silt | 0.002–0.05 mm
  [1:0.2] Clay | Less than 0.002 mm
[1:1] - **Water-holding capacity:** **clay > silt > sand** – the smaller the particle, the more water held; sand drains fastest.
[1:2] - **Clay** holds the **maximum** water, but its pores are **small, not large** (high total porosity, tiny pores). So in the UPPSC A–R: A true, **R false**.
[1:3] - **Loam:** contains **all three** particle types in roughly balanced proportions ("equal sand, silt and clay" = loamy soil); a typical loam ≈ **40% sand, 42% silt, 18% clay**; best for farming.
[1:4] - Clay binds organic carbon; sandy soils lose it by leaching (app CA, Nov 2025).
[1:5] - **Rainforest soils are poor** despite lush forest: heavy rain **leaches** nutrients and dead matter decomposes rapidly.
[1:6] - **UPSC 2018 (agricultural soils):** high organic matter **raises** (not reduces) water-holding capacity; soil **does** take part in the **sulphur cycle**; **long irrigation can cause salinisation** – only the third statement is correct.
[1:7] > Clay < 0.002 mm (asked twice); clay > silt > sand.
[1:8] ~~ Sources: PMF Physical Geography 07 p.10-13 (soils); NCERT Class 11 Fundamentals of Physical Geography ch.6 (soil formation); USDA soil texture classes; UPSC CSE 2018 answer key; app notes 4:11 sub 6
## SUB 2 | Soil Formation – Pedology, Factors and Profile
[2:0] - **Pedology** = the study of soils in their natural setting (soil science); **petrology** = study of rocks; geology = study of the Earth.
[2:1] - **Five classical factors:** **parent material, climate, relief/topography, organisms (biological activity), time** – **latitude is NOT** one.
[2:2] TABLE head: Active factors | Passive factors
  [2:2.0] Climate, biological activity (vegetation, micro-organisms) – supply energy and drive weathering and organic processes | Parent material, topography (relief), time
[2:3] - **APPSC G1:** active factors = climate and biological activity (**III and IV only**).
[2:4] - Soils are **surveyed and classified** on the basis of the distinctive characteristics of their **soil horizons** (the soil profile).
[2:5] TABLE head: Horizon | Character
  [2:5.0] O | Surface organic layer of decaying plant matter
  [2:5.1] A | Topsoil: minerals + organic matter; most plant roots
  [2:5.2] E | Light-coloured leached layer (not in every profile)
  [2:5.3] B | Subsoil: leached minerals collect
  [2:5.4] C | Weathered parent material, little organic matter
  [2:5.5] R | Unweathered bedrock
[2:6] - **AP:** climate, altitude and **bedrock** shape the soils; river silt of the **Godavari, Krishna and Pennar** is among AP's parent materials.
[2:7] ~~ Sources: NCERT Class 11 Fundamentals of Physical Geography ch.6 (soil-forming factors; OnlyIAS NCERT Physical Geography 05 p.12-14); PMF Physical Geography 07 p.10-13; app notes 4:11 sub 5, 7
## SUB 3 | World Soil Types – Zonal, Intrazonal, Azonal; Residual and Transported
[3:0] TABLE head: Group | Meaning | Examples
  [3:0.0] Zonal | Mature soils reflecting climate and vegetation | Podzol, chernozem, laterite, sierozem (desert grey)
  [3:0.1] Intrazonal | Local conditions (drainage, salts) dominate | Solonchak (saline), bog soils
  [3:0.2] Azonal | Young, no developed profile | Lithosol (thin soil on rock), alluvium, regosol
[3:1] - Major zonal and other world soils:
[3:2] TABLE head: Soil | Climate / setting
  [3:2.0] Podzol | Cold taiga (boreal conifer); thin, acidic, leached, humus-poor
  [3:2.1] Laterite | Hot, heavy-rain tropics; silica and lime leached, iron and aluminium oxides left; acidic
  [3:2.2] Terra rossa | Mediterranean; red clay from the weathering of limestone (and dolomite)
  [3:2.3] Chernozem | Temperate grasslands (steppes, prairies); black, humus-rich
  [3:2.4] Oxisols | Equatorial-tropical surfaces stable for very long (APPSC 2019)
[3:3] - **In-situ / residual = sedentary** soils (formed where the rock weathered; mainly Peninsular India). **Transported (ex-situ)** by rivers, wind, ice – mainly North India; alluvium ≈ **45.6%** of India.
[3:4] - **AP cultivated land (Jul 2026):** **red ~60%** (Rayalaseema), **black (regur) ~25%**, deltaic alluvium ~6%, **laterite ~1%**.
[3:5] - **FAO Status of the World's Soil Resources 2026** (3 Sep 2026): threats led by **soil erosion**; soil biodiversity loss ranked **4th of seven**, after erosion, loss of soil organic carbon and nutrient mismanagement.
[3:6] ~~ Sources: PMF Physical Geography 07 p.10-13 (world soils); USDA/FAO zonal–azonal classification (Britannica "azonal soil", "terra rossa"); app notes 4:11 sub 8 (AP shares, FAO 2026 app CA)
## SUB 4 | Denudation and the Cycle of Erosion
[4:0] - **Denudation** = weathering + mass wasting + erosion + transportation; order: **weathering** (first stage) → mass wasting/erosion → transportation → **deposition**.
[4:1] - Agents: **running water** (most powerful), wind, ice, waves, gravity. Water = strongest cause of soil erosion (**sheet → rill → gully**; Chambal ravines = gully erosion); **wind** leads in arid areas like the **Thar**.
[4:2] TABLE head: Person | Contribution
  [4:2.0] W.M. Davis (1899) | "Father of (American) geomorphology"; geographical cycle of erosion: landscape = structure + process + stage; youth → maturity → old age; ends in a near-flat peneplain with monadnocks; renewed uplift = rejuvenation
  [4:2.1] Walther Penck | Uplift and erosion act simultaneously; landform depends on their ratio; slopes retreat parallel
[4:3] - Davis put forward the cycle in **1899**.
[4:4] TABLE head: Term | Meaning
  [4:4.0] Peneplain | Low, almost featureless plain worn by rivers almost to base level (Davis's term)
  [4:4.1] Monadnock | Resistant residual hill on a peneplain
  [4:4.2] Inselberg | Isolated residual hill of arid/semi-arid regions (Uluru)
[4:5] - **Peninsular plateau** of India = old peneplain, **rejuvenated** in places. Wegener = continental drift, Dutton = isostasy – not geomorphology's father.
[4:6] ~~ Sources: NCERT Class 11 Fundamentals of Physical Geography ch.6-7 (OnlyIAS NCERT Physical Geography 05 p.4-5; 06 p.1-2); PMF Physical Geography 02 p.106 (book p.216, peneplain); Britannica "William Morris Davis"; app notes 4:11 sub 0, 4
## SUB 5 | Mass Wasting
[5:0] - Downslope movement of rock and earth material **under gravity** (no transporting medium) = mass movement; the common term in exams = **landslide** (APPSC 2019).
[5:1] TABLE head: Type | Nature
  [5:1.0] Rockfall | Free fall of rocks from steep cliffs
  [5:1.1] Slide | Whole coherent mass slips along a surface
  [5:1.2] Slump | Rotational slip along a curved surface in weak, wet soils
  [5:1.3] Debris flow / mudflow / earthflow | Fast water-soil-rock mix after heavy rain or snowmelt
  [5:1.4] Creep | Very slow movement from wetting-drying, heating-cooling
[5:2] - Conditions: slopes **above ~30 degrees**, weak/fractured rock, bare slopes; saturated clay and silt.
[5:3] - India's main trigger: **SW monsoon rainfall**, then **earthquakes**. Most at risk: **Himalaya and North-East** (also Western Ghats).
[5:4] ~~ Sources: NCERT Class 11 Fundamentals of Physical Geography ch.6 (OnlyIAS NCERT Physical Geography 05 p.9-11, mass movements); PMF Physical Geography 01 p.83 (earthflow, slump); app notes 4:11 sub 3
