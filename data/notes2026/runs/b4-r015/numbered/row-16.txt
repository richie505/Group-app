## SUB 1 | Pressure Belts – Doldrums, Horse Latitudes, ITCZ
[1:0] # The belts (asked 11 times)
[1:1] TABLE head: Belt | Latitude | Other name / nature
  [1:1.0] Equatorial low | 0–5° N and S, on both sides of the Equator | Doldrums – calm, rising air; NE and SE trades converge (ITCZ)
  [1:1.1] Subtropical high | 30–35° N and S | Horse latitudes – calm, sinking dry air, hot deserts; dynamically induced
  [1:1.2] Subpolar low | 60–65° | Westerlies meet polar easterlies; storms; dynamically induced
  [1:1.3] Polar high | Poles (90°) | Cold, dense, sinking air; thermally induced
[1:2] - **ITCZ** = low-pressure zone **at / near the Equator** where the **north-east and south-east trades converge** (APPSC Asst. Chemist; MPPCS 2018; CDS 2007) – not at the Tropics or poles.
[1:3] - **Doldrums** = the **equatorial low-pressure belt** (NDA 2007) with calm winds – **not** strong winds (APPSC Group-1: "doldrums are strong winds" and "NH trades blow from NW" are both wrong → **neither**).
[1:4] - **Horse latitudes** ~**30° (30–35°)** – sub-tropical high, **dynamically induced** by sinking air of the Hadley cell, calm (NDA 2012); sailors threw horses overboard when becalmed.
[1:5] - **Hadley cell**: air rises at the ITCZ and **descends at the subtropical high-pressure cells** (~30°) (CDS 2020).
[1:6] - Subpolar low at 60–65° instead of a high (IAS 2002): converging westerlies and polar easterlies; the lows are semi-permanent **over oceans** (Aleutian, Icelandic) – both true, but R does not explain A.
[1:7] - Belts **shift 5–10°** with the overhead Sun – **north in the NH summer**, south in winter.
[1:8] ~~ Sources: PMF Physical Geography 05 p.5-7; NCERT Fundamentals of Physical Geography ch.10; app notes 4:16 subs 2-3
## SUB 2 | Atmospheric Pressure – Causes, Gradient and Instruments
[2:0] # Pressure and weather (asked 10 times)
[2:1] - Atmospheric pressure = **weight of the air column**, held by **gravitational pull** (APPSC Panchayat Secretary).
[2:2] - Heated air **expands → density falls → low pressure**; cooled air contracts → high pressure.
[2:3] - Pressure **falls with altitude** → at high altitudes some people get **nose bleeding** (low pressure) (APPSC AE).
[2:4] - **Standard sea-level pressure 1013.25 mb (hPa)**; **isobars** join places of equal pressure.
[2:5] - **Pressure gradient** decides **wind velocity** (APPSC Group-II 2011): closely spaced isobars = steep gradient = strong wind. Air moves high → low.
[2:6] - **Sudden fall of the barometer** (mercury) = **stormy weather** (UKPSC 2002; IAS 2001); rising = fair weather.
[2:7] - A **low-pressure area** with converging winds = **cyclone**; high = anticyclone.
[2:8] # Instruments
[2:9] TABLE head: Instrument | Measures
  [2:9.0] Barometer (mercury / aneroid), barograph | Atmospheric pressure (APPSC; NDA 2018)
  [2:9.1] Aneroid barometer | No liquid – an evacuated metal box
  [2:9.2] Anemometer | Wind speed
  [2:9.3] Wind vane | Wind direction
[2:10] ~~ Sources: NextGen Rocket General Geography #7 p.51 (pressure); PMF Physical Geography 05 p.1-3; app notes 4:16 sub 0
## SUB 3 | Jet Streams and the Polar Vortex
[3:0] # Jet streams (asked 8 times)
[3:1] - Narrow bands of **high-velocity westerly winds** in the **upper troposphere near the tropopause** (8–15 km; generally **10–12 km**) (NDA 2011; CDS 2017).
[3:2] - Found in **both hemispheres** – not the NH only (UPSC 2020); NH jets stronger.
[3:3] - **Sub-tropical jet** forms at about **10–16 km** (option "10–20 km", APPSC AD 2017); polar front jet lower (6–9 km).
[3:4] - **Polar front jet is strongest in winter**, not summer (CDS 2017) – greatest temperature contrast.
[3:5] - Associated with the **monsoon** (APSLPRB SI 2023, 2018): they regulate onset and withdrawal.
[3:6] TABLE head: Jet | Position / season | Role
  [3:6.0] Sub-Tropical Westerly Jet | South of the Himalayas in winter, north in summer | Brings western disturbances; leaves before the monsoon "burst"; returns ~October
  [3:6.1] Tropical Easterly Jet | Summer, 5–20°N (South China Sea → East Africa) | Strengthens the SW monsoon
  [3:6.2] Somali (Findlater) Jet | Low-level, off East Africa | Boosts monsoon flow
[3:7] - NCERT: in winter the westerly jet blows across Asia **north of the Himalayas, parallel to the Tibetan highlands**; **Tibet splits it into two branches**; the **southern branch** (south of the Himalayas) controls India's winter weather – not the northern branch → "1 and 2" (State PSC).
[3:8] - First identified by **Wasaburo Ooishi** (Japan, 1920s); widely known in **World War II**.
[3:9] # Polar vortex
[3:10] - Cold **low** around the poles, **anticlockwise** (NH), strongest in **winter**. A **strong vortex keeps cold air up north** (APPSC 2018); when weakened (Rossby waves, sudden stratospheric warming) cold spells reach mid-latitudes.
[3:11] ~~ Sources: PMF Physical Geography 06 p.24-25; NextGen Indian Geography 06 p.10, 13 (jets and monsoon); NCERT India Physical Environment ch.4; app notes 4:16 sub 6
## SUB 4 | Planetary Winds and Wind Controls
[4:0] # Planetary winds (asked 7 times)
[4:1] TABLE head: Wind | From → to | NH | SH
  [4:1.0] Trade winds (easterlies) | Horse latitudes (subtropical high) → equatorial low | North-east (blow towards the south-west) | South-east
  [4:1.1] Westerlies | Subtropical high → subpolar low (~35–60°) | South-west | North-west
  [4:1.2] Polar easterlies | Polar high → subpolar low | North-east | South-east
[4:2] - **Trade winds = planetary winds**; monsoon is periodic; Chinook and Mistral are local (IAS 2009).
[4:3] - Order from Equator to pole: **doldrums → trade winds → westerlies → polar winds** (NDA 2014).
[4:4] - Westerlies blow between about 35° and 60° in **each** hemisphere – not "30°N to 60°S"; the **western disturbances** that give NW India winter rain come with the westerlies (UPSC 2015 → only II).
[4:5] - **NW European ports** stay ice-free all year because of the warm **North Atlantic Drift**; the south-westerlies do blow there all year, but are not the reason (UPPCS 2002: both true, R not the explanation).
[4:6] - Trades push tropical cyclones **west onto east coasts**; westerlies turn them north-east.
[4:7] # Wind controls (asked 4 times)
[4:8] - Surface horizontal wind = **pressure gradient + friction + Coriolis force** (CDS 2015).
[4:9] - **Coriolis force**: deflects **right in NH, left in SH** (**Ferrel's law**); **zero at the Equator, maximum at the poles**; grows with wind speed; depends on latitude, **not on hemisphere**.
[4:10] - **Geostrophic wind**: upper air, parallel to isobars, when **pressure gradient force = Coriolis force** (UKPSC 2024).
[4:11] - **SW monsoon** direction: SE trades crossing the Equator are deflected to the **right by the Coriolis force** (NDA 2011).
[4:12] - **Friction** slows surface winds, strongest over land, negligible aloft. No cyclones within ~5° of the Equator (Coriolis negligible).
[4:13] - **Walker circulation** = east–west Pacific cell; Hadley cell = meridional.
[4:14] ~~ Sources: PMF Physical Geography 05 p.11-14; NextGen Indian Geography 03 p.9 (Coriolis); NCERT Fundamentals of Physical Geography ch.10; app notes 4:16 subs 1, 4
## SUB 5 | Roaring Forties, Furious Fifties, Shrieking Sixties
[5:0] # The southern westerlies (asked 7 times)
[5:1] TABLE head: Name | Latitude
  [5:1.0] Roaring Forties | 40–50° S (strong ocean westerlies, "40–60° S" option)
  [5:1.1] Furious Fifties | 50–60° S
  [5:1.2] Shrieking Sixties | Around 60° S
[5:2] - They are the **westerlies of the Southern Hemisphere** (MPPCS 2015; State PSC) – stronger and steadier because the SH has **less landmass** (open ocean, little friction) (IAS 2011 → only I; Coriolis is not higher in the SH).
[5:3] - UPSC 2000: they blow with **great strength and constancy**, generally **from north-west to east (west to east)** in the SH, with **overcast skies, rain and raw weather** → **2, 3 and 4**; they do **not** blow uninterrupted in the NH (land breaks them).
[5:4] - NDA 2018: strong westerlies of the SH oceans (true); they blow **west to east**, not east to west, and there is **no** abundance of landmasses → **only I**.
[5:5] - Region: the southern oceans, incl. the **Indian Ocean** (of the options given).
[5:6] ~~ Sources: PMF Physical Geography 05 p.13; G.C. Leong ch.12; app notes 4:16 sub 5
## SUB 6 | Monsoon and Periodic Winds
[6:0] # Monsoon (asked 6 times)
[6:1] - Word from Arabic **"mausim" = season** (MPPCS 2013; APPSC ASO 2017).
[6:2] - **Seasonal reversal of winds** due to **differential heating of land and sea** (State PSC).
[6:3] - Summer: **high temperature and low pressure** over the subcontinent draw air from the Indian Ocean → **south-west monsoon** (UPSC 1996), flowing **from south-west to north-east** (MPPCS 2012).
[6:4] - Modern theory (1950s): ITCZ shift + jet streams + Tibet Plateau + ENSO. Halley (1686): giant land–sea breeze.
[6:5] - Mediterranean (UPPCS 1992 key "monsoonal"): in winter air from the surrounding high-pressure lands flows towards the warmer sea, reversing in summer; its winter rain itself comes with the westerlies.
[6:6] - **El Niño** weakens the monsoon; **positive IOD** can offset it.
[6:7] - AP: SW monsoon 2026 entered via **Rayalaseema** (6 June 2026); IMD expected **below-normal** AP rain under El Niño. AP coastline **~1,053 km – third longest** (AP Socio-Economic Survey 2025-26).
[6:8] # Breezes
[6:9] TABLE head: Breeze | Time | Direction
  [6:9.0] Sea breeze | Day | Sea → land (land heats faster, lower pressure)
  [6:9.1] Land breeze | Night | Land → sea
  [6:9.2] Valley (anabatic) | Day | Uphill
  [6:9.3] Mountain (katabatic) | Night | Downhill; cold air in valleys → inversion, frost
[6:10] - Land and sea breezes are **daily**; they moderate **AP coastal** temperatures.
[6:11] ~~ Sources: NextGen Indian Geography 06 p.13 (monsoon theories); NCERT India Physical Environment ch.4; app notes 4:16 sub 7
