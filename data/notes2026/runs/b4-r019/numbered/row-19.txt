## SUB 0 | Cyclones of India and Andhra Pradesh – Exposure and Major Cyclones
[0:0] # Exposure (asked 25 times)
[0:1] - India's coast is exposed to nearly **10%** of the world's tropical cyclones (APPSC-GS 2023; NDMA). Coastline: old **7,516.6 km**; remeasured 2023–24 = **11,098.81 km**; about 5,900 km directly exposed.
[0:2] - **Bay of Bengal : Arabian Sea ≈ 4 : 1** – the Bay has about **4 to 5 times** more cyclones (75–80% of the total) because it is **warmer (high SST)**, enclosed by land and receives **remnants of Pacific typhoons**; its funnel shape worsens storm surges.
[0:3] - **Arabian Sea**: fewer cyclones but becoming **more intense**; 2019 Arabian Sea storms = **Vayu, Hikka, Kyarr, Maha, Pavan** (Pabuk, Fani, Bulbul were Bay systems).
[0:4] - **East coast** (Odisha, AP, WB, TN) is more cyclone-prone than the west coast; **Odisha** faces the most cyclone disasters.
[0:5] - Season **bimodal**: main peak **Oct–Dec** (retreating monsoon), secondary **Apr–May**; some at monsoon onset (June) and withdrawal (Sept). A May (pre-monsoon) cyclone is linked to **local temperature variation** (strong local heating).
[0:6] - Bay of Bengal cyclones move **west, north-west or north**, recurving north/north-east later; some cross the peninsula into the Arabian Sea and revive.
[0:7] - **INCOIS (Hyderabad)** 1995–2019 study: El Nino may **reduce** post-monsoon Bay cyclones (15 in 9 El Nino years vs 33 in 11 La Nina years).
[0:8] # Andhra Pradesh
[0:9] - AP coastline **1,053 km** (3rd longest); **cyclones are AP's main hazard** (more than floods, landslides, earthquakes); hit mostly **Oct–Dec** (Hudhud, Titli, Michaung, Montha).
[0:10] - IMD record 1891–2017: AP coast hit **77 times** by cyclones (34 kt+), **31 of them severe** cyclonic storms.
[0:11] - **Montha** (Oct 2025, Kakinada): RTGS **AWARE 2.0** auto-evacuations, 423 drones, alerts to 3.60 crore people, only 15 deaths.

  [1:1.1] Fujita / Enhanced Fujita (Ted Fujita) | Tornadoes – wind and damage; EF is the current standard (USA, 2007) | EF0–EF5 (6 categories)

[1:5] - **Super typhoon**: about 240 km/h (strong Cat 4–5); Super Typhoon **Bavi** = 3rd Cat-5 storm of 2026 (10 Jul 2026).

## SUB 3 | Tropical Cyclones – Formation and Where They Form
[3:0] # Conditions (asked 10 times)
[3:1] TABLE head: Condition | Role
  [3:1.0] Warm ocean, SST above 26.5–27°C over a large area | Heat and moisture – most essential
  [3:1.1] Large supply of warm, moist air | Latent heat of condensation = energy source
  [3:1.2] Coriolis force | Rotation; too weak within about 5° of the Equator
  [3:1.3] Unstable troposphere, high humidity | Vigorous convection
  [3:1.4] Low vertical wind shear | Keeps the vertical structure organised
  [3:1.5] Pre-existing disturbance; upper-level divergence | Seed low / cyclonic circulation
[3:2] - A cyclone is a **violent storm of strong wind and heavy rain**; form **only over warm oceans**, not rivers, lakes or ponds (APPSC-GS 2025).
[3:3] - Warm sea, high humidity and instability = **formative / initial stage** (NDA 2017).
[3:4] - **Moving away from the sea its force decreases** because the latent-heat (moisture) supply is cut off – weakens after **landfall** (A–R: both true, R explains A).
[3:5] # Where they form
[3:6] - Belt: **between 30°N and 30°S** (APPSC 2022); mainly **5°–30°** N and S, western parts of oceans; **not** in mid-latitudes and **none at the Equator** (no Coriolis).
[3:7] - **None in the South Atlantic and SE Pacific** – UPSC 2015 key: **ITCZ seldom occurs** there; also **cold currents** (Benguela, Humboldt) keep SST low.
[3:8] - Leong: typhoons at **6°–20° N and S**, most frequent **July–October**, winds **over 100 mph**, torrential rain with thunder and lightning.
[3:9] - Marine heatwaves raise cyclone winds by up to 20% and rain by up to 12% (2026 study).
[3:10] ~~ Sources: PMF Physical Geography 05 p.50, 06 p.48; NCERT Fundamentals ch.9 (OnlyIAS p.15–16); G.C. Leong ch.12 p.3; app notes 4:19 sub 1
## SUB 4 | Tornadoes, Thunderstorms and Pre-monsoon Local Storms
[4:0] # Tornadoes (asked 11 times)
[4:1] - Rotating funnel from a **cumulonimbus/supercell thunderstorm** to the ground; winds **105–322 km/h**; moves up to about 90–100 km/h; lasts **minutes** (rarely over 15–20).
[4:2] - **Land-based** (hurricanes, typhoons, willy-willies are ocean-born); much **smaller** than hurricanes – both are destructive rotating storms.
[4:3] - Air spiralling to the centre conserves angular momentum → **angular speed increases** towards the centre.
[4:4] - **Tornado Alley**: central US plains (**Mississippi plains / Great Plains**) where cold polar air meets warm, moist **Gulf of Mexico** air.
[4:5] - **India is least vulnerable to tornadoes** among major hazards (rare; pre-monsoon; Belda 1998).
[4:6] TABLE head: Feature | Tornado | Hurricane
  [4:6.0] Size, life | Small, minutes | Hundreds of km, days
  [4:6.1] Where | Land-based | Ocean-born
[4:7] - **Waterspout** = tornado over water; seen over the **Brahmaputra, Barpeta (Assam)**, 26 Aug 2026. An ordinary thunderstorm is a short-lived single-cell Cb; **supercells** spawn tornadoes.
[4:8] # Thunderstorms
[4:9] - Thundercloud (Cb, up to 15 km) needs **condensation, high temperature and humidity, orography and vertical winds – all four** (IAS 2002). **Thunder** = air expanding explosively after lightning heats it.
[4:10] - **Hailstorms**: instability, moisture, updrafts; **synoptic** causes = Western Disturbances and wind discontinuity lines.
[4:11] - **Damini App**: lightning alerts in 15 languages; 104 detection locations.
[4:12] # Pre-monsoon local storms of India
[4:13] TABLE head: Storm / shower | Season / region
  [4:13.0] Mango showers | End of summer (March–April); Kerala, coastal Karnataka, nearby TN; early ripening of mangoes
  [4:13.1] Blossom (cherry blossom) showers | Kerala and nearby; coffee flowering
  [4:13.2] Kaal Baisakhi / Nor'westers | West Bengal, Bihar, Odisha, Assam ("tea showers" in Assam); approach from NW
  [4:13.3] Aandhi (dust storm) | Summer; Uttar Pradesh, Punjab – dry plains of NW India
  [4:13.4] Loo | Hot dry wind of the northern plains
  [4:13.5] Cloudburst | Himalaya, June–August; about 100 mm/hour over 20–30 sq km
[4:14] - Hindi-medium key: mango showers = "rainfall in March–April in Bihar and Bengal" – strictly Kerala/Karnataka.
[4:15] - **Blizzard**: severe snowstorm, winds 35 mph (about 56 km/h)+, visibility a quarter mile or less for 3+ hours.

## SUB 5 | Structure, Rotation and Steering of Cyclones
[5:0] # Structure (asked 8 times)
[5:1] TABLE head: Part | Feature
  [5:1.0] Eye | Calm, clear, sinking air; lowest pressure; higher temperature (warm core); 20–50 km across
  [5:1.1] Eyewall | Ring of towering cumulonimbus; strongest winds, heaviest rain
  [5:1.2] Spiral rainbands | Curved bands of rain and wind – a mature cyclone shows a spiral pattern
[5:2] - Air moves **inward and rises**; rotation **anticlockwise (counter-clockwise) in NH, clockwise in SH** – spin from the **Coriolis effect**.
[5:3] - Approach of a cyclone = **falling** barometer.
[5:4] - Hurricanes usually **grow larger** poleward, and some are **under 150 km** across (Tracy 1974) – CDS 2010: only "spin from Coriolis" is right.
[5:5] - Steering: **trade winds** push tropical cyclones **west**; mature storms **recurve north-east** under the **westerlies**.
[5:6] - Threats: high winds, heavy rain, flooding, **storm surge** – abnormal rise of sea level, the **biggest killer**.
[5:7] ~~ Sources: PMF Physical Geography 06 p.4; NCERT Fundamentals ch.9; G.C. Leong ch.12 p.3; Environment Canada / NOAA JetStream (hurricane size); app notes 4:19 sub 2

## SUB 7 | Cyclones vs Anticyclones; Disturbances at a Glance
[7:0] # Cyclone vs anticyclone (asked 2 times)
[7:1] TABLE head: Basis | Cyclone | Anticyclone
  [7:1.0] Centre | Low pressure, surrounded by higher | High pressure, surrounded by lower
  [7:1.1] Air | Inward, rising | Outward, sinking (diverging)
  [7:1.2] Weather | Cloudy, stormy | Clear, dry, calm, settled
  [7:1.3] NH rotation | Anticlockwise | Clockwise
  [7:1.4] SH rotation | Clockwise | Anticlockwise
[7:2] - **Continually rising pressure = fine, settled weather** (anticyclone building).
[7:3] - **Heat dome**: a stalled warm high traps hot air (27 May 2026).
[7:4] # At a glance
[7:5] TABLE head: Disturbance | Nature | Clouds
  [7:5.0] Tropical cyclone | Large low over warm tropical oceans | Cumulonimbus, rainbands
  [7:5.1] Temperate cyclone | Large mid-latitude low from air-mass interaction, with fronts | Stratiform and cumuliform
  [7:5.2] Anticyclone | Large high, outward sinking air | Generally clear
  [7:5.3] Thunderstorm | Local violent convective storm with lightning, thunder | Cumulonimbus
  [7:5.4] Tornado | Very local rotating funnel | Cumulonimbus supercell
  [7:5.5] Cloudburst, squall | Very local intense rain / sudden gusty wind | –
[7:6] - **Fronts** belong to **temperate cyclones only**.

## SUB 8 | Temperate Cyclones and Western Disturbances
[8:0] # Temperate vs tropical (asked 1 time)
[8:1] TABLE head: Feature | Tropical | Temperate (extra-tropical)
  [8:1.0] Latitude | 5–30° | 35–65° (mid and high)
  [8:1.1] Origin | Only over warm seas | Land and sea
  [8:1.2] Fronts | None | Clear frontal system (warm, cold; occluded)
  [8:1.3] Size, winds | Smaller, very intense (100–500 km) | Larger area (300–2,000 km), gentler winds; long rain
  [8:1.4] Movement | East to west (trades) | West to east (westerlies)
[8:2] - APPSC G1: "Extra-tropical cyclones **do not have a clear frontal system**" is the **INCORRECT** statement.
[8:3] - **Western Disturbance**: extratropical low from the **Mediterranean**; reaches **NW India in winter** at the occluded stage; about **10–15 cm** rain for **rabi** crops, snow on the mountains.
