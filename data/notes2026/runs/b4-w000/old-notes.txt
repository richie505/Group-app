[0:0:9] - **Pluto** (dwarf planet since 2006) has the longest year of all (**247.9 years**); it was farther in than **Neptune** from **1979 to 1999**, when Neptune was the farthest.

[0:1:6] # The Moon
[0:1:7] - Mean distance **384,400 km**; gravity **1/6** of Earth's; rotation = revolution = **27 d 7 h 43 min** (same face); synodic (lunar) month **29.5 days**.
[0:1:8] - **Supermoon:** full moon when the Moon is **nearest the Earth (perigee)**, with Sun, Earth and Moon in line → bigger, brighter, higher tides. **Micromoon:** full moon at **apogee**, 6–7% smaller.
[0:1:9] - **Blue moon:** 2nd full moon in a calendar month; **blue micromoon** in 2026. Half moon: angle **90°**.
[0:1:10] TABLE head: Basis | Solar eclipse | Lunar eclipse
  [0:1:10.0] Position | Moon between Sun and Earth | Earth between Sun and Moon (Earth's shadow on the Moon)
  [0:1:10.1] Phase | New moon (Amavasya) | Full moon
  [0:1:10.2] Feature | Corona, diamond ring | Reddish "blood moon"
[0:1:11] - Longest total solar eclipse of the 21st century: **22 July 2009**; longest annular eclipse of the millennium: **15 January 2010**.
[0:1:12] - **Apollo 11** (20 July 1969): Armstrong and Aldrin walked, **Collins** orbited. **Blue Marble** photo: Apollo 17.

[0:1:14] - **Moon's core:** small, iron-rich and **solid** at the centre (solid inner core ~500 km across, Briaud et al., *Nature* 2023), unlike the Earth's **liquid outer core**.
[0:1:15] - **Sea of Tranquility** (Mare Tranquillitatis): a dark lava plain (mare) on the **Moon**; Apollo 11 landed there (1969).
[0:1:16] - **Diamond ring:** beads of sunlight shining through valleys on the Moon's rugged edge just **before and after totality**; UPSC 1996 key (PMF): seen **only along the peripheral regions of the totality trail**.
[0:1:17] - **TOI 700 e** (NASA TESS, January 2023): planet about **95% of Earth's size** in the **habitable zone** of the red dwarf TOI 700.
[0:1:18] - **Last man on the Moon:** **Eugene Cernan**, Apollo 17 (December 1972); 12 men in all walked on it (Artemis II, 2026, was a flyby, no landing).
[0:1:19] - **Escape velocity:** Earth **11.2 km/s** (Moon ~2.4 km/s); orbital velocity near Earth ~7.9 km/s.
[0:1:20] - **21 August 2017:** total solar eclipse across **North America** (the USA coast to coast – "Great American Eclipse").
[0:1:21] - **Totality only in a narrow path:** the **Moon's shadow (umbra) on the Earth is small**, so a total solar eclipse is seen only along a narrow **path of totality**.
[0:1:22] - **No air on the Moon:** there is **no atmosphere / no oxygen**, so nothing can burn there, sound does not travel and the sky is black.
## SUB 2 | Distance Units, Light Travel and the Universe
[0:2:0] # Units of astronomical distance
[0:2:1] TABLE head: Unit | Meaning | Value
  [0:2:1.0] Astronomical unit (AU) | Mean Earth–Sun distance | ~149.6 million km (149.598 × 10⁶ km)
  [0:2:1.1] Light year | Distance light travels in one year | 9.461 × 10¹² km (~9.46 trillion km)
  [0:2:1.2] Parsec | Largest of the three | 3.26 light years
[0:2:2] - **Light year = distance, not time** (asked 9 times); it is the unit for **distances of stars**. Sun–Earth = **1 AU**.
[0:2:3] - **Why light years:** the **speed of light is constant** (~3 × 10⁵ km/s) (UPSC 2021).
[0:2:4] - **"4.3 light years away"** (Alpha Centauri) = its light takes **4.3 years** to reach Earth.
[0:2:5] - **Sunlight reaches Earth in 8 min 19 s** (~**500 seconds**, "about 8 minutes").
[0:2:6] - **Order of size:** AU < light year < parsec. **Not** distance units: mile/fathom are not used for stars; "longsec" is not a unit at all.
[0:2:7] - **Cepheids** (stars that brighten and dim) are used to measure astronomical distances.
[0:2:8] # The universe
[0:2:9] - **Cosmology** = study of the origin, evolution and structure of the **universe**.
[0:2:10] - **Age of the universe: 13.8 billion years** (APPSC 2019). **Age of the Earth: ~4.54 billion years** (~4.6 billion for the solar system).
[0:2:11] - **Most abundant element** in the universe and in the Sun: **hydrogen**, then **helium** (Sun ~74% H, ~24% He).
[0:2:12] - **Milky Way** = **barred spiral galaxy**; the Sun lies in the **Orion Arm**, ~26,000 light years from the centre, and takes **~225–250 million years (~25 crore years)** to go once round the galactic centre (a "galactic year").
[0:2:13] - **Constellations:** **88** recognised by the **IAU** (founded 1919, Paris).
[0:2:14] - News terms **event horizon, singularity, string theory, Standard Model** belong to the observation and understanding of the **universe** (UPSC 2017).
[0:2:15] ~~ Sources: Rocket General Geography #1 p.5-6; PMF Physical Geography 01 p.8, p.19; NextGen Indian Geography - APPSC Exams 01 p.7; app notes 4:0 sub 0
[0:2:16] - **Cosmic dust:** tiny solid particles of matter spread throughout space (interplanetary and interstellar dust).
[0:2:17] - **Moonlight reaches Earth in ~1.3 seconds** (384,400 km ÷ 3 × 10⁵ km/s).
[0:2:18] - **Intergalactic space** is almost a perfect **vacuum** (only a few atoms of hot, thin gas per cubic metre).

## SUB 4 | Stars – Nearest Star, Colour, Brightness, Twinkling and Constellations
[0:4:0] # Nearest stars
[0:4:1] - **Nearest star to Earth: the Sun**; next: **Proxima Centauri** (4.2 ly), part of the **Alpha Centauri** system (4.37 ly).
[0:4:2] - **Sirius** (Dog Star, in Canis Major) = **brightest star** of the night sky; found by following the line of **Orion's belt** – as the Pole Star is found with Ursa Major.
[0:4:3] # Colour, brightness, twinkling
[0:4:4] - **Colour of a star = its surface temperature** (asked 3 times): **blue** (hottest, 50,000–28,000 K) → white → **yellow** (Sun, 6,000–4,900 K) → orange → **red** (coolest).
[0:4:5]   - Decreasing temperature: **Blue, White, Yellow, Orange, Red**.
[0:4:6] - **Apparent brightness** depends on a star's **size, temperature and distance from the Earth**.
[0:4:7] - **Magnitude:** lower number = brighter (logarithmic); **absolute magnitude** = brightness at **10 parsecs**.
[0:4:8] - **Twinkling:** starlight passing through the atmosphere is refracted by air of **fluctuating density / temperature** (changing **refractive index**); stars twinkle because they are **very distant** point sources; planets do not.
[0:4:9] - **In space** (no atmosphere): the **sky is black** and **stars do not twinkle**.
[0:4:10] - Hubble's first direct image of the surface of another star: **Betelgeuse** (red supergiant in Orion).
[0:4:11] # Constellations and the Pole Star
[0:4:12] - **Constellation** = a pattern of stars in the sky; its stars are **not equidistant** from Earth – they only look grouped.
[0:4:13] - **Saptarishi** (Big Dipper, part of **Ursa Major**) points to the **Pole Star** and so gives the direction of the pole.
[0:4:14] - **Polaris** (**Ursa Minor**) lies within 1° of the north celestial pole → always shows **north**; walking **east**, keep the Pole Star on your **left**. **Vega** will be pole star in ~**12,000 years**.
[0:4:15] - **Nebulae** (gas and dust clouds where stars form) are **not members of the solar system**; asteroids, comets and planets are.

## SUB 5 | Stars – Life Cycle, Black Holes and the Chandrasekhar Limit
[0:5:0] # Life cycle
[0:5:1] - **Nebula** → **protostar** → **main sequence**; then low/medium mass (Sun): **red giant** → planetary nebula → **white dwarf**; massive: **supergiant** → **supernova** → **neutron star** or **black hole**.
[0:5:2] - **Supernova** = the explosive death of a star (**a dying star**).
[0:5:3] TABLE head: Term | Meaning
  [0:5:3.0] Nebulae | Giant clouds of dust and gas
  [0:5:3.1] Cepheids | Stars that brighten and dim periodically
  [0:5:3.2] Pulsars | Spinning neutron stars formed when massive stars run out of fuel and collapse
  [0:5:3.3] Black hole | Remains of a collapsed massive star; no light escapes
[0:5:4] - UPSC 2023 pairs: Cepheids–"clouds of dust" ✗, Nebulae–"brighten and dim" ✗, Pulsars ✓ → **only one** correct.
[0:5:5] # Black holes
[0:5:6] - **Black hole:** gravity so strong (very **high density**) that **not even light escapes** → it **cannot be seen by a telescope** (A-R: both true, R explains A).
[0:5:7] - A black hole is a **collapsed star**, a celestial phenomenon of stars; Pulsar, Black Hole, Quasar are in space – **Brittle Star** is a sea animal (echinoderm).
[0:5:8] # Chandrasekhar
[0:5:9] - **S. Chandrasekhar** gave black-hole/white-dwarf theory (paper to the Royal Astronomical Society, London, **11 January 1935**; Nobel 1983).
[0:5:10] - **Chandrasekhar limit ≈ 1.44 solar masses**: stars below it end as **white dwarfs**; above it the core suffers **internal collapse** (neutron star or black hole).
[0:5:11] ~~ Sources: PMF Physical Geography 01 p.9-12; UPSC CSE 2023 pair question; app notes 4:0 sub 2-3
[0:5:12] - **Sun replaced by a black hole of equal mass:** the Earth's **orbit would stay nearly the same** – gravity depends only on mass and distance; the Earth would just go dark and cold.

## SUB 7 | The Sun – Composition, Layers and Activity
[0:7:0] - **Chief body of the solar system** and the **primary source of energy for the Earth**; **99.8%** of the solar system's mass; diameter **109 × Earth's**.
[0:7:1] - **Composition:** mostly **hydrogen** (~74%), then helium; energy from **nuclear fusion** of H into He in the core (**~15 million °C**).
[0:7:2] - **State:** the interior is **plasma (gas)** – the centre is in the **gaseous state only**, not liquid or solid.
[0:7:3] - **Light of the Sun is white** (all colours together); it looks yellow from Earth because of scattering.
[0:7:4] # Layers (centre outward)
[0:7:5] TABLE head: Layer | Key fact
  [0:7:5.0] Core | Fusion H → He; ~15 million °C
  [0:7:5.1] Photosphere | Bright visible outer surface emitting most radiation; ~5,500–6,000°C (5,778 K)
  [0:7:5.2] Chromosphere | Reddish lower atmosphere (~4,300°C), seen at eclipses
  [0:7:5.3] Corona | Outermost; visible only during a total solar eclipse; over 1 million °C
[0:7:6] # Solar activity
[0:7:7] TABLE head: Phenomenon | Fact
  [0:7:7.0] Sunspots | Cooler dark magnetic patches on the photosphere; the interval between two maxima (~11 years) = cycle of solar activity (solar cycle)
  [0:7:7.1] Solar flares | Bursts from magnetic reconnection; classes A, B, C, M, X (X strongest)
  [0:7:7.2] CMEs | Coronal plasma (not rock), 250–3,000 km/s, reach Earth in 1–3 days
  [0:7:7.3] Aurora | Borealis (north), Australis (south)

## SUB 1 | Shape and Size of the Earth – Geoid, Gravity, Dimensions
[1:1:0] # Shape
[1:1:1] - **Geoid** = "earth-shaped"; the Earth is an **oblate spheroid** – **flattened at the poles, bulging at the Equator**.
[1:1:2] - **Cause:** **centrifugal force of rotation**, maximum at the Equator (the faster-moving equatorial belt) – not the Sun's pull or stronger sunlight (CDS 2010: "only I").
[1:1:3] - **Equatorial bulge:** equatorial radius exceeds polar radius by **~21 km** (diameters by ~43 km) (CDS 2008, NDA 2016).
[1:1:4] - **Gravity and weight:** **g is greater at the poles** than at the Equator; **weight is least at the Equator, greatest at the poles** (poles are ~21 km nearer the centre + no centrifugal force). Moving to **higher latitude, weight increases** (IAS 2003 A-R: A false, R true).
[1:1:5] - Surface point nearest the Earth's centre: the **poles** (Arctic Ocean floor near the North Pole).
[1:1:6] - **Proofs of sphericity:** circular shadow of the Earth on the Moon in a **lunar eclipse**; ship's mast seen first; circumnavigation; satellite images.
[1:1:7] - **First to say the Earth is spherical:** **Pythagoras** (c. 6th century BC) proposed it; **Aristotle** (4th century BC, *On the Heavens*) gave the first proofs – Indian books and keys say **Aristotle** when Pythagoras is not offered (IAS 2001). Not Copernicus (heliocentric model), Ptolemy, Newton (polar flattening) or Columbus.
[1:1:8] - **Stars rise perpendicular to the horizon** for an observer on the **Equator** (celestial poles lie on the horizon there); at the poles stars circle parallel to the horizon (UPSC 2001, asked twice).
[1:1:9] # Dimensions
[1:1:10] TABLE head: Measure | Value
  [1:1:10.0] Equatorial diameter | 12,756 km (~12,800 km)
  [1:1:10.1] Polar diameter | 12,714 km (~43 km less)
  [1:1:10.2] Mean radius | 6,371 km
  [1:1:10.3] Equatorial circumference (length of the Equator) | ~40,075 km (~40,000 km)
  [1:1:10.4] Water : land | 71 : 29 – about two-thirds of the surface is water
[1:1:11] ~~ Sources: NextGen Rocket General Geography #1 p.4, p.7; PMF Physical Geography 01; Wikipedia "Equatorial bulge" (WGS-84: radii differ by 21.38 km); app notes 4:1 sub 0-1
[1:1:12] - **Level surface:** a surface **parallel to the mean spheroidal surface** of the Earth (perpendicular to the plumb line everywhere).
## SUB 2 | The Graticule – Latitude, Longitude, Great Circles
[1:2:0] # Latitude and longitude
[1:2:1] - **Latitude** = angular distance of a point **north or south of the Equator**, measured **from the centre of the Earth** (NDA 2011); 0°–90°.
[1:2:2] - **Parallels of latitude run east–west** (APPSC G1); **meridians run north–south**, pole to pole; longitude 0°–180° E or W.
[1:2:3] - Parallels at 1°: **179** (89 N + 89 S + Equator; the poles are points). Meridians: **360**.
[1:2:4] - 1° of latitude ≈ **111 km** everywhere; 1° of longitude: 111 km at the Equator → **55.5 km at 60°** → 0 at the poles.
[1:2:5] - **Antipode** of (x°N, y°E) = (x°S, (180 − y)°W): 20°N 80°E → **20°S 100°W**.
[1:2:6] # Great circles
[1:2:7] - A **great circle** divides the globe into **two equal halves** (its plane passes through the centre): the **Equator** (the only great-circle parallel) and every meridian with its opposite – **0° + 180°** form one (UPPSC 2000).
[1:2:8] - **60°N latitude is NOT a great circle** (all parallels except the Equator are small circles); 60°E longitude is half of a great circle.
[1:2:9] - NDA 2009 A-R: Equator is a great circle ✓; R (its plane divides the Earth into two equal halves) ✓ and explains A.
[1:2:10] # What varies with latitude (CDS 2017: all three true)
[1:2:11] - **Rainfall, temperature and vegetation** vary with latitude; the **difference between longest and shortest day increases with latitude**.
[1:2:12] - **Longitude does not affect insolation** (latitude, atmospheric transparency, distance from sea do).
[1:2:13] # Places by longitude / latitude
[1:2:14] - **West to east:** **Lisbon (9°W) → London (0°) → Frankfurt (8.7°E) → Beirut (35.5°E)** (MPPSC 2008).
[1:2:15] - **Nearest the Equator:** **Singapore (1°17'N)** < Jakarta (6°12'S) < Colombo (6°56'N) < Manila (14°35'N) (IAS 2008).
[1:2:16] ~~ Sources: NextGen Rocket General Geography #2; PMF Physical Geography 01; NCERT Class 6 "The Earth: Our Habitat" Ch. 2; app notes 4:1 sub 2
[1:2:17] - **Shapes on the globe:** a meridian of longitude = **semicircle**; a pole = **point (dot)**; the Equator (prime latitude) = **circle**; the axis = **straight line**.

=== NOTES 4:2 | Motions of the Earth – Rotation and Revolution; Effects (day and Night, Coriolis Force, Tides)
## SUB 0 | Rotation – Axis, Speed and Effects
[2:0:0] # Rotation basics
[2:0:1] - **Rotation** = the Earth spinning on its **axis** (line through both poles); **revolution** = moving round the Sun.
[2:0:2] - Direction **west to east** – **counter-clockwise** seen from above the North Pole; only **Venus and Uranus** spin the other way.
[2:0:3] - **Axis tilted 23½° (23°30') from the perpendicular to the orbital plane** ("from the ecliptic axis"; 66½° with the orbital plane) – so it is **not at 90°** to the orbit (NDA 2009: both statements ✓).
[2:0:4] - Angular speed **15° per hour** (1° in 4 min). Linear speed **~1,670 km/h at the Equator – the maximum velocity of rotation** – falling to **zero at the poles**.
[2:0:5] TABLE head: Day | Measured against | Length
  [2:0:5.0] Sidereal (one true rotation) | A distant star | 23 h 56 min 4.09 s
  [2:0:5.1] Solar | The Sun | 24 h (~4 min longer, as the Earth also moves along its orbit)
[2:0:6] - Not felt: motion is uniform and the atmosphere moves along with the Earth.
[2:0:7] - **Pole Star** seems fixed because it lies almost **in line with the Earth's axis of rotation**.
[2:0:8] # Effects of rotation (Strahler; NDA 2019: all three)
[2:0:9] - **Day and night** – the diurnal rhythm of daylight and air temperature.
[2:0:10] - **Coriolis deflection** – flow paths of air and water turned sideways.
[2:0:11] - **Movement of the tides** – a place is carried through the tidal bulges twice a day.
[2:0:12] - Also: time difference between longitudes, centrifugal bulge.
[2:0:13] - **Seasons are NOT caused by rotation** (they come from revolution + tilt); day and night are **not** caused by revolution (Jharkhand 2021: both statements false).
[2:0:14] - **No rotation** → no sunrise/sunset and no day-night cycle, but seasons would remain (CDS 2015: I and II).
[2:0:15] - **Faster rotation** → stronger centrifugal effect → **g decreases at the Equator, unchanged at the poles** (NDA 2018).
[2:0:16] # Evidence and pioneers
[2:0:17] - **Aryabhata** (5th century) first explained that the **rotation of the Earth** causes the daily rising and setting of the Sun (*Aryabhatiya*; also his *Aryasiddhanta*).
[2:0:18] - **Foucault pendulum**, Paris **1851**: the plane of swing slowly turns. Other proofs: Coriolis deflection, equatorial bulge, apparent east-to-west motion of the sky.
[2:0:19] # The axis is shifting (UPSC 2025)
[2:0:20] - Studies (ETH Zurich, *Nature Geoscience*/*PNAS* 2024) show the rotation axis drifting and the day lengthening; cause = **mass redistribution**: as **polar ice melts, water moves towards the Equator** (also groundwater loss) – day longer by **~1.33 ms per century** (2000–2020).
[2:0:21] - **Solar flares and CMEs** do bombard the upper atmosphere with energy (true) but **do not shift the axis** → "both II and III correct, only one (III) explains I".
[2:0:22] ~~ Sources: NextGen Rocket General Geography #2 p.11-13, p.18; NextGen Indian Geography - APPSC Exams 01 p.8-9; Strahler, Physical Geography (effects of rotation); app notes 4:2 sub 0-1
[2:0:23] - **Direction by the Pole Star:** it marks **north**, so to walk **east** keep the Pole Star on your **left**.
## SUB 1 | Coriolis and Centrifugal Effects
[2:1:0] - **Coriolis force** = an **apparent force** caused by the **Earth's rotation** (not revolution, tilt, pressure, temperature or magnetism); it shapes winds, ocean currents, cyclones and **large-scale weather patterns**.
[2:1:1] - **Ferrel's law:** moving air and water are deflected to the **right in the Northern Hemisphere** and to the **left in the Southern Hemisphere** – "left in NH" is the trap (APPSC 2022: I and III only).
[2:1:2] - Force = **2Ωv sin(latitude)**: **zero at the Equator**, **maximum at the poles**; grows with **latitude and wind speed**; linked to the **decreasing rotational velocity with increasing latitude** (CDS 2012: all three).
[2:1:3] - Cyclones rarely form within **5° of the Equator**; NH cyclones spin **anticlockwise**, SH clockwise.
[2:1:4] - AP angle: **Cyclone Montha (Oct 2025)** was steered north-northwest to the **Kakinada** coast.
[2:1:5] - **Centrifugal force** of rotation: maximum at the Equator, zero at the poles → **equatorial bulge, polar flattening (oblate spheroid)**; **weight of a body is maximum at the poles**, least at the Equator.
[2:1:6] ~~ Sources: NextGen Rocket General Geography #2 p.12-13; NextGen Indian Geography - APPSC Exams 03 p.12; app notes 4:2 sub 3
## SUB 2 | Tides
[2:2:0] - **Causes (UPSC 2015: a, b and c):** gravitational pull of the **Moon** (main) and the **Sun**, and the **centrifugal force** of the Earth–Moon system (bulge on the far side). Tide-generating force = gravitational attraction − centrifugal force.
[2:2:1] - Semi-diurnal tide: **two high and two low tides a day**; successive high tides **~12 h 25 min apart** (≈ **12 h 26 min**, NDA 2010).
[2:2:2] - The **lunar day is ~24 h 52 min**, so high tide comes **~52 min later each day**: **8:00 am → 8:52 am** (APPSC G1 2002, asked 3 times).
[2:2:3] TABLE head: Tide | Position | When
  [2:2:3.0] Spring (highest) | Sun, Earth, Moon in a straight line | New moon and full moon – twice a month
  [2:2:3.1] Neap (lowest range) | Sun and Moon at right angles | First and third quarter, ~7 days after spring tides – twice a month
[2:2:4] - Highest spring tides: full/new moon at an **equinox**; tides are larger when the Moon is at **perigee**. Ebb = falling tide, flow/flood = rising tide.
[2:2:5] ~~ Sources: NextGen Indian Geography - APPSC Exams 01 p.3-4, 03 p.9-11; app notes 4:2 sub 4
## SUB 3 | Day and Night, Day Length, Midnight Sun
[2:3:0] - **Day and night** are caused by the **rotation of the Earth on its axis** – not revolution, not tilt alone, not the Moon.
[2:3:1] - **Circle of illumination** = terminator = "**grey line**" (APPSC 2019); passes through both poles **only at the equinoxes**.
[2:3:2] - **Unequal day and night:** tilt + revolution. The difference is **least near the Equator** (~12 h day all year) and **increases away from it** to the poles (CDS 2014).
[2:3:3] - **Poles:** ~6 months of day + 6 months of night (**revolution with axial tilt**). **Midnight Sun** in the **Arctic and Antarctic regions** (beyond the polar circles) near the summer solstice; **Norway** = "Land of the Midnight Sun".
[2:3:4] - **Twilight** is shortest at the Equator (Sun rises almost vertically) and longest at high latitudes.
[2:3:5] - Equatorial seasons are marked by **wet and dry periods**, not by day length.
[2:3:6] ~~ Sources: NextGen Rocket General Geography #2 p.11-12, p.15; app notes 4:2 sub 2
## SUB 4 | Revolution, Seasons and Leap Year
[2:4:0] - Orbit slightly **elliptical**, Sun at one focus; period **~365¼ days** (365.25; sidereal year 365 d 6 h 9 min 9.5 s); mean speed **29.78 km/s**; the **Sun's gravity** is the centripetal force.
[2:4:1] - **Perihelion** early January (~147 million km; **3 Jan in 2026**), **aphelion** early July (~152 million km; **6 July in 2026**) – Earth moves faster at perihelion; the Earth–Sun distance is **not constant**.
[2:4:2] - **Seasons = revolution + 23½° tilt + parallelism of the axis**; Sun overhead at the Equator on **21 March and 23 September**, at Cancer on 21 June, at Capricorn on 22 December.
[2:4:3] - **Circular orbit instead of elliptical** → the varying distance would vanish, so the **difference between seasons would be reduced** (CDS 2011 key); the effect is small because tilt is the main cause.
[2:4:4] - Extra ¼ day a year → **leap year** of 366 days (29 February); a century year only if divisible by **400** (**2000 yes, 1900 no**).
[2:4:5] - Rotation gives day and night, time zones and Coriolis; **revolution** gives the year, seasons, solstices, equinoxes and perihelion/aphelion.
[2:4:6] ~~ Sources: NextGen Rocket General Geography #2 p.13-17, #3 p.19; app notes 4:2 sub 5

=== NOTES 4:3 | Concept of Time – Local and Standard Time, Gmt/utc, Indian Standard Time, Time Zones, International Date Line
## SUB 0 | Longitude and Local Time – Calculations
[3:0:0] # The rule (asked most: 23 PYQs)
[3:0:1] - Earth turns **360° in 24 h**, **west to east** → **15° = 1 hour**, **1° = 4 minutes**, **1' of arc = 4 seconds**.
[3:0:2] TABLE head: Longitude gap | Time gap
  [3:0:2.0] 1° | 4 minutes
  [3:0:2.1] 15° | 1 hour (60 min)
  [3:0:2.2] 90° | 6 hours
  [3:0:2.3] 180° | 12 hours (720 min)
  [3:0:2.4] 1' of arc | 4 seconds
[3:0:3] - **East is ahead (add), west is behind (subtract)**; moving westward local time **falls behind**. A place 30° west of Greenwich is **2 hours behind** GMT; the 15°E meridian is **1 hour fast**.
[3:0:4] - **Same side** of Greenwich → subtract the longitudes; **opposite sides** → add them (25°E and 35°W = 60° = **4 h**; 6°W and 9°E = 15° = 1 h).
[3:0:5] - **Same meridian = same local time**, however far apart north–south (NDA 2008: four places 500 km apart on one meridian all show **12:00 noon**).
[3:0:6] - Journey/message: arrival local time = departure + travel time ± longitude time. Flight 10:00 a.m., 3 h, 15° east → **2:00 p.m.**; telegram from Greenwich at noon, 12 min in transit, arrives 6:00 p.m. → 5 h 48 min = 348 min ÷ 4 = **87°E**.

## SUB 1 | Indian Standard Time and the Standard Meridian
[3:1:0] # IST (asked 21 times)
[3:1:1] TABLE head: Item | Fact
  [3:1:1.0] Standard meridian | 82°30'E (82.5°E) – India's central meridian
  [3:1:1.1] IST vs GMT | GMT + 5 h 30 min (82.5 × 4 = 330 min), i.e. 5½ h ahead; 0000 UTC = 5:30 a.m. IST
  [3:1:1.2] Reference town | Mirzapur, UP (clock tower); the meridian passes just east of Prayagraj (Allahabad – Naini) – the town given in the keys
  [3:1:1.3] City closest (MPPSC 2008) | Rewa (MP, ~81°18'E)
  [3:1:1.4] States crossed, W→E | Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Odisha, Andhra Pradesh – 5; not West Bengal (APPSC); last state = Andhra Pradesh (Kakinada district)
  [3:1:1.5] Meets the Tropic of Cancer | Koriya district, Chhattisgarh, on the Baghelkhand Plateau
  [3:1:1.6] Keeper | CSIR-National Physical Laboratory, New Delhi
[3:1:2] - **History:** India's time zones were set in **1884** – **Bombay Time** (GMT + 4 h 51 min) and **Calcutta Time** (GMT + 5 h 53 min 20 s); a single IST was adopted after Independence (**1947**; Calcutta Time lasted till 1948, Bombay Time till 1955). Calling Calcutta Time "4 h 51 min" and Bombay "5 h 30 min" is the false statement (APPSC flashcard).
[3:1:3] - **Basis of standard time:** a country's standard time is the local time of a **chosen longitude** (usually its central meridian), counted from Greenwich.
[3:1:4] - **City longitudes:** Delhi ~77°12'E – closest among the options is **Bengaluru** (~77°35'E); Hyderabad ~78°29'E, Nagpur ~79°05'E, Pune ~73°51'E (UPSC 2018).
[3:1:5] # India's span and the two-time-zone debate
[3:1:6] - India: **68°7'E (Guhar Moti, Kutch)** to **97°25'E** (Arunachal; easternmost between 97°25' and 97°30'E) → ~29° ≈ 116 min → **about 2 hours** between Arunachal and Gujarat (not 2½) (NDA 2017; Jharkhand 2023).
[3:1:7] - Latitudes **8°4'N** (mainland, Kanyakumari) to **37°6'N** (Indira Col); both extents **about 30°**. Older texts quote **8°4'28"N to 37°17'53"N**.
[3:1:8] - North-East loses daylight hours; proposals: advance IST by **30 min**, or a second zone ~1 h ahead. Assam tea gardens kept an informal **"Bagan time" (Chaibagaan time)**, 1 h ahead of IST – not official.
[3:1:9] - Andhra Pradesh: **12°41'N – 19°07'N**, ~77°E – 84°40'E.
[3:1:10] # IST in 2026 (current linkage)
[3:1:11] - **Jul 2026:** **White Rabbit** IST distribution network at the Regional Reference Standards Laboratory, **Jakkur (Bengaluru)** – sub-nanosecond sync over fibre, independent of GPS; the protocol was developed at **CERN** (not ISRO); "One Nation, One Time" (NPL–ISRO). It provides synchronised high-precision IST time-stamps for banking, payments, railways, airports, power grids and telecom, and guards against GPS spoofing.
[3:1:12] - **27 Aug 2026:** Consumer Affairs Ministry notified the **Legal Metrology (Indian Standard Time) Rules, 2026** – IST the single legal reference; in force **180 days** after Gazette publication (not immediately).

## SUB 4 | International Date Line
[3:4:0] - Imaginary **pole-to-pole** line roughly along **180°** in the **middle of the Pacific Ocean** (APPSC, State PSC); **12 hours** (720 min) from Greenwich because it is the 180° meridian (A and R true, R explains A).
[3:4:1] - Nearest strait: **Bering Strait** (UPSC 2008, asked twice) – between the **Chukchi** (Russia) and **Seward** (Alaska) peninsulas.
[3:4:2] - **Zigzags** where **land is in the way**: Chukotka, Aleutians, Kiribati, Fiji, Tonga, Samoa.
[3:4:3] TABLE head: Crossing | Date change
  [3:4:3.0] Eastward (Asia → America) | Subtract a day (gain a day)
  [3:4:3.1] Westward (America → Asia) | Add a day (lose a day)
[3:4:4] - **Big Diomede (Russia) and Little Diomede (USA)** are neighbours on opposite sides, a day apart.
[3:4:5] - **Samoa** moved to the Asian side in **2011**; **Kiribati's Line Islands** (UTC+14) are first to see a new day.
[3:4:6] - Old UPPCS 1992 key: a ship's least-time path is "along the meridian" – strictly the shortest route is a **great-circle** route (meridians are great circles).

## SUB 1 | Overhead Sun, Shadows and Insolation
[4:1:0] # Vertical rays and shadows
[4:1:1] - Noon Sun overhead only **between the Tropics**, **twice a year** (**zero-shadow days**); places north of 23½°N – **Delhi, Srinagar** – **never** get vertical rays; **Hyderabad, Mumbai, Kolkata** (south of the Tropic) do (APPSC G1 2002).
[4:1:2] - **Andhra Pradesh** (12°41'N–19°07'N) gets the overhead noon Sun **twice a year**.
[4:1:3] - **21 June:** Sun overhead at the **Tropic of Cancer** – no noon shadow there; in north India the noon **shadow is smallest on 21 June** (UPPCS 2006), longest in December. On 21 June the Sun **does not set at the Arctic Circle** (UPSC 2019).
[4:1:4] - At the **Equator** there is no noon shadow in **March and September**.
[4:1:5] - **Delhi is hotter at noon in summer** because in winter the Sun's rays fall **more slanting (oblique)** – not because the Earth is nearer the Sun (IAS 1993).
[4:1:6] TABLE head: Basis | Summer | Winter
  [4:1:6.0] Hemisphere | Tilted towards the Sun | Tilted away
  [4:1:6.1] Rays | More direct | More oblique – same energy spread over a larger area
  [4:1:6.2] Days | Longer | Shorter
  [4:1:6.3] Sun altitude / shadow | Higher / shorter | Lower / longer
[4:1:7] # Insolation and latitude zones
[4:1:8] - Factors: **angle of incidence**, **duration of day**, **transparency of the atmosphere**, land configuration – **not** the direction of ocean tides.
[4:1:9] - Insolation generally **falls from the tropics to the poles**; maximum over **cloudless subtropical deserts**, not the Equator.
[4:1:10] TABLE head: Zone | Seasonal feature
  [4:1:10.0] Low latitudes | Small temperature contrast; wet and dry seasons
  [4:1:10.1] Mid-latitudes | Four distinct seasons
  [4:1:10.2] High latitudes | Greatest seasonal differences – day length, insolation and annual temperature range all largest
[4:1:11] - **Seasonal variation increases with latitude** → "seasonal differences are maximum in **high latitudes**" (APPSC G1).
[4:1:12] - **Southern Hemisphere**: hottest summer month **January**; **Christmas in Australia falls in summer** (UKPSC 2010).
[4:1:13] # Related one-liners
[4:1:14] - **Albedo** = share of the Sun's radiation reflected by the Earth's surface; **weather changes occur in the troposphere**, not the stratosphere – the **stratosphere** holds the ozone layer (**Ozonosphere**).
[4:1:15] - India: hot season March–May, ~**50°C at Ganganagar (1935)**; **IMD's four seasons** – winter, pre-monsoon, **South-West monsoon**, **post-monsoon**; traditional calendar: **six ritus**.
[4:1:16] - AP: SW monsoon (June–September) brings about **two-thirds** of the rain; winter October–February; coldest place **Lambasingi** (~2°C).

## SUB 2 | Why Seasons Occur – Tilt, Revolution, Perihelion
[4:2:0] - **Seasons = revolution of the Earth + inclination (tilt) of its axis** (Jharkhand 2013, NDA 2014); **day-length variation** from season to season = **revolution on a tilted axis** (UPSC 2013, APPSC 2018) – not rotation, not the elliptical orbit, not oceans/mountains/altitude.
[4:2:1] - Axis tilted **23½° (23.5°) from the perpendicular** = **66½° to the orbital plane**.
[4:2:2] - **Parallelism**: the axis keeps pointing the same way (towards the **Pole Star**) through the year – because of **angular momentum** – so hemispheres tilt alternately towards (summer) and away (winter) → **opposite seasons**.
[4:2:3] - Full set: **axial inclination + parallelism + revolution + spherical shape** → changing **angle of insolation** and **day length**.
[4:2:4] - Tilt gives seasons, unequal days, the tropics and polar circles – **not day and night** (that is rotation, with the **circle of illumination** dividing day from night; rotation also gives the **Coriolis** deflection, to the **right in the NH**).
[4:2:5] - **What-if:** tilt raised from 23.5° to **30°** → **more intense seasonal contrasts**; axis **perpendicular** to the orbit → no seasons, equal day and night all year, Sun always over the Equator – but the North Pole would **not** lie always in darkness (NDA 2011).
[4:2:6] TABLE head: Point | Date | Distance | NH season
  [4:2:6.0] Perihelion (nearest the Sun) | ~3/4 January | ~147 million km | Winter
  [4:2:6.1] Aphelion (farthest) | ~4 July | ~152 million km | Summer
[4:2:7] - Earth is **nearest the Sun in northern winter**, so distance does not cause seasons (NDA 2014 trap).
[4:2:8] - Long term: **precession** (axial wobble) – **Vega** was pole star ~14,000 years ago and will be again in ~12,000 years; **Milankovitch cycles** drive long-term climate change. Melting ice moving mass away from the axis **slows rotation**.
