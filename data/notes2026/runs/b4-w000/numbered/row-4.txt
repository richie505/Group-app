## SUB 0 | Solstices, Equinoxes and Day Length
[0:0] # The four positions (asked most: 29 PYQs)
[0:1] TABLE head: Date | Event (NH) | Sun overhead at | NH | SH
  [0:1.0] 21 March (20/21) | Vernal (spring) equinox | Equator | Spring; day = night | Autumn begins
  [0:1.1] 21 June (20/21) | Summer solstice | Tropic of Cancer | Longest day | Shortest day
  [0:1.2] 23 September (22/23) | Autumnal equinox | Equator | Autumn; day = night | Spring begins
  [0:1.3] 22 December (21/22) | Winter solstice | Tropic of Capricorn | Shortest day | Longest day
[0:2] - Matches asked: winter solstice **22 Dec**, summer solstice **21 June**, vernal equinox **21 March**, autumnal equinox **23 Sept** (APPSC G1 2018, CDS 2014, WBCS 2020: all correct).
[0:3] - Order in a year: March equinox → June solstice → September equinox → December solstice.
[0:4] - **Equinox** = "equal night": Sun **overhead at the Equator**, **day = night everywhere** on Earth (SSC 2020); never at the Arctic Circle (NDA 2008 wrong pair). The tilt itself does not change – statement "day length changes because the tilt changes" is false (NDA 2009: I and II only).
[0:5] - "Vernal equinox": **20/21 March** in the NH; in the **Southern Hemisphere** the spring (vernal) equinox is **22/23 September** – so the APPSC flashcard key is "**both a and b**".
[0:6] - **Longest NH day:** **second half of June** (UPSC 2022, asked twice); **SH longest day: 22 December** (second half of December); **SH shortest day: 21 June**; NH shortest **22 December**.
[0:7] - On the shortest day in India (22 Dec) it is the **longest day in Australia** (also New Zealand) – Southern Hemisphere.
[0:8] - **Uttarayana** = the Sun's apparent **northward** movement, **22/23 Dec → 21 June**; Dakshinayana the reverse.
[0:9] - **21 June** = **International Day of Yoga** (UN, since 2015; 2026 theme "**Yoga for Healthy Ageing**").

  [0:11.0] Equator | 12 h | 12 h | 12 h
  [0:11.1] Tropic of Cancer | ~13½ h | 12 h | ~10½ h

[0:12] - **Equator: day = night all year** (12 h on every date, incl. the September equinox) – at the June solstice the 12-h day is at the Equator.
[0:13] - **North Pole lit from 21 March to 23 September** (about **six months** of day, then six of night); when the North Pole has continuous daylight the South Pole has continuous darkness.
[0:14] - 23 Sept → 21 Dec: NH days **become progressively shorter**.
[0:15] - **June day length grows northward**: **Srinagar > Delhi > Hyderabad > Chennai > Kanyakumari/Thiruvananthapuram** – Srinagar has the longest summer sunlight, Delhi the longest June day of Delhi/Bhopal/Hyderabad/Chennai, Thiruvananthapuram the shortest.
[0:16] - **Midnight sun** = Sun above the horizon at midnight, beyond the polar circles in summer.

## SUB 1 | Why Seasons Occur – Tilt, Revolution, Perihelion
[1:0] - **Seasons = revolution of the Earth + inclination (tilt) of its axis** (Jharkhand 2013, NDA 2014); **day-length variation** from season to season = **revolution on a tilted axis** (UPSC 2013, APPSC 2018) – not rotation, not the elliptical orbit, not oceans/mountains/altitude.
[1:1] - Axis tilted **23½° (23.5°) from the perpendicular** = **66½° to the orbital plane**.
[1:2] - **Parallelism**: the axis keeps pointing the same way (towards the **Pole Star**) through the year – because of **angular momentum** – so hemispheres tilt alternately towards (summer) and away (winter) → **opposite seasons**.
[1:3] - Full set: **axial inclination + parallelism + revolution + spherical shape** → changing **angle of insolation** and **day length**.
[1:4] - Tilt gives seasons, unequal days, the tropics and polar circles – **not day and night** (that is rotation, with the **circle of illumination** dividing day from night; rotation also gives the **Coriolis** deflection, to the **right in the NH**).
[1:5] - **What-if:** tilt raised from 23.5° to **30°** → **more intense seasonal contrasts**; axis **perpendicular** to the orbit → no seasons, equal day and night all year, Sun always over the Equator – but the North Pole would **not** lie always in darkness (NDA 2011).
[1:6] TABLE head: Point | Date | Distance | NH season
  [1:6.0] Perihelion (nearest the Sun) | ~3/4 January | ~147 million km | Winter
  [1:6.1] Aphelion (farthest) | ~4 July | ~152 million km | Summer
[1:7] - Earth is **nearest the Sun in northern winter**, so distance does not cause seasons (NDA 2014 trap).
[1:8] - Long term: **precession** (axial wobble) – **Vega** was pole star ~14,000 years ago and will be again in ~12,000 years; **Milankovitch cycles** drive long-term climate change. Melting ice moving mass away from the axis **slows rotation**.
[1:9] > Cause angle: "revolution on an inclined axis" is the answer; rotation and distance are the traps.

## SUB 2 | Overhead Sun, Shadows and Insolation
[2:0] # Vertical rays and shadows
[2:1] - Noon Sun overhead only **between the Tropics**, **twice a year** (**zero-shadow days**); places north of 23½°N – **Delhi, Srinagar** – **never** get vertical rays; **Hyderabad, Mumbai, Kolkata** (south of the Tropic) do (APPSC G1 2002).
[2:2] - **Andhra Pradesh** (12°41'N–19°07'N) gets the overhead noon Sun **twice a year**.
[2:3] - **21 June:** Sun overhead at the **Tropic of Cancer** – no noon shadow there; in north India the noon **shadow is smallest on 21 June** (UPPCS 2006), longest in December. On 21 June the Sun **does not set at the Arctic Circle** (UPSC 2019).
[2:4] - At the **Equator** there is no noon shadow in **March and September**.
[2:5] - **Delhi is hotter at noon in summer** because in winter the Sun's rays fall **more slanting (oblique)** – not because the Earth is nearer the Sun (IAS 1993).
[2:6] TABLE head: Basis | Summer | Winter
  [2:6.0] Hemisphere | Tilted towards the Sun | Tilted away
  [2:6.1] Rays | More direct | More oblique – same energy spread over a larger area
  [2:6.2] Days | Longer | Shorter
  [2:6.3] Sun altitude / shadow | Higher / shorter | Lower / longer
[2:7] # Insolation and latitude zones
[2:8] - Factors: **angle of incidence**, **duration of day**, **transparency of the atmosphere**, land configuration – **not** the direction of ocean tides.
[2:9] - Insolation generally **falls from the tropics to the poles**; maximum over **cloudless subtropical deserts**, not the Equator.
[2:10] TABLE head: Zone | Seasonal feature
  [2:10.0] Low latitudes | Small temperature contrast; wet and dry seasons
  [2:10.1] Mid-latitudes | Four distinct seasons
  [2:10.2] High latitudes | Greatest seasonal differences – day length, insolation and annual temperature range all largest
[2:11] - **Seasonal variation increases with latitude** → "seasonal differences are maximum in **high latitudes**" (APPSC G1).
[2:12] - **Southern Hemisphere**: hottest summer month **January**; **Christmas in Australia falls in summer** (UKPSC 2010).
[2:13] # Related one-liners
[2:14] - **Albedo** = share of the Sun's radiation reflected by the Earth's surface; **weather changes occur in the troposphere**, not the stratosphere – the **stratosphere** holds the ozone layer (**Ozonosphere**).
[2:15] - India: hot season March–May, ~**50°C at Ganganagar (1935)**; **IMD's four seasons** – winter, pre-monsoon, **South-West monsoon**, **post-monsoon**; traditional calendar: **six ritus**.
[2:16] - AP: SW monsoon (June–September) brings about **two-thirds** of the rain; winter October–February; coldest place **Lambasingi** (~2°C).
