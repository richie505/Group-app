=== Informal Sector and Quality of Employment
| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Formal / organised sector | Public sector + private establishments with **10 or more hired workers**; social security, higher wages |
| Informal / unorganised sector (enterprise) | Unincorporated private enterprises of individuals / households with **fewer than 10 workers** |
| Informal employment (worker) | Workers without social security, paid leave or dismissal notice – in **any** sector |
- **Unemployment and underemployment are concentrated in the unorganised sector**; it runs on **cash (currency transactions)** – the "informal, cash-intensive parts of the economy".
- **NCEUS** (National Commission for Enterprises in the Unorganised Sector), set up **2004**, chaired by **Arjun Sengupta**.
- Nearly **82%** of the workforce is in the informal sector and nearly **90%** is informally employed (India Employment Report 2024); 2011-12: only about **6%** in the formal sector (NCERT).
- **AP:** informal share **62.3% rural, 45.6% urban**; overall about **55%**.
- **Casual workers (UPSC 2021):** EPF coverage is **not** for all casual workers (depends on establishment size and wage ceiling); they are entitled to regular working hours and overtime (minimum wage rules); the Government may by notification require an establishment to **pay wages only by cheque or into the bank account** (Payment of Wages Amendment 2017) → **2 and 3 only**.
- **India's wage policy** (minimum wages, DA) is linked to the **cost of living**.
> APPSC Group-II 2017: NCEUS = Arjun Sengupta; WBCS: unemployment concentrated in the unorganised sector.

=== Jobless Growth and Employment Elasticity
- **Jobless growth:** a widening gap between GDP growth and employment growth – more goods and services produced without proportionate jobs; NCERT dates the "disheartening development" to the **late 1990s**; employment growth was near zero again in **2004-05 to 2009-10** (the decade **2001-2010** in the AEE 2016 key).
- Term coined by **Nick Perna** (early 1990s), not Arthur Lewis.
- **Main reason after 1991:** reforms were **mainly confined to the formal sector** while most jobs are informal; growth led by **capital-intensive** IT and finance, not **labour-intensive manufacturing** ("**missing middle**").
- **Post-1991 (UPSC 2020):** rural **non-farm** growth rose and **rural employment growth fell**; productivity rose in rural and urban areas; the rural share of the workforce fell → **III and IV only**.
- **Employment elasticity** = % change in employment ÷ % change in output; **low** = output grows with little rise in jobs.
| Sector (1999-2000 to 2009-10) | Employment elasticity |
|---|---|
| Construction | 1.13 |
| Finance and real estate | 0.66 |
| Manufacturing | 0.09 |
| **Agriculture (mixed farming)** | **0.04 – very low** |
- About **12 million** enter the job market every year; direct factory employment fell 61% → 47% (2011-2023) as **contract workers rose to 42%** (13 Mar 2026).

=== Female Labour Force Participation
| PLFS round | Female LFPR (15+, usual status) |
|---|---|
| **2017-18** | **23.3%** |
| 2020-21 | 32.5% |
| **2022-23** | **37.0%** |
| 2023-24 | **41.7%** |
| 2025 | about 40% (rural 45.9%, urban **27.7%**) |
- **Earlier decline:** female LFPR fell sharply from 2004-05 to 2017-18; the usual explanation – **rising family income (income effect) and more girls in education** (A and R both true, R explains A in UPPSC 2019; some keys say R does not fully explain A).
- **Economic Survey 2017-18:** most women's indicators improved, but **female labour force participation and the fertility rate declined**.
- **Rural women = 80%** of the rise in female workers (2017-18 to 2023-24) – subsistence farming, unpaid family work; the **rural-urban gap is widening** (24 Aug 2026).
- **U-shaped hypothesis:** female LFPR falls with early income growth and rises later with education and service jobs.
- **Feminisation of agriculture:** basic reason = **rural-to-urban migration of men**.
- **Barriers:** social norms, unpaid care work, unsafe transport (not the expansion of service jobs).
- PLFS 2025: LFPR about **59%**, WPR about **57%**, UR about **3%**.
- **AP (PLFS 2023-24):** female LFPR all ages **35.8%** (India **31.7%**), 15+ **44.8%** (India 41.7%); overall LFPR 47.5% (rural 50.1, urban 41.3).
~~ Sources: AP Socio-Economic Survey 2024-25 p.287-288; OnlyIAS NCERT Economy 04 p.84; Ramesh Singh Indian Economy 10th ed. ch.20 p.61-62; app notes 3:35 sub 5, 8

=== Quarterly Employment Survey and Organised-Sector Jobs
- **QES** by the **Labour Bureau**: establishments with **10+ workers** in **9 sectors** – manufacturing, construction, trade, transport, accommodation and restaurants, IT/BPO, education, health, financial services (not agriculture).
- **Manufacturing** is the largest QES employer (38.5%, Jan-Mar 2022), then **education** (21.7%).
- **28th QES (2015, eight sectors):** only **1.35 lakh** jobs added; **IT/BPO led with about 76,000**, then textiles including apparel (72,000) and metals (37,000). Textiles led only the Oct-Dec 2015 quarter.
- **Organised-sector employment growth declined between 1994 and 2007** (Economic Survey 2009-10); reforms reduced formal-sector employment (NCERT).

=== Gig Economy, Labour Codes and e-Shram
| Item | Fact |
|---|---|
| **Gig economy** | Engagement of independent contractors / freelancers instead of permanent workers |
| NITI Aayog "India's Booming Gig and Platform Economy" (June 2022) | **77 lakh** gig workers (2020-21) |
| Projection | **2.35 crore by 2029-30** (about 6.7% of workforce) |
| Code on Social Security, 2020 | Defines gig and platform workers; aggregators pay **1-2% of annual turnover** to a fund |
| **Online Labour Index** (**Oxford Internet Institute**) | **India** is the largest supplier of online freelance labour, mainly **software and tech** work |
- FY25: gig workforce grew 55% to about **120 lakh**; **PLFS does not yet capture gig workers** separately; most are outside e-Shram.
- **4 Labour Codes** consolidate **29 central labour laws**, in force from **21 Nov 2025**: Code on Wages 2019, Code on Social Security 2020, Industrial Relations Code 2020, OSH and Working Conditions Code 2020.
- **e-Shram** portal (**26 Aug 2021**): national database of **unorganised workers**; each gets a **12-digit Universal Account Number**; about **31.89 crore** registrations (Aug 2026).
