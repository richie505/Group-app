## SUB 1 | Red Data Book, IUCN and Biodiversity Reports
[86:1:0] # Red Data Book / Red List
[86:1:1] - Published by **IUCN**; lists **threatened (endangered) plants and animals** in danger of extinction – **not** endemic species of hotspots, **not** protected sites.
[86:1:2] - IUCN's endangered-species list = **Red List**; Brown, White, Black, Green, Yellow lists are distractors. "Red" symbolises danger.
[86:1:3] - Red List started **1964**; first Red Data Book volumes issued **1966** by IUCN's Species Survival Commission; nickname "**Barometer of Life**"; measures **extinction risk**.
[86:1:4] TABLE head: Page colour | Meaning
  [86:1:4.0] Red / pink | Endangered / critically endangered
  [86:1:4.1] Green | Formerly threatened, now recovered
[86:1:5] - **IUCN Green Status (2021)** measures species **recovery**.
[86:1:6] - Red List **2026-1 (July 2026)**: **175,909** assessed; **49,505 (~28%)** threatened. India's **Red List Roadmap**: **70,000** species by **2027**, led by the **NBA**; 7,076 Indian animal species in Red List categories.
[86:1:7] # IUCN
[86:1:8] - **International Union for Conservation of Nature** (and Natural Resources); founded **1948**; HQ **Gland, Switzerland** (older books: Morges); a union of governments and NGOs – **NOT a UN agency**.
[86:1:9] - **Species Survival Commission** → **Invasive Species Specialist Group (ISSG)**, which manages the **Global Invasive Species Database** and the "100 of the World's Worst" list.
[86:1:10] - World Conservation Congress 2025: **Abu Dhabi**, "Our World, Our Future". **Phoenix Species Project** (Aug 2026): **$200 million**, Re:wild and Bezos Earth Fund, to recover **100** most threatened species.
[86:1:11] # Report → publisher
[86:1:12] TABLE head: Report / database | Publisher
  [86:1:12.0] Red List / Red Data Book | IUCN
  [86:1:12.1] Global Invasive Species Database | IUCN SSC ISSG
  [86:1:12.2] Living Planet Report (biennial, since 1998) / Living Planet Index (vertebrate populations since 1970) | WWF with Zoological Society of London
  [86:1:12.3] Global Assessment on Biodiversity (2019): ~25% of species threatened, up to 1 million face extinction | IPBES (not WWF)
[86:1:13] - **Living Planet Report 2022:** global biodiversity and planet health; average **69% decline** in monitored wildlife populations **since 1970** (LPR 2024: 73%).

## SUB 2 | Causes of Biodiversity Loss – Habitat Destruction First
[86:2:0] # The most-asked fact
[86:2:1] - **Greatest / most important / main cause:** **habitat destruction (loss of natural habitats and vegetation)** – NextGen: "the primary and major reason for biodiversity loss"; IPBES also ranks it first. Not climate change, invasives, over-hunting or pollution alone.
[86:2:2] - **Deforestation → biodiversity loss:** cutting and burning forests **destroys natural habitats, driving species to extinction and disrupting ecosystems**.
[86:2:3] - **Primary driver of deforestation and habitat loss:** **agricultural expansion and urban development** (also land clearance for agriculture, mines, dams, harbours, industries, settlements); in the world the major cause of deforestation is **expansion of agriculture** (not paper industry, firewood or fires).
[86:2:4] - Main cause overall = **human influence**: habitat alteration and loss, exotic species, over-exploitation, pollution, GMOs; **over-consumption of resources** destroys habitats, raises pollution and drives climate change.
[86:2:5] - In India: habitat loss (urbanisation, infrastructure, mining, farming) comes first, then climate change, invasives, pollution, over-exploitation.
[86:2:6] - Loss of biodiversity is a major problem resulting from **modern agriculture** (monoculture, agro-chemicals).
[86:2:7] # Evil Quartet vs HIPPO
[86:2:8] TABLE head: Evil Quartet (NCERT / NextGen) | HIPPO (popularised by E. O. Wilson)
  [86:2:8.0] Habitat loss and fragmentation | Habitat destruction
  [86:2:8.1] Over-exploitation (Steller's sea cow, passenger pigeon) | Invasive species
  [86:2:8.2] Invasion of alien species (Nile perch → 200+ cichlids lost in Lake Victoria) | Pollution
  [86:2:8.3] Co-extinctions | human Population
  [86:2:8.4] — | Over-harvesting
[86:2:9] - **HIPPO** was popularised by **E. O. Wilson**. **Co-extinction** is only in the Evil Quartet; **pollution** and **population** only in HIPPO. Newer lists add **climate change** and **disease**.
[86:2:10] # Fragmentation and edge effect
[86:2:11] TABLE head: Term | Meaning
  [86:2:11.0] Fragmentation | Large habitats broken into small patches; hits migratory animals and wide-ranging mammals and birds hardest
  [86:2:11.1] Edge effect | Patch boundaries get more light, wind, heat, predators and weeds; as fragments shrink, interior species decline
[86:2:12] - Tropical rainforest cover fell from **14% to 6%** of the land surface; Amazon cleared for soybean and cattle.
[86:2:13] # Disturbs vs restores ecological balance
[86:2:14] TABLE head: Disturbs (a cause of loss) | Restores (NOT a cause)
  [86:2:14.0] Roads, urbanisation, extension of agriculture, clear-felling, jhum (shifting) cultivation, mining, quarrying, encroachment, exploitation of forest produce | Afforestation of degraded land, sacred groves, protected areas, biosphere reserves, wildlife corridors
  [86:2:14.1] Draining wetlands, mangroves to shrimp farms | Removal of invasives with native restocking
[86:2:15] > Exam angle: "greatest / main / most important cause" (habitat destruction ×8), "all of the above", primary driver (agriculture + urban development), and NOT-a-cause pickers (afforestation, sacred groves, biosphere reserves).

## SUB 3 | Invasive Alien Species
[86:3:0] - **Invasive alien species:** non-native organisms introduced outside their natural range that "**arrive, survive and thrive**", outcompete natives and cause economic, environmental and health harm; exotic plants that **crowd out native plants** = **invasive plants**.
[86:3:1] - Three tests: **non-native origin, uncontrolled spread, measurable harm** (NOT commercial usefulness). Defined in India under the **WPA 1972** (Amendment 2022, **Section 62A**: Centre regulates/prohibits IAS; new **Schedule IV** = CITES-appendix specimens, NOT invasives).
[86:3:2] - Invasives contributed to nearly **40%** of known animal extinctions since the **17th century**; threaten **1 in 10** Red List species; damage per species is higher in the **Global South**.
[86:3:3] - Invasive plants cut **medicinal plants, fruits and fodder grass** used by indigenous communities, and degrade the **herbivore forage on which tigers depend** – both harms are real.

## SUB 4 | Threats in Statement Questions and Species Declines
[86:4:0] # Accepted threats vs distractors
[86:4:1] TABLE head: Threat (correct) | NOT a threat (distractor)
  [86:4:1.0] Global warming, habitat fragmentation, invasion of alien species | Promotion of vegetarianism
  [86:4:1.1] Loss of habitat, loss of genetic variation | Loss of appetite
  [86:4:1.2] Habitat pollution, over-exploitation, illegal wildlife trade, forest fires | Creation of biosphere reserves, expansion of protected areas
[86:4:2] - UPSC 2012 / RAS 2016: threats = **1, 2 and 3** (global warming, fragmentation, alien species), not vegetarianism.
[86:4:3] - Direct threats (NextGen): habitat destruction, exotic species, diseases, pollution, **genetic assimilation**.
[86:4:4] - **Extinction-prone traits:** large body size, small population, low reproductive rate, **narrow (specialised) niche**, **lack of genetic variability**, endemism; a **broad niche** makes a species adaptable – NOT a cause.
[86:4:5] - When a species becomes extinct, **its obligately associated species also go extinct** (co-extinction) – so "associated species do not become extinct" is false.

  [86:4:7.3] Wildlife in general | Loss of habitat, illegal trade, forest fires; population growth acts mainly indirectly

## SUB 5 | Extinction – Mass Extinctions and Co-extinction
[86:5:0] TABLE head: Term | Meaning
  [86:5:0.0] Extinction | Disappearance of all members of a species (death of the last individual), globally
  [86:5:0.1] Extirpation | Local extinction only
  [86:5:0.2] Co-extinction | Loss of one species drags down its obligate partners (plant–pollinator, host–parasite)
  [86:5:0.3] Background extinction | Slow natural loss at a low steady rate
  [86:5:0.4] Mass extinction | High percentage of species lost in a short geological period
  [86:5:0.5] Sixth extinction | Present, human-driven wave – over-exploitation of resources, habitat loss and fragmentation, ecosystem destruction, pollution, climate change
[86:5:1] - **Five** mass extinctions so far; the **fifth** (end-Cretaceous, ~**66 million years ago**) ended the dinosaurs – **asteroid-impact theory of Luis Alvarez** (1980, with Walter Alvarez).
[86:5:2] - Sixth extinction: rates **100-1,000 times** pre-human rates (NextGen); IPBES: tens to hundreds of times background. It is not about GM crops, monoculture or a feared meteorite.
[86:5:3] - **Dodo** (Mauritius): classic **co-extinction** case – the tree whose seeds it dispersed (*Calvaria*/tambalacoque) failed to reproduce.
[86:5:4] - IUCN Red List (2014): **784** extinctions in 500 years; 128 bird species lost in 500 years. Extinct: **passenger pigeon, Steller's sea cow** (over-exploitation), Bali, Javan and Caspian tigers.
[86:5:5] - **Extinct in the Wild:** Hawaiian crow, Père David's deer (only in captivity).
[86:5:6] - **India:** **cheetah** declared extinct **1952** – the only large mammal lost since Independence; ZSI lists **cheetah, pink-headed duck, mountain quail** as extinct from India (not the passenger pigeon).
[86:5:7] > Exam angle: definition (extinction, extirpation), sixth extinction (UPSC 2018 ×2), dodo (UPSC 1998 ×2), Alvarez (SSC 2019).
[86:5:8] ~~ Sources: NextGen Environment APPSC 04 p.10-11; NextGen Environment APPSC 05 p.4-5; NextGen Environment APPSC 06 p.8; PMF Species & PAN Part 1 p.76 (dodo EX); app notes 5:86 sub 3; Alvarez, dodo tree: general knowledge, not checked
[86:5:9] - **Extinct vs alive:** dodo, **West African black rhino** (declared extinct 2011) and **Chinese river dolphin / baiji** (functionally extinct 2006) are gone; the **red panda** is Endangered but still alive (Eastern Himalaya).
## SUB 6 | IUCN Red List Categories and Criteria
[86:6:0] - **Nine** categories, highest to lowest risk: **EX → EW → CR → EN → VU → NT → LC**, plus **DD** and **NE**.
[86:6:1] TABLE head: Code | Full form | Meaning
  [86:6:1.0] EX | Extinct | No reasonable doubt that the last individual has died, after exhaustive surveys – no fixed number of years
  [86:6:1.1] EW | Extinct in the Wild | Survives only in cultivation, captivity or as a naturalised population well outside its past range
  [86:6:1.2] CR | Critically Endangered | Extremely high risk of extinction in the wild (not "critically extinct")
  [86:6:1.3] EN | Endangered | Very high risk; "species in danger of extinction"; numbers reduced to a critical level
  [86:6:1.4] VU | Vulnerable | High risk; continuous decline from over-exploitation or habitat destruction
  [86:6:1.5] NT | Near Threatened (not "Not Threatened") | Close to qualifying for a threatened category
  [86:6:1.6] LC | Least Concern | Widespread and abundant
  [86:6:1.7] DD | Data Deficient | Inadequate information
  [86:6:1.8] NE | Not Evaluated | Not yet assessed
[86:6:2] - **Threatened = CR + EN + VU.** "Rare" (older Indian usage) = not endangered or vulnerable now but at risk.
[86:6:3] - **50-year rule:** older books say a species is extinct if **not seen in the wild for 50 years** (a 1980s suggestion); IUCN today uses "no reasonable doubt" instead.
[86:6:4] - **Extirpation** = local extinction: a species gone **from a specific area but surviving elsewhere** (e.g. tigers extirpated from Kawal and Sri Venkateswara NP).
[86:6:5] # Criteria A-E
[86:6:6] TABLE head: Criterion | Basis
  [86:6:6.0] A | Declining population (past, present, projected)
  [86:6:6.1] B | Geographic range (size, fragmentation, decline)
  [86:6:6.2] C | Small population size and decline
  [86:6:6.3] D | Very small or restricted population
  [86:6:6.4] E | Quantitative analysis of extinction risk (PVA)
[86:6:7] - Economic value in trade is NOT a criterion.
[86:6:8] > Exam angle: CR full form (APPSC Group 2), "species in danger of extinction" = endangered, extirpation, 50-year rule.

## SUB 9 | Climate Change and Consequences of Loss
[86:9:0] - Warming causes **coral bleaching**, ocean acidification, shifts in ecosystem boundaries and **disrupted migration**; rising temperatures **shift species ranges** and raise extinction risk in hotspots (Jun 2026); a warming Arctic is opening to **alien plants** (Feb 2026).
[86:9:1] - Loss disrupts species interactions, **pollination**, **nutrient cycling**, water and soil services → lower **food security** for resource-dependent communities; once lost, biodiversity cannot be recreated.
[86:9:2] - **Zoonotic spillover** rises with habitat disruption and wildlife trade → **One Health** approach (humans, animals, ecosystems).

## SUB 0 | Biosphere Reserves and the MAB Programme
[87:0:0] # Concept (most asked)
[87:0:1] - **Biosphere Reserve (BR):** large terrestrial/coastal/marine area recognised under **UNESCO's Man and the Biosphere (MAB) Programme** (launched **1971**; not IUCN); UNESCO calls BRs "learning places for sustainable development".
[87:0:2] - **Purpose:** conserve the **genetic diversity** of the whole landscape **together with the traditional life of local/tribal people** – so the "most important strategy for conservation of biodiversity together with traditional human life" is the **biosphere reserve**, not an NP, sanctuary or botanical garden.
[87:0:3] - Designation **does not change legal status or ownership**; a BR may contain one or more NPs/sanctuaries. **IN-MAB Committee (MoEFCC)** recommends sites; State prepares the management plan; funding **60:40** (**90:10** for NE and three Himalayan States). **Seville Strategy (1995)** added social, cultural and economic needs.
[87:0:4] - Not a protected-area category under the WPA 1972 (those are NP, sanctuary, conservation reserve, community reserve).

## SUB 0 | Project Tiger – Launch, First Reserves and Tiger Reserves
[89:0:0] # Launch
[89:0:1] - **Project Tiger:** launched **1973** (Centrally Sponsored Scheme) on the advice of a **task force set up by PM Indira Gandhi**; aim = **save the tiger from extinction** by keeping viable populations in their natural habitats (core-buffer strategy).
[89:0:2] - **First reserve:** **Jim Corbett NP (Uttarakhand)**, under Project Tiger from **1 April 1973**; Corbett = India's first NP (1936, Hailey NP); not Bandhavgarh, Gir or Sundarbans.
[89:0:3] - **First nine reserves (1973):** Corbett, Kanha, Manas, Palamau, Similipal, Melghat, Bandipur, **Ranthambhore** and Sundarbans – so Ranthambhore became a tiger reserve in **1973** (1955 and 1980 are distractors; Ranthambhore NP itself dates from 1980).
[89:0:4] - **Launch order of projects:** Project Tiger 1973 → Crocodile project 1975 → Project Elephant 1992 → Indian Rhino Vision 2020 (2005) → Project Snow Leopard 2009 → Project Lion and Project Dolphin 2020 → Project Cheetah 2022.
[89:0:5] - **Five new national projects (MoEFCC, 2026):** dolphin, Asian elephant, **gharial**, **GIB**, **Olive Ridley** (not snow leopard).
[89:0:6] - **Tiger = National Animal of India**; the special projects in India's wildlife programme are for **tigers and elephants** (Project Tiger 1973, Project Elephant 1992).

## SUB 1 | Vultures and Diclofenac
[89:1:0] # Why vultures vanished
[89:1:1] - **Cause:** **diclofenac**, a **painkiller (NSAID)** given to **cattle**; vultures eating the carcasses suffer **kidney failure (visceral gout)** – not viral/bacterial infection, food scarcity or nesting loss; confirmed by **Peregrine Fund** research (May 2003).
[89:1:2] - **Decline first noticed:** **Keoladeo NP, Bharatpur (1998)**.
[89:1:3] - **Ban:** veterinary diclofenac banned **May 2006**; **aceclofenac and ketoprofen** banned for animals (2023).
[89:1:4] - **Meloxicam** = safe alternative; it is a **non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID)** (prednisolone, betamethasone, hydrocortisone are steroids; atropine, alum, ranitidine are not anti-inflammatories).
[89:1:5] - **9** vulture species in India; **4 resident ones CR**: white-rumped (white-backed), slender-billed, long-billed (Indian), red-headed.
[89:1:6] # Conservation
[89:1:7] - **Vulture Recovery Plan / Action Plan 2006**; **Action Plan for Vulture Conservation 2020-25**: **State-level vulture committees**, more breeding centres, Vulture Safe Zones.
[89:1:8] - **Tamil Nadu** was the first to set up a **State-Level Committee for Vulture Conservation** (GO 19 Oct 2022).
[89:1:9] - **Jatayu Conservation Breeding Centre, Pinjore (Haryana)** – first (2001); **Asia's first Gyps Vulture Reintroduction Programme (2016): Pinjore** – two Himalayan griffons released.
[89:1:10] - **SAVE** = **Saving Asia's Vultures from Extinction** (consortium; 2011 Nepal-India programme).
[89:1:11] - **Coringa WLS (AP)** hosts the CR **white-backed and long-billed vultures**.
[89:1:12] - **Aug 2026:** world's first release of captive-bred **slender-billed vultures** (5 birds, BNHS centre, **Assam**).

## SUB 2 | Ganges River Dolphin and Project Dolphin
[89:2:0] - **National Aquatic Animal:** **Ganges River Dolphin** (*Platanista gangetica*), declared **5 October 2009** (not crocodile, Olive Ridley, gharial, coral or jellyfish); **5 October = National Dolphin Day**.
[89:2:1] - **Dolphins are mammals** (cetaceans), not fish, amphibians or reptiles.
[89:2:2] - **Status:** IUCN **Endangered** (not Vulnerable); WPA **Schedule I**; CITES App. I; found in **India, Nepal, Bangladesh** (Ganga-Brahmaputra-Meghna, Karnaphuli-Sangu).
[89:2:3] - **Threats:** **dams and barrages**, pollution incl. **agricultural chemicals**, **fishing-net entanglement**, poaching for oil – **NOT** "more crocodiles".
[89:2:4] - **Places:** **Vikramshila Gangetic Dolphin Sanctuary – Bhagalpur, Bihar**; **National Dolphin Research Centre – Patna** (India's and Asia's first); **National Chambal Sanctuary** (MP-Rajasthan-UP) = Gangetic dolphin, **mugger** and **gharial**.

## SUB 3 | Great Indian Bustard, CMS and Other Species
[89:3:0] # Great Indian Bustard
[89:3:1] - *Ardeotis nigriceps* (Godawan), Rajasthan's state bird; IUCN **Critically Endangered**; main range **Thar (Rajasthan)**; in AP at **Rollapadu**.
[89:3:2] - **Threat:** collision with **overhead power lines** in solar and wind zones.
[89:3:3] - **M.K. Ranjitsinh v. Union of India (March 2024):** modified (did not uphold unchanged) the 2021 blanket undergrounding order; recognised a **right against adverse effects of climate change** under **Arts 14 and 21**.
[89:3:4] - Breeding at **Sam and Ramdevra, Jaisalmer**.
[89:3:5] # CMS (Bonn Convention)
[89:3:6] - **COP13: Gandhinagar, Feb 2020**; mascot **Gibi = GIB** (not Amur falcon); **Asian elephant, GIB and Bengal florican** added to **Appendix I** (endangered migratory species) – not the Bengal tiger.
[89:3:7] - **COP14: Samarkand, Feb 2024**. Amur falcon stopover: **Doyang reservoir**; **Pangti** = "Falcon Capital of the World" (Nagaland).
[89:3:8] # Other species
