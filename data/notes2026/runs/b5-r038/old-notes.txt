[38:0:1] # GPS basics
[38:0:2] - **GPS = Global Positioning System**; begun **1973**, first satellite 1978.
[38:0:3] - **24 satellites in 6 orbits** at **20,200 km**; each circles Earth **twice a day**; about 30 now (Mar 2026).
[38:0:4] - Signals **L1** (1,575.42 MHz) + **L2**.
[38:0:5] - **1 microsecond** clock error ≈ **300 m** position error.
## SUB 1 | Navic – Constellation and Services
[38:1:0] - **NavIC = Navigation with Indian Constellation**; formerly **IRNSS**; fully operational **2018**.
[38:1:1] - Genesis: US denied GPS data in the **1999 Kargil War**.
[38:1:2] - **7 satellites: 3 GEO + 4 inclined GSO** (at **29°**).
[38:1:3] - Coverage: India + **1,500 km** beyond its borders.
[38:1:4] - Bands: **L5 and S**; NVS satellites add civilian **L1**.

[38:1:7] GPS = 24 satellites, 6 orbits, global; NavIC = 7, regional, L5 + S (L1/L2 are GPS).

=== NOTES 5:39 | Earth Observation – Irs, Cartosat, Resourcesat, Oceansat, Risat, Eos
## SUB 0 | Remote Sensing – Definition and Components
[39:0:0] - **Remote sensing**: information about the Earth **without physical contact**, by measuring **reflected and emitted electromagnetic radiation** from a distance (satellite or aircraft).
[39:0:1] - It uses **electromagnetic waves** (part of the EM spectrum).
[39:0:2] # Components (stages)
[39:0:3] - 1. Energy source / illumination
[39:0:4] - 2. Radiation through the atmosphere
[39:0:5] - 3. Interaction with the target
[39:0:6] - 4. Recording by the sensor
[39:0:7] - 5. Transmission, reception and processing
[39:0:8] - 6. Interpretation and analysis
[39:0:9] - 7. Application
[39:0:10] APPSC 2022: "Interaction of Energy with **Minimisation**" is NOT a component; nor is physical sampling.
## SUB 1 | Sensors, Imaging Types and Resolution
[39:1:0] # Passive vs active
[39:1:1] TABLE head: Type | Energy | Examples
  [39:1:1.0] Passive | Reflected sunlight or emitted heat | IRS, Resourcesat, Oceansat, visible cameras, multispectral imagers, infrared radiometers
  [39:1:1.1] Active | Sends its own pulse, reads the echo | RISAT SAR (microwave), laser/LiDAR
[39:1:2] APPSC 2023: **Laser = active sensor**; heat of volcanic eruptions is captured by **infrared-sensitive** sensors.
[39:1:3] # Optical vs SAR vs hyperspectral vs thermal
[39:1:4] TABLE head: Imaging | Feature
  [39:1:4.0] Optical (visible/NIR) | Reflected light; needs daylight and clear sky
  [39:1:4.1] SAR (microwave) | Pierces clouds and darkness; all-weather, day-night
  [39:1:4.2] Hyperspectral | Hundreds of narrow bands → identifies materials/chemicals
  [39:1:4.3] Thermal infrared | Emitted heat → volcanoes, forest fires, urban heat
[39:1:5] APPSC 2021 key gave **Visible** as the band for cloud-covered disaster areas; in principle SAR (microwave) sees through cloud. Follow the key in that exact question.
[39:1:6] # Resolution types
[39:1:7] - Four: **spatial, spectral, temporal, radiometric**.
[39:1:8] APPSC 2025: "**Chemical** resolution" does not exist.
[39:1:9] - **EM spectrum order:** wavelength falls from radio → microwave → infrared → visible → ultraviolet → X-rays → **gamma rays**, so gamma rays have the shortest wavelength.
[39:1:10] - **Resolution meanings:** spatial = smallest object a sensor can see; spectral = number and width of bands; **temporal = how often the satellite revisits the same place**; radiometric = sensitivity to small differences in energy.
## SUB 2 | Remote-sensing Techniques → What They Map
[39:2:0] TABLE head: Technique | Maps
  [39:2:0.0] InSAR | Ground deformation – subsidence, earthquake/volcano uplift
  [39:2:0.1] LiDAR | Laser ranging – elevation, 3D terrain, canopy height
  [39:2:0.2] NDVI | Vegetation health from red vs near-infrared reflectance
  [39:2:0.3] Thermal infrared | Volcanic heat, forest fires, urban heat
  [39:2:0.4] Hyperspectral | Materials and chemicals
[39:2:1] - **MODIS** = Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectro-radiometer; detected **52,785 forest fires** (Nov 2020 – Jun 2021).
## SUB 3 | Orbits for Earth Observation
[39:3:0] TABLE head: Orbit | Altitude | Use
  [39:3:0.0] LEO (Low Earth Orbit) | 200–2,000 km | Earth observation, ISS, Starlink-type constellations
  [39:3:0.1] MEO | 2,000–30,000 km | Navigation (GPS)
  [39:3:0.2] GEO (geostationary) | 35,786 km over the equator | Communication, weather; appears fixed
  [39:3:0.3] GSO (geosynchronous) | Same height, inclined | Traces a figure-8; NavIC's 4 inclined satellites
  [39:3:0.4] SSPO (sun-synchronous polar) | ~500–900 km | Polar imaging
[39:3:1] - **Sun-synchronous**: near-polar; passes each place at the **same local solar time** → even lighting; so most EO satellites use it.
[39:3:2] - **Geostationary** = geosynchronous, **circular**, in the **plane of the equator**, zero inclination; fixed over one longitude; also called **Clarke's orbit**; used for telecom relay.
[39:3:3] - GEO and GSO period = one **sidereal day, 23 h 56 min 4.09 s**; only geostationary looks fixed.
[39:3:4] - Trade-off: **LEO** = finer detail, periodic revisit; **geosynchronous** imagers = coarser detail, very frequent viewing (EOS-05: targets every 5 min, all India every 30 min).
