## SUB 0 | Causes of Biodiversity Loss – Habitat Destruction First
[0:0] # The most-asked fact
[0:1] - **Greatest / most important / main cause:** **habitat destruction (loss of natural habitats and vegetation)** – NextGen: "the primary and major reason for biodiversity loss"; IPBES also ranks it first. Not climate change, invasives, over-hunting or pollution alone.
[0:2] - **Deforestation → biodiversity loss:** cutting and burning forests **destroys natural habitats, driving species to extinction and disrupting ecosystems**.
[0:3] - **Primary driver of deforestation and habitat loss:** **agricultural expansion and urban development** (also land clearance for agriculture, mines, dams, harbours, industries, settlements); in the world the major cause of deforestation is **expansion of agriculture** (not paper industry, firewood or fires).
[0:4] - Main cause overall = **human influence**: habitat alteration and loss, exotic species, over-exploitation, pollution, GMOs; **over-consumption of resources** destroys habitats, raises pollution and drives climate change.
[0:5] - In India: habitat loss (urbanisation, infrastructure, mining, farming) comes first, then climate change, invasives, pollution, over-exploitation.
[0:6] - Loss of biodiversity is a major problem resulting from **modern agriculture** (monoculture, agro-chemicals).
[0:7] # Evil Quartet vs HIPPO
[0:8] TABLE head: Evil Quartet (NCERT / NextGen) | HIPPO (popularised by E. O. Wilson)
  [0:8.0] Habitat loss and fragmentation | Habitat destruction
  [0:8.1] Over-exploitation (Steller's sea cow, passenger pigeon) | Invasive species
  [0:8.2] Invasion of alien species (Nile perch → 200+ cichlids lost in Lake Victoria) | Pollution
  [0:8.3] Co-extinctions | human Population
  [0:8.4] — | Over-harvesting
[0:9] - **HIPPO** was popularised by **E. O. Wilson**. **Co-extinction** is only in the Evil Quartet; **pollution** and **population** only in HIPPO. Newer lists add **climate change** and **disease**.
[0:10] # Fragmentation and edge effect
[0:11] TABLE head: Term | Meaning
  [0:11.0] Fragmentation | Large habitats broken into small patches; hits migratory animals and wide-ranging mammals and birds hardest
  [0:11.1] Edge effect | Patch boundaries get more light, wind, heat, predators and weeds; as fragments shrink, interior species decline
[0:12] - Tropical rainforest cover fell from **14% to 6%** of the land surface; Amazon cleared for soybean and cattle.
[0:13] # Disturbs vs restores ecological balance
[0:14] TABLE head: Disturbs (a cause of loss) | Restores (NOT a cause)
  [0:14.0] Roads, urbanisation, extension of agriculture, clear-felling, jhum (shifting) cultivation, mining, quarrying, encroachment, exploitation of forest produce | Afforestation of degraded land, sacred groves, protected areas, biosphere reserves, wildlife corridors
  [0:14.1] Draining wetlands, mangroves to shrimp farms | Removal of invasives with native restocking

[1:0] # Accepted threats vs distractors
[1:1] TABLE head: Threat (correct) | NOT a threat (distractor)
  [1:1.0] Global warming, habitat fragmentation, invasion of alien species | Promotion of vegetarianism
  [1:1.1] Loss of habitat, loss of genetic variation | Loss of appetite
  [1:1.2] Habitat pollution, over-exploitation, illegal wildlife trade, forest fires | Creation of biosphere reserves, expansion of protected areas
[1:2] - UPSC 2012 / RAS 2016: threats = **1, 2 and 3** (global warming, fragmentation, alien species), not vegetarianism.
[1:3] - Direct threats (NextGen): habitat destruction, exotic species, diseases, pollution, **genetic assimilation**.
[1:4] - **Extinction-prone traits:** large body size, small population, low reproductive rate, **narrow (specialised) niche**, **lack of genetic variability**, endemism; a **broad niche** makes a species adaptable – NOT a cause.
[1:5] - When a species becomes extinct, **its obligately associated species also go extinct** (co-extinction) – so "associated species do not become extinct" is false.

## SUB 2 | IUCN Red List Categories and Criteria
[2:0] - **Nine** categories, highest to lowest risk: **EX → EW → CR → EN → VU → NT → LC**, plus **DD** and **NE**.
[2:1] TABLE head: Code | Full form | Meaning
  [2:1.0] EX | Extinct | No reasonable doubt that the last individual has died, after exhaustive surveys – no fixed number of years
  [2:1.1] EW | Extinct in the Wild | Survives only in cultivation, captivity or as a naturalised population well outside its past range
  [2:1.2] CR | Critically Endangered | Extremely high risk of extinction in the wild (not "critically extinct")
  [2:1.3] EN | Endangered | Very high risk; "species in danger of extinction"; numbers reduced to a critical level
  [2:1.4] VU | Vulnerable | High risk; continuous decline from over-exploitation or habitat destruction
  [2:1.5] NT | Near Threatened (not "Not Threatened") | Close to qualifying for a threatened category
  [2:1.6] LC | Least Concern | Widespread and abundant
  [2:1.7] DD | Data Deficient | Inadequate information
  [2:1.8] NE | Not Evaluated | Not yet assessed
[2:2] - **Threatened = CR + EN + VU.** "Rare" (older Indian usage) = not endangered or vulnerable now but at risk.
[2:3] - **50-year rule:** older books say a species is extinct if **not seen in the wild for 50 years** (a 1980s suggestion); IUCN today uses "no reasonable doubt" instead.
[2:4] - **Extirpation** = local extinction: a species gone **from a specific area but surviving elsewhere** (e.g. tigers extirpated from Kawal and Sri Venkateswara NP).
[2:5] # Criteria A-E
[2:6] TABLE head: Criterion | Basis
  [2:6.0] A | Declining population (past, present, projected)
  [2:6.1] B | Geographic range (size, fragmentation, decline)
  [2:6.2] C | Small population size and decline
  [2:6.3] D | Very small or restricted population
  [2:6.4] E | Quantitative analysis of extinction risk (PVA)
[2:7] - Economic value in trade is NOT a criterion.
[2:8] > Exam angle: CR full form (APPSC Group 2), "species in danger of extinction" = endangered, extirpation, 50-year rule.

## SUB 4 | Red Data Book, IUCN and Biodiversity Reports
[4:0] # Red Data Book / Red List
[4:1] - Published by **IUCN**; lists **threatened (endangered) plants and animals** in danger of extinction – **not** endemic species of hotspots, **not** protected sites.
[4:2] - IUCN's endangered-species list = **Red List**; Brown, White, Black, Green, Yellow lists are distractors. "Red" symbolises danger.
[4:3] - Red List started **1964**; first Red Data Book volumes issued **1966** by IUCN's Species Survival Commission; nickname "**Barometer of Life**"; measures **extinction risk**.
[4:4] TABLE head: Page colour | Meaning
  [4:4.0] Red / pink | Endangered / critically endangered
  [4:4.1] Green | Formerly threatened, now recovered
[4:5] - **IUCN Green Status (2021)** measures species **recovery**.
[4:6] - Red List **2026-1 (July 2026)**: **175,909** assessed; **49,505 (~28%)** threatened. India's **Red List Roadmap**: **70,000** species by **2027**, led by the **NBA**; 7,076 Indian animal species in Red List categories.
[4:7] # IUCN

## SUB 5 | Invasive Alien Species
[5:0] - **Invasive alien species:** non-native organisms introduced outside their natural range that "**arrive, survive and thrive**", outcompete natives and cause economic, environmental and health harm; exotic plants that **crowd out native plants** = **invasive plants**.
[5:1] - Three tests: **non-native origin, uncontrolled spread, measurable harm** (NOT commercial usefulness). Defined in India under the **WPA 1972** (Amendment 2022, **Section 62A**: Centre regulates/prohibits IAS; new **Schedule IV** = CITES-appendix specimens, NOT invasives).
[5:2] - Invasives contributed to nearly **40%** of known animal extinctions since the **17th century**; threaten **1 in 10** Red List species; damage per species is higher in the **Global South**.
[5:3] - Invasive plants cut **medicinal plants, fruits and fodder grass** used by indigenous communities, and degrade the **herbivore forage on which tigers depend** – both harms are real.

## SUB 6 | Extinction – Mass Extinctions and Co-extinction
[6:0] TABLE head: Term | Meaning
  [6:0.0] Extinction | Disappearance of all members of a species (death of the last individual), globally
  [6:0.1] Extirpation | Local extinction only
  [6:0.2] Co-extinction | Loss of one species drags down its obligate partners (plant–pollinator, host–parasite)
  [6:0.3] Background extinction | Slow natural loss at a low steady rate
  [6:0.4] Mass extinction | High percentage of species lost in a short geological period
  [6:0.5] Sixth extinction | Present, human-driven wave – over-exploitation of resources, habitat loss and fragmentation, ecosystem destruction, pollution, climate change
[6:1] - **Five** mass extinctions so far; the **fifth** (end-Cretaceous, ~**66 million years ago**) ended the dinosaurs – **asteroid-impact theory of Luis Alvarez** (1980, with Walter Alvarez).
[6:2] - Sixth extinction: rates **100-1,000 times** pre-human rates (NextGen); IPBES: tens to hundreds of times background. It is not about GM crops, monoculture or a feared meteorite.
[6:3] - **Dodo** (Mauritius): classic **co-extinction** case – the tree whose seeds it dispersed (*Calvaria*/tambalacoque) failed to reproduce.
[6:4] - IUCN Red List (2014): **784** extinctions in 500 years; 128 bird species lost in 500 years. Extinct: **passenger pigeon, Steller's sea cow** (over-exploitation), Bali, Javan and Caspian tigers.
[6:5] - **Extinct in the Wild:** Hawaiian crow, Père David's deer (only in captivity).
[6:6] - **India:** **cheetah** declared extinct **1952** – the only large mammal lost since Independence; ZSI lists **cheetah, pink-headed duck, mountain quail** as extinct from India (not the passenger pigeon).
[6:7] > Exam angle: definition (extinction, extirpation), sixth extinction (UPSC 2018 ×2), dodo (UPSC 1998 ×2), Alvarez (SSC 2019).

## SUB 9 | Climate Change and Consequences of Loss
[9:0] - Warming causes **coral bleaching**, ocean acidification, shifts in ecosystem boundaries and **disrupted migration**; rising temperatures **shift species ranges** and raise extinction risk in hotspots (Jun 2026); a warming Arctic is opening to **alien plants** (Feb 2026).
[9:1] - Loss disrupts species interactions, **pollination**, **nutrient cycling**, water and soil services → lower **food security** for resource-dependent communities; once lost, biodiversity cannot be recreated.
[9:2] - **Zoonotic spillover** rises with habitat disruption and wildlife trade → **One Health** approach (humans, animals, ecosystems).
[9:3] > Exam angle: own MCQs only (assertion-reason, consequences).
