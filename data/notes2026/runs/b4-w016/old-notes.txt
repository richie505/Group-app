## SUB 1 | Pressure Belts – Doldrums, Horse Latitudes, ITCZ
[16:1:0] # The belts (asked 11 times)
[16:1:1] TABLE head: Belt | Latitude | Other name / nature
  [16:1:1.0] Equatorial low | 0–5° N and S, on both sides of the Equator | Doldrums – calm, rising air; NE and SE trades converge (ITCZ)
  [16:1:1.1] Subtropical high | 30–35° N and S | Horse latitudes – calm, sinking dry air, hot deserts; dynamically induced
  [16:1:1.2] Subpolar low | 60–65° | Westerlies meet polar easterlies; storms; dynamically induced
  [16:1:1.3] Polar high | Poles (90°) | Cold, dense, sinking air; thermally induced
[16:1:2] - **ITCZ** = low-pressure zone **at / near the Equator** where the **north-east and south-east trades converge** (APPSC Asst. Chemist; MPPCS 2018; CDS 2007) – not at the Tropics or poles.
[16:1:3] - **Doldrums** = the **equatorial low-pressure belt** (NDA 2007) with calm winds – **not** strong winds (APPSC Group-1: "doldrums are strong winds" and "NH trades blow from NW" are both wrong → **neither**).
[16:1:4] - **Horse latitudes** ~**30° (30–35°)** – sub-tropical high, **dynamically induced** by sinking air of the Hadley cell, calm (NDA 2012); sailors threw horses overboard when becalmed.
[16:1:5] - **Hadley cell**: air rises at the ITCZ and **descends at the subtropical high-pressure cells** (~30°) (CDS 2020).
[16:1:6] - Subpolar low at 60–65° instead of a high (IAS 2002): converging westerlies and polar easterlies; the lows are semi-permanent **over oceans** (Aleutian, Icelandic) – both true, but R does not explain A.
[16:1:7] - Belts **shift 5–10°** with the overhead Sun – **north in the NH summer**, south in winter.
[16:1:8] ~~ Sources: PMF Physical Geography 05 p.5-7; NCERT Fundamentals of Physical Geography ch.10; app notes 4:16 subs 2-3
## SUB 2 | Atmospheric Pressure – Causes, Gradient and Instruments
[16:2:0] # Pressure and weather (asked 10 times)
[16:2:1] - Atmospheric pressure = **weight of the air column**, held by **gravitational pull** (APPSC Panchayat Secretary).
[16:2:2] - Heated air **expands → density falls → low pressure**; cooled air contracts → high pressure.
[16:2:3] - Pressure **falls with altitude** → at high altitudes some people get **nose bleeding** (low pressure) (APPSC AE).
[16:2:4] - **Standard sea-level pressure 1013.25 mb (hPa)**; **isobars** join places of equal pressure.
[16:2:5] - **Pressure gradient** decides **wind velocity** (APPSC Group-II 2011): closely spaced isobars = steep gradient = strong wind. Air moves high → low.
[16:2:6] - **Sudden fall of the barometer** (mercury) = **stormy weather** (UKPSC 2002; IAS 2001); rising = fair weather.
[16:2:7] - A **low-pressure area** with converging winds = **cyclone**; high = anticyclone.
[16:2:8] # Instruments
[16:2:9] TABLE head: Instrument | Measures
  [16:2:9.0] Barometer (mercury / aneroid), barograph | Atmospheric pressure (APPSC; NDA 2018)
  [16:2:9.1] Aneroid barometer | No liquid – an evacuated metal box
  [16:2:9.2] Anemometer | Wind speed
  [16:2:9.3] Wind vane | Wind direction
[16:2:10] ~~ Sources: NextGen Rocket General Geography #7 p.51 (pressure); PMF Physical Geography 05 p.1-3; app notes 4:16 sub 0
[16:2:11] - **Boiling point is highest at sea level**, where air pressure is greatest – so water boils hotter at coastal **Visakhapatnam** than at higher places inland.
## SUB 3 | Planetary Winds and Wind Controls
[16:3:0] # Planetary winds (asked 7 times)
[16:3:1] TABLE head: Wind | From → to | NH | SH
  [16:3:1.0] Trade winds (easterlies) | Horse latitudes (subtropical high) → equatorial low | North-east (blow towards the south-west) | South-east
  [16:3:1.1] Westerlies | Subtropical high → subpolar low (~35–60°) | South-west | North-west
  [16:3:1.2] Polar easterlies | Polar high → subpolar low | North-east | South-east
[16:3:2] - **Trade winds = planetary winds**; monsoon is periodic; Chinook and Mistral are local (IAS 2009).
[16:3:3] - Order from Equator to pole: **doldrums → trade winds → westerlies → polar winds** (NDA 2014).
[16:3:4] - Westerlies blow between about 35° and 60° in **each** hemisphere – not "30°N to 60°S"; the **western disturbances** that give NW India winter rain come with the westerlies (UPSC 2015 → only II).
[16:3:5] - **NW European ports** stay ice-free all year because of the warm **North Atlantic Drift**; the south-westerlies do blow there all year, but are not the reason (UPPCS 2002: both true, R not the explanation).
[16:3:6] - Trades push tropical cyclones **west onto east coasts**; westerlies turn them north-east.
[16:3:7] # Wind controls (asked 4 times)
[16:3:8] - Surface horizontal wind = **pressure gradient + friction + Coriolis force** (CDS 2015).
[16:3:9] - **Coriolis force**: deflects **right in NH, left in SH** (**Ferrel's law**); **zero at the Equator, maximum at the poles**; grows with wind speed; depends on latitude, **not on hemisphere**.
[16:3:10] - **Geostrophic wind**: upper air, parallel to isobars, when **pressure gradient force = Coriolis force** (UKPSC 2024).
[16:3:11] - **SW monsoon** direction: SE trades crossing the Equator are deflected to the **right by the Coriolis force** (NDA 2011).
[16:3:12] - **Friction** slows surface winds, strongest over land, negligible aloft. No cyclones within ~5° of the Equator (Coriolis negligible).
[16:3:13] - **Walker circulation** = east–west Pacific cell; Hadley cell = meridional.
[16:3:14] ~~ Sources: PMF Physical Geography 05 p.11-14; NextGen Indian Geography 03 p.9 (Coriolis); NCERT Fundamentals of Physical Geography ch.10; app notes 4:16 subs 1, 4
## SUB 4 | Jet Streams and the Polar Vortex
[16:4:0] # Jet streams (asked 8 times)
[16:4:1] - Narrow bands of **high-velocity westerly winds** in the **upper troposphere near the tropopause** (8–15 km; generally **10–12 km**) (NDA 2011; CDS 2017).
[16:4:2] - Found in **both hemispheres** – not the NH only (UPSC 2020); NH jets stronger.
[16:4:3] - **Sub-tropical jet** forms at about **10–16 km** (option "10–20 km", APPSC AD 2017); polar front jet lower (6–9 km).
[16:4:4] - **Polar front jet is strongest in winter**, not summer (CDS 2017) – greatest temperature contrast.
[16:4:5] - Associated with the **monsoon** (APSLPRB SI 2023, 2018): they regulate onset and withdrawal.
[16:4:6] TABLE head: Jet | Position / season | Role
  [16:4:6.0] Sub-Tropical Westerly Jet | South of the Himalayas in winter, north in summer | Brings western disturbances; leaves before the monsoon "burst"; returns ~October
  [16:4:6.1] Tropical Easterly Jet | Summer, 5–20°N (South China Sea → East Africa) | Strengthens the SW monsoon
  [16:4:6.2] Somali (Findlater) Jet | Low-level, off East Africa | Boosts monsoon flow
[16:4:7] - NCERT: in winter the westerly jet blows across Asia **north of the Himalayas, parallel to the Tibetan highlands**; **Tibet splits it into two branches**; the **southern branch** (south of the Himalayas) controls India's winter weather – not the northern branch → "1 and 2" (State PSC).
[16:4:8] - First identified by **Wasaburo Ooishi** (Japan, 1920s); widely known in **World War II**.
[16:4:9] # Polar vortex
[16:4:10] - Cold **low** around the poles, **anticlockwise** (NH), strongest in **winter**. A **strong vortex keeps cold air up north** (APPSC 2018); when weakened (Rossby waves, sudden stratospheric warming) cold spells reach mid-latitudes.
[16:4:11] ~~ Sources: PMF Physical Geography 06 p.24-25; NextGen Indian Geography 06 p.10, 13 (jets and monsoon); NCERT India Physical Environment ch.4; app notes 4:16 sub 6
## SUB 5 | Roaring Forties, Furious Fifties, Shrieking Sixties
[16:5:0] # The southern westerlies (asked 7 times)
[16:5:1] TABLE head: Name | Latitude
  [16:5:1.0] Roaring Forties | 40–50° S (strong ocean westerlies, "40–60° S" option)
  [16:5:1.1] Furious Fifties | 50–60° S
  [16:5:1.2] Shrieking Sixties | Around 60° S
[16:5:2] - They are the **westerlies of the Southern Hemisphere** (MPPCS 2015; State PSC) – stronger and steadier because the SH has **less landmass** (open ocean, little friction) (IAS 2011 → only I; Coriolis is not higher in the SH).
[16:5:3] - UPSC 2000: they blow with **great strength and constancy**, generally **from north-west to east (west to east)** in the SH, with **overcast skies, rain and raw weather** → **2, 3 and 4**; they do **not** blow uninterrupted in the NH (land breaks them).
[16:5:4] - NDA 2018: strong westerlies of the SH oceans (true); they blow **west to east**, not east to west, and there is **no** abundance of landmasses → **only I**.
[16:5:5] - Region: the southern oceans, incl. the **Indian Ocean** (of the options given).
[16:5:6] ~~ Sources: PMF Physical Geography 05 p.13; G.C. Leong ch.12; app notes 4:16 sub 5
## SUB 6 | Monsoon and Periodic Winds
[16:6:0] # Monsoon (asked 6 times)
[16:6:1] - Word from Arabic **"mausim" = season** (MPPCS 2013; APPSC ASO 2017).
[16:6:2] - **Seasonal reversal of winds** due to **differential heating of land and sea** (State PSC).
[16:6:3] - Summer: **high temperature and low pressure** over the subcontinent draw air from the Indian Ocean → **south-west monsoon** (UPSC 1996), flowing **from south-west to north-east** (MPPCS 2012).
[16:6:4] - Modern theory (**1957**): ITCZ shift + jet streams + Tibet Plateau + ENSO. Halley (1686): giant land–sea breeze.
[16:6:5] - Mediterranean (UPPCS 1992 key "monsoonal"): in winter air from the surrounding high-pressure lands flows towards the warmer sea, reversing in summer; its winter rain itself comes with the westerlies.
[16:6:6] - **ITCZ** moves north over the Indian plains in summer (monsoon trough) – this **helps** the monsoon; in winter it moves south and highs over **Tibet and Siberia** set up the **north-east monsoon**.
[16:6:7] - The **Himalayas** act as a **climatic divide**, giving India its distinct monsoon climate.
[16:6:8] - **El Niño** weakens the monsoon; **positive IOD** can offset it.
[16:6:9] - AP: SW monsoon 2026 entered via **Rayalaseema** (6 June 2026); IMD expected **below-normal** AP rain under El Niño. AP coastline **~1,053 km – third longest** (AP Socio-Economic Survey 2025-26).
[16:6:10] # Breezes
[16:6:11] TABLE head: Breeze | Time | Direction
  [16:6:11.0] Sea breeze | Day | Sea → land (land heats faster, lower pressure)
  [16:6:11.1] Land breeze | Night | Land → sea
  [16:6:11.2] Valley (anabatic) | Day | Uphill
  [16:6:11.3] Mountain (katabatic) | Night | Downhill; cold air in valleys → inversion, frost
[16:6:12] - Land and sea breezes are **daily**; they moderate **AP coastal** temperatures.
[16:6:13] ~~ Sources: NextGen Indian Geography 06 p.13 (monsoon theories); NCERT India Physical Environment ch.4; app notes 4:16 sub 7
[16:6:14] - **Western disturbances** = winter rain-bearing lows from the Mediterranean carried east by the westerly jet (not a warm Indian Ocean); **Southern Oscillation** = sea-level pressure see-saw between the eastern Pacific and the Indian Ocean.

=== NOTES 4:17 | Humidity, Evaporation, Condensation, Clouds and Forms of Precipitation
## SUB 0 | Clouds – Families, Types and Thunderclouds
[17:0:0] # Cloud basics (asked 15 times)
[17:0:1] - **Cloud** = mass of minute water droplets or ice crystals formed by **condensation** of water vapour in free air around **hygroscopic nuclei**, mainly by **adiabatic cooling** of rising air below its dew point – clouds are the result of **condensation** (UKPSC 2016), not evaporation.
[17:0:2] - Clouds **float** because of their **low density** (UPSC 1995): the droplets are tiny, fall extremely slowly and are held up by gentle updrafts.
[17:0:3] - Four basic forms: **cirrus, cumulus, stratus, nimbus** ("nimbus" = rain-bearing; black or dark grey, dense, opaque).
[17:0:4] TABLE head: Family (height) | Cloud | Appearance | Weather
  [17:0:4.0] High (8,000–12,000 m; ice crystals) | Cirrus | Thin, detached, feathery, silky, always white | Fair; no rain
  [17:0:4.1] High | Cirrostratus | Thin milky sheet; produces the halo | Warm front coming
  [17:0:4.2] High | Cirrocumulus | Small globular masses, ripples ("mackerel sky") | Fair
  [17:0:4.3] Middle (2–6 km) | Altostratus / altocumulus | Grey-blue sheet; Sun as through ground glass | Light continuous rain
  [17:0:4.4] Low (below 2 km) | Stratus, stratocumulus, nimbostratus | Layered; nimbostratus dark, thick, shapeless | Stratus – drizzle; nimbostratus – steady, continuous rain
  [17:0:4.5] Vertical development | Cumulus | Heaped cauliflower, flat base, cotton wool (4,000–7,000 m) | Fair; convective; grows into Cb
  [17:0:4.6] Vertical development (to ~15 km) | Cumulonimbus | Towering, anvil top | Thunderstorm, heavy short rain, hail, cloudburst
[17:0:5] - APPSC Group-I 2024 match: **Cirrus – silky; Cirrostratus – milky; Cirrocumulus – small globular masses; Cumulus – cauliflower** (A-II, B-I, C-IV, D-III).
[17:0:6] - **Cirrus clouds are composed of ice crystals** (UPSC 2004 correct statement); rising domes with flat base = cumulus, and cumulus is **not** a high cloud.
[17:0:7] - **Low cloud set: stratus, nimbus (nimbostratus), cumulus** (IAS 2004); **nimbostratus is a low cloud** (NDA 2007), cirro- = high, alto- = middle.
[17:0:8] - **Continuous precipitation = nimbostratus** (NDA 2018); **highly intense rain = cumulonimbus** (JPSC 2003).
[17:0:9] - Highest of a given set: **cirrostratus** is the only high cloud among altocumulus, altostratus, cumulus, cirrostratus (UP Lower 2009).
[17:0:10] - **Greatest vertical extent**, convectional rain with lightning and thunder = **cumulonimbus** (IAS 2003); rain-bearing clouds = **nimbostratus and cumulonimbus**.
[17:0:11] - **Tornadoes form from cumulonimbus** (supercell) clouds (APPSC FRO 2020); the funnel cloud and wall cloud are parts hanging from the Cb, not the parent cloud.
[17:0:12] - Rain clouds look **dark/black** because they are thick and dense with droplets, so very little sunlight gets through to the base (CDS 2012 – book key: the droplets **absorb** the sunlight; strictly the light is mostly scattered/reflected away).
[17:0:13] # Thunderclouds and thunder
[17:0:14] - Thundercloud (Cb) development needs **condensation, high temperature and humidity, orography and vertical winds – all four** (UPSC 2002; orographic thunderstorms are common).
[17:0:15] - **Thunder** is the sound of air **suddenly heated and expanded by lightning** (shock wave) – not clouds meeting, lightning "separating" clouds or upward air movement → **none of these** (IAS 2013).
[17:0:16] - **Waterspout**: funnel extending from a cumulus/cumulonimbus cloud **over water**; one recorded over the Brahmaputra in Barpeta, Assam (26 Aug 2026).

## SUB 1 | Evaporation, Latent Heat, Boiling Point and Adiabatic Change
[17:1:0] # Evaporation (asked 15 times)
[17:1:1] - **Evaporation / vaporisation** = change from liquid to gas (SSC 2019); it is a **physical change** (UPPSC 1992) and goes on at all temperatures from the surface.
[17:1:2] - Rate **rises with larger surface area** (CDS 2019), higher temperature, **lower humidity**, **stronger wind** and lower pressure; falls with salinity.
[17:1:3] - Oceans give **84%** of annual evaporation, continents 16%.
[17:1:4] # Latent heat
[17:1:5] - **Evaporation absorbs** latent heat; **condensation releases** it → powers **thunderstorms and cyclones**. Freezing (snow forming in clouds) also releases heat = **exothermic**; melting ice absorbs heat = **endothermic**.
[17:1:6] TABLE head: Change | Latent heat | Note
  [17:1:6.0] Fusion of ice | 80 cal/g (3.34 × 10⁵ J/kg) | Melts at 0°C; temperature constant
  [17:1:6.1] Vaporisation of water | 540 cal/g (2.26 × 10⁶ J/kg) | Boils at 100°C; temperature constant
[17:1:7] - Heat taken to turn liquid into gas **without change in temperature = latent heat of vaporisation** (NDA 2017); at the boiling point the **temperature remains constant** (SSC 2016).
[17:1:8] - Latent heat of vaporisation is **much greater** than that of fusion; **steam at 100°C holds more heat** than water at 100°C → **steam burns are worse** (SSC 2015) – it releases latent heat on condensing.
[17:1:9] - UPSC 2003: only "an air-conditioner extracts heat at the **evaporator** coils and rejects it at the **condenser** coils" is correct. **Refrigerator**: refrigerant absorbs heat on evaporation, releases it on condensation.
[17:1:10] # Pressure and boiling point
[17:1:11] - **Lower pressure lowers the boiling point** (Asst. Comm. 2018): at high altitude water boils below 100°C because **air pressure is less** (NDA 2017) → a **pressure cooker** raises pressure and boiling point (CDS 2010).
[17:1:12] # Adiabatic change and the uplift chain
[17:1:13] - **Adiabatic** temperature change = by **expansion (cooling) and compression (warming)** of air, without heat being added or removed (CDS 2018); compressing gas in a bicycle pump = **adiabatic heating**.
[17:1:14] - Air **descending** a mountain is compressed → **adiabatic warming** (AE 2017), as in the leeward rain shadow.
[17:1:15] - Uplift chain: warm air rises → **adiabatic cooling to the dew point** → **condensation** around nuclei → clouds → droplets grow and fall → precipitation. Rising air does **not** become warmer.
[17:1:16] ~~ Sources: PMF Physical Geography 05 p.22-24 (evaporation, condensation, adiabatic change); NextGen Rocket General Geography #7 p.52; NCERT Science (latent heat, boiling point); app notes 4:17 sub 1

## SUB 3 | Precipitation – Forms and Types of Rainfall
[17:3:0] # Forms (asked 10 times)
[17:3:1] TABLE head: Form | Feature
  [17:3:1.0] Rain | Drops over 0.5 mm
  [17:3:1.1] Drizzle | Fine drops under 0.5 mm (APPSC FSO flashcard), mostly from stratus
  [17:3:1.2] Snow | Ice crystals formed below 0°C
  [17:3:1.3] Sleet | Rain frozen into pellets falling through cold air
  [17:3:1.4] Hail | Layered ice balls (5–50 mm) in cumulonimbus with strong updrafts
[17:3:2] - Raindrops grow by two processes: **collision–coalescence** in **warm clouds that do not extend beyond the freezing level** (CDS 2018) and the **ice-crystal (Bergeron)** process in clouds rising above it.
[17:3:3] - **Langmuir precipitation** = Langmuir's **chain reaction** of drop collision, break-up and coalescence in warm convective clouds, giving heavy showers – APPSC FSO key: **cloud burst**.
[17:3:4] - **Cloudburst**: about **100 mm/hour** or more over **20–30 sq km** from cumulonimbus; the stored water falls when **updrafts weaken**; Dharali, **Uttarkashi** (Aug 2025).
[17:3:5] - **Blood (coloured) rain**: dust or spores mixed with raindrops – Kerala's red rain (**2001**). 16th Finance Commission recommended adding **heatwaves and lightning** to notified disasters (Apr 2026).
[17:3:6] # Types of rainfall
[17:3:7] TABLE head: Type | Mechanism | Where
  [17:3:7.0] Convectional | Strong heating → air rises, cools → cumulonimbus; afternoon, short, heavy | Equatorial belt (Amazon, Congo, SE Asian islands); mango showers
  [17:3:7.1] Cyclonic / frontal | Warm air lifted over cold air along fronts – cyclonic activity (SSC 2011) | Temperate regions; western disturbances (10–15 cm winter rain, NW India)
  [17:3:7.2] Orographic (relief) | Moist wind forced up a mountain barrier | Western Ghats (Agumbe), Mawsynram; leeward = rain shadow
[17:3:8] - **Amazon basin** has convectional rainfall (IAS 2007); convectional rain occurs **daily in the afternoon** near the equator, is **short** and comes from **cumulonimbus** – all three true (NDA 2008).
[17:3:9] - Equatorial areas get rain **all year** because high temperature and humidity cause **afternoon convectional rain** – A and R true, R explains A (IAS 2003; UPSC 1996).
[17:3:10] - **Rain-shadow effect** and **inversion of rainfall** (rain increases with height only up to a level, then decreases) are features of **orographic rainfall** (NDA 2011; NDA 2019).
[17:3:11] - Rain-shadow area = dry **leeward** side; descending air warms and dries (Pune ~70 cm vs Mahabaleshwar 600 cm). **Eastern side of the Western Ghats** is leeward → peninsular rainfall **falls west to east**; **Aravallis** give little rain to Rajasthan as winds blow **parallel** to them.

## SUB 4 | Cloud Seeding and Acid Rain
[17:4:0] # Cloud seeding (asked 10 times)
[17:4:1] - Artificial rain uses **silver iodide (AgI)** – its crystal shape mimics ice (UPPCS 2008, 2013, Spl. 2008; APPSC flashcard); also **potassium iodide**, **sodium chloride**, **dry ice** (solid CO₂), liquid propane; not calcium carbonate.
[17:4:2] - Artificial rain to reduce air pollution: **silver iodide and potassium iodide** (UPSC 2025).
[17:4:3] - **AP cloud seeding (July 2004)**: **silver iodide–sodium chloride**.
[17:4:4] - Works **only when suitable clouds already exist**. Kazakhstan began seeding with UAE help (Aug 2026).
[17:4:5] # Acid rain

## SUB 5 | Hydrological Cycle and Transpiration
[17:5:0] # Water cycle (asked 9 times)
[17:5:1] - Continuous exchange of water by **evaporation, transpiration, condensation and precipitation**; correct sequence **evaporation → condensation → precipitation → infiltration** (NDA 2010).
[17:5:2] TABLE head: In the cycle | NOT in the cycle
  [17:5:2.0] Evaporation, transpiration (= evapotranspiration) | Photosynthesis
  [17:5:2.1] Sublimation, condensation, precipitation | Combustion
  [17:5:2.2] Infiltration/percolation, runoff, groundwater flow | Respiration, hydration
[17:5:3] - **Hydration** = water absorbed into minerals (a weathering process) – **not** part of the hydrological cycle (APPSC Group-1).
[17:5:4] - **Underground water may be connected to surface water** (CDS 2015); transpiration does contribute to clouds and rainfall recharges groundwater.
[17:5:5] - **Forests** control the hydrological cycle (UPRO 2023) – "water engineers": interception, transpiration, deep roots, groundwater recharge.
[17:5:6] # Transpiration
[17:5:7] - Plants lose water by **transpiration** (APPSC Group-1), mainly through **stomata**; it pulls water up, **cools the plant** = "**nature's air conditioner**", adds moisture to air and enhances rainfall – it does **not decrease humidity**.
[17:5:8] - Rate is **reduced by decreased light intensity** (stomata close), high humidity, low temperature and still air.
[17:5:9] - **Hydrometeorology** = transfer of water and energy between land surface and lower atmosphere. India: precipitation ~**4,000 BCM**/year; utilisable **1,139 BCM** (**690 surface + 449 groundwater**).

## SUB 6 | Humidity and Dew Point
[17:6:0] # Measures of humidity (asked 7 times)
[17:6:1] - **Humidity** = water vapour present in the air – the result of the **presence of moisture in the air** (JPSC 2016); humid summer heat = **muggy** weather (UPUDA 2002).
[17:6:2] TABLE head: Measure | Meaning | Unit
  [17:6:2.0] Absolute humidity | Mass of water vapour per unit volume of air | g/m³
  [17:6:2.1] Specific humidity | Vapour per unit weight of air; unchanged by volume/temperature change | g/kg
  [17:6:2.2] Relative humidity (RH) | Actual vapour ÷ capacity at that temperature × 100 | %
  [17:6:2.3] Dew point | Temperature at which air becomes saturated; condensation begins below it | °C
[17:6:3] - When air temperature **equals the dew point, RH = 100%** (APPSC FSO flashcard) = saturation.
[17:6:4] - **Saturation deficit** = capacity minus actual vapour; **dew point** = temperature to which air must be cooled to saturate → both correct (NDA 2009).
[17:6:5] - Warmer air can hold **more** vapour – capacity **increases** with temperature (JPSC 2003); same vapour + warming → **RH falls**.
[17:6:6] - Water vapour: 0–4% of the air (up to **4%** in humid tropics), **decreases with height and latitude**; RH higher over oceans than continents.
[17:6:7] - AP: coastal Andhra RH **70–90%**; Rayalaseema below **30%** in peak summer.

## SUB 7 | Optical Phenomena – Rainbow and Halo
[17:7:0] # Rainbow and halo (asked 7 times)
[17:7:1] TABLE head: Phenomenon | Cause
  [17:7:1.0] Rainbow | Refraction, dispersion and (total) internal reflection of sunlight in raindrops; Sun behind the observer
  [17:7:1.1] Halo | Refraction through hexagonal ice crystals of thin high cirrus/cirrostratus; ring of about 22°
  [17:7:1.2] Corona | Diffraction by small water droplets of thin clouds
[17:7:2] - Rainbow is formed by **reflection, refraction and dispersion** (APPSC ASO 2017; APPSC Group-1); if only one is offered, **dispersion** (APPSC ABCWO; NDA 2017).
[17:7:3] - **Primary rainbow**: one internal reflection, **red outside, violet inside** → terminal bands **violet and red** (UP Lower 2015). Secondary: two reflections, colours reversed.
[17:7:4] - APPSC Group-I 2024: Sun halo = refraction in hexagonal ice crystals (I) and a **22° ring** (II) correct; III wrong – the halo comes from thin **cirrostratus**, not cumulonimbus.
[17:7:5] - Sun's halo = refraction in **ice crystals in cirrus clouds** (IAS 2002).

=== NOTES 4:18 | Air Masses and Fronts – Classification and Associated Weather
## SUB 0 | Air Masses – Nature, Source Regions and Classification
[18:0:0] # Air mass (asked 2 times)
[18:0:1] - **Air mass** = a large body of air with relatively **uniform temperature and humidity**, formed over a uniform **source region** (land **or** ocean); it controls **temperature, humidity, stability and precipitation**, and a new air mass changes the weather.
[18:0:2] - Ideal source regions are **high-pressure areas with gentle, divergent (anticyclonic) circulation** – the subtropical highs and the polar highs (Siberian, Canadian). Air masses do **not** develop in **cyclonic** conditions, and there are no major source regions in the mid-latitudes (NDA 2019).
[18:0:3] - **Bergeron** code: **first letter = moisture** (c = continental, dry; m = maritime, moist); **second letter = thermal source region** (T tropical, P polar, A arctic, E equatorial).
[18:0:4] TABLE head: Code | Source region | Properties
  [18:0:4.0] mE | Warm equatorial oceans | Hot, very humid
  [18:0:4.1] mT | Tropical oceans (Gulf of Mexico, Pacific, Atlantic) | Warm, moist, unstable
  [18:0:4.2] cT | Hot deserts – Sahara, Arabia, Australia | Hot, dry, stable
  [18:0:4.3] mP | Cold high-latitude (polar) oceans, 40°–60° | Cool, moist
  [18:0:4.4] cP | Siberia, northern Canada (cold land) | Cold, dry, stable
  [18:0:4.5] cA | Arctic basin, Greenland ice | Very cold, very dry
[18:0:5] - NDA 2015 correct pair: **Maritime Equatorial – warm oceans in the equatorial zone**; mP is not from warm tropical oceans, cT is a desert (not ocean) air mass, cP forms over land.
[18:0:6] - **Polar anticyclones** (Siberian, Canadian, Antarctic Highs) are built by cold, dense **cP/cA** air: **cold-core, strongest near the surface**; clear skies, fog, frost.
[18:0:7] ~~ Sources: PMF Physical Geography 06 p.34-37; NextGen Rocket General Geography #8 p.55; app notes 4:18 sub 0
## SUB 1 | Air-mass Modification
[18:1:0] # Modification
[18:1:1] - Leaving its source, an air mass **exchanges heat and moisture** with the surfaces it crosses and loses its original character.
[18:1:2] TABLE head: Situation | Result | Weather
  [18:1:2.0] Cold air over a warmer surface | Heated from below, unstable | Convection, cumulus, showers
  [18:1:2.1] Warm air over a colder surface | Cooled from below, stable | Fog, stratus, drizzle
  [18:1:2.2] Over ocean / land | Gains / loses moisture | –
[18:1:3] - A new air mass can cause a **sudden rise or fall in temperature**; cold waves, heat waves, blizzards follow air-mass shifts.
[18:1:4] - **Hygroscopic nuclei**: dust and salt particles on which vapour condenses.
[18:1:5] - **Atmospheric pressure** = force exerted by the weight of air; **wind** blows from **high to low pressure**.
[18:1:6] ~~ Sources: NextGen Rocket General Geography #8 p.55-57; PMF Physical Geography 06 p.35; app notes 4:18 sub 1
## SUB 2 | Fronts and the Polar Front Theory
[18:2:0] # Fronts
[18:2:1] - **Front** = sloping boundary zone between two contrasting air masses; lighter **warm air rises over denser cold air** (never the reverse) → adiabatic cooling, cloud, rain. **Mid-latitude (temperate) cyclones form along fronts**.
[18:2:2] TABLE head: Front | Formation | Slope | Weather
  [18:2:2.0] Warm | Warm air rides over cold | Gentle, gradual uplift | Prolonged light–moderate rain
  [18:2:2.1] Cold | Cold air undercuts warm, lifts it sharply | Steep | Cumulonimbus, heavy short showers, squalls, sudden temperature fall
  [18:2:2.2] Occluded | Cold front overtakes warm front | – | Mixed; last stage of a temperate cyclone
  [18:2:2.3] Stationary | Neither air mass advances | – | Cloud and rain for days
[18:2:3] - **Frontogenesis** = forming/sharpening of a front; **frontolysis** = its weakening and dissolution.
[18:2:4] - Warm front approach: **cirrus → cirrostratus → altostratus → nimbostratus**; after a cold front passes: **cold, clear air**. **Arcus (shelf/roll) clouds** form at the leading edge of thunderstorm outflows or cold fronts.
[18:2:5] # Polar front theory
[18:2:6] - By **Vilhelm and Jacob Bjerknes**, **Bergen School (Norway)**: wave on the polar front → warm and cold fronts → **occlusion** → dissipation.
[18:2:7] - **Western disturbances** reach NW India at the **occluded** stage.
[18:2:8] ~~ Sources: PMF Physical Geography 06 p.37-38; NextGen Rocket General Geography #8 p.56; app notes 4:18 sub 2-3
## SUB 3 | Air Masses Affecting India and in News
[18:3:0] # India and news
[18:3:1] - Winter: the **Himalayas block cold, dry Central Asian air** → north India about **3–8°C** warmer than places on the same latitude; the anticyclonic sinking air gives **clear skies, light northerly winds, low humidity, large daily range**.
[18:3:2] - SW monsoon = **moist maritime Indian Ocean air** fed by the low-level **Somali Jet**; AP also gets the **NE monsoon** when winter air returns over the Bay of Bengal.
[18:3:3] - Windward slope = orographic rain; **leeward slope = rain shadow**.
[18:3:4] - **Tornado Alley** (US Great Plains): **polar cold air meets warm, humid tropical (Gulf) air**.
[18:3:5] - **Strong polar vortex** keeps cold air confined to the north; **Arctic amplification**: a meandering jet carries warm air into the Arctic.
[18:3:6] - Jul 2025: an Antarctic high reaching far north brought snow to Chile's **Atacama** Desert, the first in over a decade.
[18:3:7] ~~ Sources: NCERT India Physical Environment ch.4; NextGen Rocket General Geography #9 p.61; app notes 4:18 sub 4

## SUB 3 | Tornadoes, Thunderstorms and Pre-monsoon Local Storms
[19:3:0] # Tornadoes (asked 11 times)
[19:3:1] - Rotating funnel from a **cumulonimbus/supercell thunderstorm** to the ground; winds **105–322 km/h**; moves up to about 90–100 km/h; lasts **minutes** (rarely over 15–20).
[19:3:2] - **Land-based** (hurricanes, typhoons, willy-willies are ocean-born); much **smaller** than hurricanes – both are destructive rotating storms.
[19:3:3] - Air spiralling to the centre conserves angular momentum → **angular speed increases** towards the centre.
[19:3:4] - **Tornado Alley**: central US plains (**Mississippi plains / Great Plains**) where cold polar air meets warm, moist **Gulf of Mexico** air.
[19:3:5] - **India is least vulnerable to tornadoes** among major hazards (rare; pre-monsoon; Belda 1998).
[19:3:6] TABLE head: Feature | Tornado | Hurricane
  [19:3:6.0] Size, life | Small, minutes | Hundreds of km, days
  [19:3:6.1] Where | Land-based | Ocean-born
[19:3:7] - **Waterspout** = tornado over water; seen over the **Brahmaputra, Barpeta (Assam)**, 26 Aug 2026. An ordinary thunderstorm is a short-lived single-cell Cb; **supercells** spawn tornadoes.
[19:3:8] # Thunderstorms
[19:3:9] - Thundercloud (Cb, up to 15 km) needs **condensation, high temperature and humidity, orography and vertical winds – all four** (IAS 2002). **Thunder** = air expanding explosively after lightning heats it.
[19:3:10] - **Hailstorms**: instability, moisture, updrafts; **synoptic** causes = Western Disturbances and wind discontinuity lines.
[19:3:11] - **Damini App**: lightning alerts in 15 languages; 104 detection locations.

## SUB 4 | Tropical Cyclones – Formation and Where They Form
[19:4:0] # Conditions (asked 10 times)
[19:4:1] TABLE head: Condition | Role
  [19:4:1.0] Warm ocean, SST above 26.5–27°C over a large area | Heat and moisture – most essential
  [19:4:1.1] Large supply of warm, moist air | Latent heat of condensation = energy source
  [19:4:1.2] Coriolis force | Rotation; too weak within about 5° of the Equator
  [19:4:1.3] Unstable troposphere, high humidity | Vigorous convection
  [19:4:1.4] Low vertical wind shear | Keeps the vertical structure organised
  [19:4:1.5] Pre-existing disturbance; upper-level divergence | Seed low / cyclonic circulation
[19:4:2] - A cyclone is a **violent storm of strong wind and heavy rain**; form **only over warm oceans**, not rivers, lakes or ponds (APPSC-GS 2025).
[19:4:3] - Warm sea, high humidity and instability = **formative / initial stage** (NDA 2017).
[19:4:4] - **Moving away from the sea its force decreases** because the latent-heat (moisture) supply is cut off – weakens after **landfall** (A–R: both true, R explains A).
[19:4:5] # Where they form
[19:4:6] - Belt: **between 30°N and 30°S** (APPSC 2022); mainly **5°–30°** N and S, western parts of oceans; **not** in mid-latitudes and **none at the Equator** (no Coriolis).
[19:4:7] - **None in the South Atlantic and SE Pacific** – UPSC 2015 key: **ITCZ seldom occurs** there; also **cold currents** (Benguela, Humboldt) keep SST low.
[19:4:8] - Leong: typhoons at **6°–20° N and S**, most frequent **July–October**, winds **over 100 mph**, torrential rain with thunder and lightning.
[19:4:9] - Marine heatwaves raise cyclone winds by up to 20% and rain by up to 12% (2026 study).

## SUB 5 | Structure, Rotation and Steering of Cyclones
[19:5:0] # Structure (asked 8 times)
[19:5:1] TABLE head: Part | Feature
  [19:5:1.0] Eye | Calm, clear, sinking air; lowest pressure; higher temperature (warm core); 20–50 km across
  [19:5:1.1] Eyewall | Ring of towering cumulonimbus; strongest winds, heaviest rain
  [19:5:1.2] Spiral rainbands | Curved bands of rain and wind – a mature cyclone shows a spiral pattern
[19:5:2] - Air moves **inward and rises**; rotation **anticlockwise (counter-clockwise) in NH, clockwise in SH** – spin from the **Coriolis effect**.
[19:5:3] - Approach of a cyclone = **falling** barometer.
[19:5:4] - Hurricanes usually **grow larger** poleward, and some are **under 150 km** across (Tracy 1974) – CDS 2010: only "spin from Coriolis" is right.
[19:5:5] - Steering: **trade winds** push tropical cyclones **west**; mature storms **recurve north-east** under the **westerlies**.
[19:5:6] - Threats: high winds, heavy rain, flooding, **storm surge** – abnormal rise of sea level, the **biggest killer**.

[19:5:8] - **Life span:** a tropical cyclone usually lasts about **a week – on average ~6 days** – from formation to dissipation.

[20:0:0] # Biome basics (asked 29 times)
[20:0:1] - **Biome:** a large naturally occurring community of flora and fauna occupying a **major habitat**, shaped mainly by climate (APPSC Panchayat Secretary).
[20:0:2] - **Anthromes** (anthropogenic biomes): units shaped by sustained human use – croplands, villages, cities.
[20:0:3] - Geomorphic factors of plant and animal distribution: **slope angle, slope aspect and relative relief** (CDS 2020).

[20:0:5] # Tropical rainforest – what is asked
[20:0:6] - **Maximum biodiversity** of all biomes (over half of known species) – UPPCS 2012, 2014.
[20:0:7] - Luxuriant growth = **hot, wet climate all year + intense sunlight**; the soils are **not fertile** (leached) and there are **no seasons** (NDA 2015).
[20:0:8] - **Leaching** is the main soil problem (UPPCS 1998); leaf litter **decomposes faster than in any other biome**, so the forest floor is often bare; plants reach the canopy by climbing (lianas) or as **epiphytes** (UPSC 2021).
[20:0:9] - **Amazon** = "**lungs of the planet**"; **Amazon fires, 2019** (Brazil; INPE recorded a sharp rise in fires). **Congo Basin** = 2nd-largest rainforest.
[20:0:10] - Tropical rainforest has the **highest share of the world's forest area** among the forest types (APSC 2017; FAO: tropical forests ≈ 45% of all forest).
[20:0:11] - Equatorial region = **megathermal** (hot all year) with **aerial streamlets** (climbers/epiphytes, water dripping from the canopy); it is **not sclerophyll** and not a region of high development.
[20:0:12] # Taiga and tundra
[20:0:13] - **Taiga = largest land biome**; it has a **very wide** (not narrow) annual temperature range – **APPSC G1** "incorrect statement"; moderate precipitation, snow in winter; acidic **podzol**; lowest productivity of forests; northernmost temperate biome (UPPCS 2016: taiga = **temperate region**).
[20:0:14] - **Tundra:** mosses and lichens, no trees, permafrost; it is wet because **low temperatures make evaporation slow** and permafrost blocks drainage. **Alpine tundra** lies above the tree line.
[20:0:15] - **Tree line:** the altitude/latitude beyond which trees cannot grow. Himalaya: **lower in the west** (about 3,300–3,600 m) than in the east (3,600–3,800 m), because the Eastern Himalaya is **nearer the Equator and the sea** (Bay of Bengal moisture) – UPSC 1995, UPPCS 2005.
[20:0:16] # Adaptations
[20:0:17] - Dry regions: smaller leaves **reduce transpiration** (UPSC 2011). Deciduous trees shed leaves in winter/dry season **to conserve water** (APPSC G1). Desert animals = **xerocoles** (fossorial = burrowing; arboreal = tree-dwelling).

## SUB 6 | Koeppen and Thornthwaite Classification
[20:6:0] - **Wladimir Koeppen**: empirical scheme on **mean temperature and precipitation**, closely linked to **natural vegetation** – "vegetation is the true index of climate" (UPPCS 2018).
[20:6:1] - **Thornthwaite (1931, revised 1948)**: **precipitation effectiveness** and **thermal efficiency**.
[20:6:2] TABLE head: Letter | Group | Criterion
  [20:6:2.0] A | Tropical humid | Coldest month above 18°C
  [20:6:2.1] B | Dry | Evaporation > precipitation (BS steppe, BW desert)
  [20:6:2.2] C | Warm temperate | Coldest month between –3 and 18°C
  [20:6:2.3] D | Cold snow-forest | Coldest month below –3°C
  [20:6:2.4] E | Polar | Warmest month below 10°C (ET tundra, EF ice cap)
  [20:6:2.5] H | Highland | Controlled by altitude
[20:6:3] - Second letters: **f** = no dry season; **m** = monsoon; **w** = dry winter; **s** = dry summer. Third letter **a** = hot summer: **Cfa = humid subtropical** (Af tropical wet, Aw tropical wet and dry, ET tundra).
