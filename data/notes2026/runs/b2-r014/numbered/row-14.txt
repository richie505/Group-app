## SUB 0 | The Five Writs – Literal Meaning and Purpose
[0:0] - **Article 32(2)** names **five writs**: **Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Certiorari, Quo Warranto** (not "Quid Warranto", not injunction). Both the **Supreme Court (Art. 32)** and the **High Courts (Art. 226)** issue **all five**.
[0:1] - Writs began as **prerogative writs of English common law**; before 1950 only the **Calcutta, Bombay and Madras** High Courts could issue them.
[0:2] TABLE head: Writ | Literal meaning | Purpose | Lies against
  [0:2.0] Habeas Corpus | "To have the body (of)" / "you may have the body" | Detained person produced before the court; release from illegal detention; court inquires into the legality of detention | Public authorities and private individuals
  [0:2.1] Mandamus | "We command" (an order) | Orders a public official, body, inferior court or government to perform a public duty it refused or neglected; can also order it not to enforce an unconstitutional law | Public officials, statutory bodies, govt companies, inferior courts, tribunals, government
  [0:2.2] Prohibition | "To forbid" / restrain | Higher court stops a lower court/tribunal exceeding its jurisdiction while the case is pending | Judicial and quasi-judicial bodies only
  [0:2.3] Certiorari | "To be certified" / informed | Higher court calls up the record of a lower court/tribunal for review; quashes an order already passed; also transfers a pending case | Judicial, quasi-judicial and (since 1991) certain administrative bodies
  [0:2.4] Quo Warranto | "By what authority (or warrant)?" / "what is your authority" | Court asks a holder to show his right to a public office; ousts an ineligible person / usurper | Holder of a substantive public office created by statute or the Constitution
[0:3] - **Habeas Corpus** = the writ of **personal liberty** – the "**bulwark of individual liberty**", the **greatest safeguard of personal liberty**; also used to produce a person **missing or in custody**. The term belongs to **law**.
[0:4] - **Mandamus** = Judiciary **orders the Executive to perform its duty**; "an order to act legally and abstain from an unlawful act". **Mandamus directs activity; Prohibition directs inactivity.**
[0:5] - **Certiorari** = the "**postmortem**" writ – examines the working of **subordinate courts**; brought to get an order **quashed**. Grounds: lack/excess of jurisdiction, **error of law on the face of the record**.
[0:6] - **Wrong pairs** asked: "Quo Warranto – certify the proceedings" (certify = Certiorari); "Prohibition – ban on liquor"; "Mandamus – produce the body".
[0:7] TABLE head: Match (APPSC G2 Mains 2025) | Meaning
  [0:7.0] Quo Warranto | ii. by what authority
  [0:7.1] Mandamus | iii. we command
  [0:7.2] Habeas Corpus | iv. to have the body of
  [0:7.3] Certiorari | i. to be certified

## SUB 1 | Heart and Soul – Article 32 as a Fundamental Right
[1:0] - **Right to Constitutional Remedies = Article 32** – the right to **move the Supreme Court directly** when a Fundamental Right is violated (not Arts. 14, 15, 17, 19, 21, 29-31, 33, 34 or 36). It is **itself a Fundamental Right**.
[1:1] - **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar**: Art. 32 is "**the very soul of the Constitution and the very heart of it**" – the Article without which the Constitution would be a **nullity** (not Freedom of Speech, Equality, Religion or Property).
[1:2] - **Why**: "a right without a remedy is a **worthless declaration**" – Art. 32 gives an **effective remedy** against violation of FRs (A-R: A true, R true, R explains A).
[1:3] - "**Soul**" alone is also used for the **Preamble** (Thakur Das Bhargava; Rocket: "key / soul of the Constitution"); with Ambedkar or "heart and soul" the answer is always **Art. 32 / Right to Constitutional Remedies**.
[1:4] - **Basic structure**: judicial review under **Art. 32** (SC) and **Arts. 226 and 227** (HCs) is part of the **inviolable basic structure** (Kesavananda Bharati 1973, 13 judges; **L. Chandra Kumar 1997**); Art. 245 is not.
[1:5] - The power of **judicial review** of the Supreme Court is exercised through **Art. 32** (not 131, 132, 134); the two provisions that most clearly express judicial review = **Arts. 32 and 226**. Judicial review rests on **Arts. 13, 32, 226** – **not Art. 225** (jurisdiction of existing HCs).
[1:6] ~~ Sources: NextGen Polity - APPSC Exams p.73, p.77, p.215-218; PMF Polity Notes Part 1 p.67-68; Rocket Polity Part 1 p.15, p.111
## SUB 2 | Writ Jurisdiction – Article 32 vs Article 226; Guardian of Fundamental Rights
[2:0] - Citizens move the **Supreme Court (Art. 32)** or the **High Court (Art. 226)** for infringement of FRs – **Arts. 32 and 226** (not 33/227, 34/228). **Only the SC and HCs** issue writs – not "all courts", Parliament or the President.
[2:1] - **Article 226** empowers the **High Courts** to issue writs (not 213, 225, 227, 228, 249, 262).
[2:2] - **Guardian / protector of Fundamental Rights = the Judiciary** (Supreme Court as "protector and guarantor") – **not the President**, Parliament, Executive, Cabinet or parties. The **Supreme Court** is also the **custodian / guardian of the Constitution**.
[2:3] - The SC's writ power is **original but not exclusive** – "SC has exclusive power to issue writs" is **false**; HCs also decide the **validity of Central laws** (A false, R true – UPPCS 2019; official key (a) was wrong).
[2:4] TABLE head: Point | Art. 32 (SC) | Art. 226 (HC)
  [2:4.0] Nature | Itself a Fundamental Right – SC cannot refuse when an FR is shown | Constitutional, discretionary remedy
  [2:4.1] Scope | Only Fundamental Rights (narrower) | FRs and "any other purpose" – constitutional and statutory (legal) rights (wider)
  [2:4.2] Territory | Whole of India (wider) | Own territory; beyond it if the cause of action arises inside (15th Amendment, 1963)
  [2:4.3] Writs | Five | Five
[2:5] - So **High Courts are in a better position** to protect citizens' rights, **because the SC issues writs only for FRs** (A and R true, R explains A). The HC writ power covers **constitutional, statutory and fundamental rights** (all).
[2:6] - A right that is **not an FR** – e.g. the **right to vote** – is enforced in the **High Court under Art. 226**, not Art. 32.
[2:7] - **Art. 226 is ordinarily refused** where an **alternative and equally efficacious remedy** exists (rule of prudence; T.R. Verma 1957) or where **disputed questions of fact** need trial; it is **not** barred merely because no FR is involved ("any other purpose").
[2:8] - **Match (APPSC G2 Mains 2025):** **Art. 137** = SC **reviews** its own judgment; **Art. 226** = HCs issue writs; **Art. 13** = SC declares a law **unconstitutional**; **Art. 32** = SC restores FRs by writs.
[2:9] - **Res judicata**: an HC dismissal **on merits** bars the same plea under Art. 32 (remedy: appeal).
[2:10] - **20 Jan 2026**: SC (Justices Dipankar Datta and S.C. Sharma) agreed to examine whether the **Enforcement Directorate** can invoke writ jurisdiction (appeals by Kerala and Tamil Nadu). **16 Jul 2026**: SC studied an **SOP for 24x7 urgent hearings** under Arts. 32 and 226 in threats to **life and liberty**.
[2:11] # Amendments
[2:12] TABLE head: Amendment | Year | Point
  [2:12.0] 42nd | 1976 | Inserted Art. 32A (no challenge to State laws under Art. 32); curbed Art. 226
  [2:12.1] 43rd | 1977 | Omitted Arts. 32A, 131A, 144A, 226A, 228A
  [2:12.2] 44th | 1978 | Restored Art. 226

## SUB 3 | Scope and Limits of Each Writ
[3:0] # Habeas Corpus
[3:1] - The **only writ that lies against private persons** as well as the State; safeguards the liberty of **all persons** in the territory, citizens or not.
[3:2] - Not issued where detention is **lawful**, by a **competent court** (judicial custody), for contempt of court/legislature, or outside the court's territory. A habeas corpus petition is **not dismissed for imperfect pleadings**.
[3:3] - **Wrong statement** (asked twice): "Habeas corpus lies against the executive only, not against private persons".
[3:4] # Mandamus
[3:5] - Lies against **public officials, government, public corporations and government companies** performing public duties, inferior courts and tribunals.
[3:6] - Does **not** lie against: a **private individual or body** (unless entrusted with a **public duty** or **colluding with a public authority**); the **President or Governors** (**Art. 361**); a **contractual** obligation; a **discretionary** duty; non-statutory instructions; a Chief Justice acting judicially.
[3:7] - **UPSC 2022**: A (no mandamus against a private body unless it has a public duty) – correct; B (no mandamus against a government company) – **wrong**; C (any public-minded person may seek Quo Warranto) – correct → **A and C only**.
[3:8] - "Mandamus cannot be issued against the **Government**" is the **incorrect** statement; it can lie against a public servant; it cannot against a private individual.
[3:9] - **Law and policy**: mandamus **cannot compel the Government to make a law or a policy** (e.g. to **appoint a commission to study climate change**), but **enforces a policy once laid down**; it does lie to compel statutory/public duties (filling seats, cleanliness, cyclone shelters). A-R: both true, R explains A.
[3:10] # Prohibition, Certiorari, Quo Warranto
[3:11] - **Prohibition**: only against **judicial and quasi-judicial bodies** – not administrative authorities, legislatures, private persons. It goes to the **lower court**, not to Parliament, an officer or the Government.
[3:12] - **Certiorari**: not against **legislative bodies** or **private persons**; since **1991** also against administrative bodies affecting rights.
[3:13] - **Quo Warranto**: **any public-minded person** may seek it (need not be aggrieved); not for **ministerial or private** office.
[3:14] TABLE head: Prohibition vs Certiorari | Prohibition | Certiorari
  [3:14.0] Stage | While proceedings are pending | After an order is passed (also transfers a pending case)
  [3:14.1] Nature | Preventive only | Preventive and curative
  [3:14.2] Effect | Stops the lower body | Quashes the order ("postmortem")
[3:15] - **Similarity**: both lie (traditionally) **only against judicial and quasi-judicial bodies**; both check excess of jurisdiction; both are issued by SC and HCs.
[3:16] ~~ Sources: NextGen Polity - APPSC Exams p.218-220; PMF Polity Notes Part 1 p.67-69 (UPSC 2022 Q with explanation p.72-73); Rocket Polity Part 1 p.111-112; Constitution of India, Art. 361
## SUB 4 | Clauses of Article 32 – What It Enforces
[4:0] TABLE head: Clause | Provision
  [4:0.0] 32(1) | Right to move the SC by appropriate proceedings for enforcement of Part III rights – itself guaranteed
  [4:0.1] 32(2) | SC may issue directions, orders or writs, including the five writs
  [4:0.2] 32(3) | Parliament may by law empower any other court to exercise these powers within its local limits – no such law made yet
  [4:0.3] 32(4) | The right shall not be suspended except as the Constitution provides (Art. 359)
[4:1] - **Only Fundamental Rights** can be enforced under Art. 32 – not constitutional, statutory or other rights. So the SC can **refuse relief** if **no Fundamental Right has been infringed** – not because another court can give relief, facts are disputed or the wrong writ was named.
[4:2] - **Right to property** is no longer an FR (**44th Amendment, 1978**; now **Art. 300A**) – its violation **cannot** be challenged by a **writ under Art. 32**, only under Art. 226, a civil suit etc.
[4:3] - Both SC and HCs share jurisdiction over **enforcement of Fundamental Rights** (Centre-State and inter-State disputes = SC only, Art. 131).
[4:4] ~~ Sources: NextGen Polity - APPSC Exams p.216-218; PMF Polity Notes Part 1 p.67-68; Rocket Polity Part 1 p.111
## SUB 5 | Suspension during Emergency; Articles 33-35
[5:0] - **Who suspends FRs? The President** – under **Art. 359**, during a National Emergency, by order suspending the right to move courts for named FRs (not Parliament, SC or PM). **Art. 358** suspends Art. 19 automatically (war/external aggression).
[5:1] - **Arts. 20 and 21 cannot be suspended** – added by the **44th Amendment (1978)** to undo **ADM Jabalpur v. Shivkant Shukla (1976)**, which had upheld suspension of habeas corpus/Art. 21 in the Emergency.
[5:2] - Limits on FRs are imposed by **Parliament** by law; the **Judiciary only judges their reasonableness** – "the power to limit FRs is with the Supreme Court" is false.
[5:3] - **Art. 33**: **Parliament** (not State legislatures) may restrict or abrogate FRs of the **armed forces, paramilitary/forces for public order (police), intelligence or counter-intelligence bodies** and persons in **telecommunication systems set up for those forces** – not people of the communication sector in general.
[5:4] - **Art. 34**: restrictions while **martial law** is in force; Parliament may **indemnify** acts done under it. Martial law is not defined in the Constitution.
[5:5] - **Art. 35**: **Parliament alone** legislates on matters under Arts. 16(3), 32(3), 33, 34 and **prescribes punishment for Part III offences** – a **State legislature cannot**.
[5:6] ~~ Sources: NextGen Polity - APPSC Exams p.218, p.223-224; PMF Polity Notes Part 1 p.69-70, p.129 (Arts. 358-359); Constitution of India, Arts. 33-35 (legislative.gov.in)
## SUB 6 | Public Interest Litigation
[6:0] - **No Article mentions PIL** – it is a judge-made tool (borrowed from the **USA**), filed under **Arts. 32 and 226**; PIL is **not itself an FR**.
[6:1] - PIL relaxes **locus standi** (only the aggrieved may sue): any **public-spirited person** may move the court for those unable to – giving the **poor and weak access to justice**, essential for the **rule of law** (A and R true, R explains A).
[6:2] - **Epistolary jurisdiction**: letters/postcards treated as writ petitions – **Sunil Batra** (prisoner's letter). **S.P. Gupta (1981)** – key PIL case; Justices **P.N. Bhagwati** and **V.R. Krishna Iyer**.
[6:3] - Judicial review lets the SC strike down laws that **violate the Fundamental Rights of Scheduled Tribes** and weaker sections; courts do not enact laws.
[6:4] ~~ Sources: NextGen Polity - APPSC Exams p.218-219; PMF Polity Notes Part 1 p.68
