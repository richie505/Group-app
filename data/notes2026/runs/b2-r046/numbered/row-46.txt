## SUB 0 | Seventh Schedule – the Three Lists, Article 246 and Counts
[0:0] # Where the division of powers sits
[0:1] - The **Seventh Schedule** (with **Article 246**, Part XI) distributes **legislative powers/subjects** between the Union and the States in **three Lists** – it reflects the **federal structure**; it was part of the original Constitution (not added later) and has **three** Lists, not two.
[0:2] - Other Schedules are distractors: **Sixth** = tribal areas of the North-East, **Eighth** = languages, **Ninth** = protected laws, **Eleventh** = Panchayats, **Fifth** = Scheduled Areas, **Third** = oaths.
[0:3] TABLE head: List | Who legislates | Original | Now (live entries)
  [0:3.0] I – Union | Parliament – exclusive (Art. 246(1)) | 97 | 98
  [0:3.1] II – State | State Legislature – exclusive "in normal circumstances" (Art. 246(3)) | 66 | 59
  [0:3.2] III – Concurrent | Both Parliament and State Legislatures (Art. 246(2)) | 47 | 52
[0:4] - **Counts settled:** Laxmikanth 8th ed. gives **98 / 59 / 52** (entries now in force); NextGen gives **100 / 61 / 52** (it counts inserted numbers such as 2A, 92A, 92B and not all omissions). Both agree on **Concurrent = 52 (originally 47)**; old exam keys "47" and "97" are the **original** figures.
[0:5] - The **Union List has more subjects than the State List** – one reason the Centre is called strong.
[0:6] # Conflicts between Lists and laws
[0:7] - Precedence: **Union List > Concurrent List > State List** when entries overlap.
[0:8] - Concurrent List conflict (**Art. 254**): the **Union law prevails** – but **not always**: a State law **reserved for and assented to by the President** prevails in that State (Art. 254(2)), until Parliament legislates again.
[0:9] - So "Union law **always** prevails" is **false**, and "**only States** can legislate on the Concurrent List" is **false**; under special circumstances (Arts 249, 250, 252, 253, 356) Parliament **can** legislate on the State List.
[0:10] - **Central sector schemes** are mostly framed on Union List subjects.
[0:11] - Entry numbering still ends at 97 / 66 / 47 because entries were inserted (e.g. **Union Entry 92A**, inter-State sales tax, by the **6th Amendment, 1956**) or omitted.

## SUB 2 | Federal vs Unitary Features
[2:0] # Classification
[2:1] TABLE head: Federal features | Unitary (non-federal) features
  [2:1.0] Dual polity – two sets of governments | Strong Centre – more and weightier Union subjects, Centre's overriding Concurrent power, residuary power with Centre
  [2:1.1] Written Constitution | Single Constitution for Union and States (only ex-J&K had its own)
  [2:1.2] Division of powers (Seventh Schedule) | Flexibility – most provisions amended by Parliament alone; States not indestructible (Art. 3)
  [2:1.3] Supremacy of the Constitution | Unequal representation of States in the Rajya Sabha
  [2:1.4] Rigid Constitution – federal provisions need ratification by half the States | Emergency provisions
  [2:1.5] Independent judiciary | Single citizenship; integrated judiciary
  [2:1.6] Bicameralism – Rajya Sabha represents the States | All India Services; integrated audit (CAG); integrated election machinery
  [2:1.7]  | Appointment of Governor by the Centre; veto over State Bills (President's approval); Parliament's authority over the State List (Art. 249)
[2:2] - **Independence of the judiciary** is the **essential** federal feature (UPSC 2021) – it interprets the Constitution and settles Centre-State disputes.
[2:3] - India is federal because of the **distribution of powers** between the Union and the States (UPSC 1994); the **Rajya Sabha** signifies the **federal structure**.
[2:4] - "Not a federal feature" answers: **Office of the Governor** (APPSC G1 2018), **CAG** (APPSC G2), "**result of an agreement among the federating units**" (UPSC 2017).
[2:5] - The **Rajya Sabha's unequal representation** is counted as a unitary feature by Laxmikanth, but UPSC 2017 listed it among the features of Indian federalism – it is never the "not a feature" answer.
[2:6] - **Violating federal principles:** **Governor, CAG, Emergency powers (esp. Art. 356)**; **Central Services** serve only the Union and are not a violation – it is the **All India Services** (Art. 312) that Laxmikanth says "violate the principle of federalism".
[2:7] - **Not violative of federalism:** the **three-List division** and **residuary power with Parliament** (Canadian model); President taking over a State under emergency **is** violative.
[2:8] - Unitary features in a code: **Appointment of Governor, All India Services, President's approval on State Bills, constitutional crisis (emergency)**; written/rigid Constitution, supremacy, decentralisation are federal.
[2:9] - **Governor** is appointed by the President (**Arts 155-156**); **All India Services** (**Art. 312**) are recruited through the **UPSC** and controlled by the Centre (Art. 312 has nothing to do with the Concurrent List).
[2:10] - **Constitutional amendment affecting the federal structure** needs ratification by **at least half the States** (Art. 368(2) proviso).
[2:11] # Borrowed features linked to federalism
[2:12] TABLE head: Feature | Source
  [2:12.0] Federation with a strong Centre, residuary powers with Centre, Governor appointed by the Centre, "Union" | Canada
  [2:12.1] Concurrent List | Australia
  [2:12.2] Federal scheme, Office of Governor, emergency provisions, judiciary | GoI Act, 1935
  [2:12.3] Suspension of FRs during emergency | Weimar (Germany)
  [2:12.4] Single citizenship, parliamentary system | UK
[2:13] - The Indian federal system is based on the **Canadian model**, not the American (formation by disintegration, the term "Union", centralising tendency).

## SUB 4 | India – a "Union of States" (Article 1)
[4:0] - **Art. 1(1):** "**India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States**" – the Constitution's names for the country are **India and Bharat** only (not Hindustan, Bharatvarsh).
[4:1] - The word "**Federation**" is **nowhere** used in the Constitution; India is described neither as a federation nor as a confederation.
[4:2] - "**Union of States**" is taken from the **British North America Act, 1867** (Canada).
[4:3] - **Ambedkar's two reasons** for "Union": (i) the federation is **not the result of an agreement** among the States (unlike the USA); (ii) the States have **no right to secede** – the Union is **indestructible**.
[4:4] - The Union of India is an **indissoluble Union of States**; it is not formed at the will of States and cannot be dissolved by Parliament, State Assemblies or plebiscite.
[4:5] - **Indestructible Union of destructible States:** Parliament can alter the area, boundaries or name of any State **without its consent** by a **simple majority** (Art. 3); USA = indestructible Union of indestructible States.
[4:6] - **Secession:** the Constitution gives **no right of self-determination/secession** to any region; advocacy of secession is **not protected** by free speech – the **16th Amendment (1963)** allows restrictions in the interest of **sovereignty and integrity of India**.
[4:7] - Territory of India = States + UTs + acquired territories; "**Union of India**" = the **States** only; both listed in the **First Schedule**.
[4:8] - **Art. 2** = admission/establishment of new States not part of India; laws under Arts 2-3 that change the First and Fourth Schedules are **not** Art. 368 amendments (Art. 4); an Art. 3 Bill needs the **President's recommendation**.

## SUB 5 | Federalism – Meaning, Types and Federal Countries
[5:0] - **Federalism:** two sets of governments **co-exist** with a **constitutional distribution of powers**, under a **written Constitution**, with an independent judiciary as umpire.
[5:1] - **Unitary government:** one supreme central government; local units are its creations with delegated powers; **no constitutional division of powers**.
[5:2] - **Confederation:** a loose league of **sovereign States** that keep the **right to secede**.
[5:3] - A **federal polity** involves Union-State relations, inter-State relations, mechanisms for **cooperation** and mechanisms for **resolving disputes**.
[5:4] # Unitary government – merits and demerits
[5:5] TABLE head: Merits | Demerits
  [5:5.0] Quick (swift) decisions, uniformity of laws, flexibility | Over-centralisation; local needs neglected
  [5:5.1] Strong state – firm crisis response | Not suited to large, diverse countries
[5:6] - "**Ideal for large countries**" is NOT a characteristic of unitary government; adaptability, wide participation and less authoritarianism are federal/democratic merits.
[5:7] - **Power sharing** is justified because it reduces conflict, prevents majority imposition and consults all affected – it does **not** speed up decision-making.
[5:8] # Coming-together vs holding-together
[5:9] TABLE head: Type | Meaning | Examples
  [5:9.0] Coming-together | Independent States pool sovereignty | USA, Switzerland, Australia
  [5:9.1] Holding-together | A large country divides power; stronger Centre | India, Spain, Belgium
[5:10] # Federal and unitary countries
[5:11] TABLE head: Federal | Unitary
  [5:11.0] USA, Canada, Australia, Argentina, Switzerland, Germany, Russia, Brazil, Belgium (since 1993) | Britain (UK), France, Italy, Japan, China, Cuba, Sri Lanka

## SUB 6 | 42nd Amendment Transfers and Residuary Powers
[6:0] # 42nd Amendment, 1976 – State List → Concurrent List
[6:1] - **Five** subjects moved: **education**, **forests**, **weights and measures**, **protection of wild animals and birds**, **administration of justice** (constitution and organisation of all courts except the SC and HCs).
[6:2] - Inserted **Entry 20A – population control and family planning**.
[6:3] - **Education** was originally a **State** subject; it went to the **Concurrent List (Entry 25)** by the **42nd Amendment** – not the 24th, 25th or 44th.
[6:4] - **Public order, police, agriculture** were **not** transferred.
[6:5] - Union use of Concurrent entries is debated – **farm laws** (Entry 33), **NEP** and **NMC Act** (education).
[6:6] # Residuary powers – Article 248
[6:7] - **Residuary powers** = matters **not enumerated in any of the three Lists**; Parliament has **exclusive** power over them (**Art. 248** and **Union Entry 97**), including **residuary taxes** (gift tax, wealth tax, expenditure tax).
[6:8] - Art. 248 is in **Part XI** (Chapter I, Legislative Relations); the **101st Amendment, 2016** made it "subject to **Article 246A**" (GST).
[6:9] TABLE head: Model | Residuary powers with
  [6:9.0] India, Canada | Centre
  [6:9.1] USA, Australia | States
  [6:9.2] GoI Act, 1935 | Governor-General
[6:10] - Former **J&K** (Art. 370): residuary powers lay with the **State** until August 2019.

## SUB 7 | Switching to Unitary Mode – Emergency and Overriding Powers
[7:0] - During an **emergency** the Centre becomes all-powerful and the federal structure **converts into a unitary one without a formal amendment** – unique among federations; so the Indian polity is described as **unitary** under the Emergency provisions.
[7:1] - Only during a **National Emergency (Art. 352)** – declared in **any part** of the country – does the Union assume **full legislative and executive control**; President's Rule covers one State only, and ordinances or martial law (Art. 34) do not.
[7:2] - Emergency provisions are in **Part XVIII (Arts 352-360)**; **three** kinds: National (352), State/President's Rule (356), Financial (360).
[7:3] # Overriding powers
[7:4] TABLE head: Article | Power
  [7:4.0] 249 | Rajya Sabha resolution (2/3 present and voting) in national interest → Parliament legislates on a State subject (1 year, renewable)
  [7:4.1] 250 | During National Emergency Parliament legislates on the State List; laws lapse 6 months after it ends
  [7:4.2] 252 | Two or more States request Parliament to legislate
  [7:4.3] 253 | Laws to implement international agreements
  [7:4.4] 354 | President may modify revenue distribution during a National Emergency – the order lasts till the end of the financial year in which the Emergency ends (not six months)
[7:5] # Emergencies compared
[7:6] TABLE head: Basis | 352 National | 356 President's Rule | 360 Financial
  [7:6.0] Ground | War, external aggression, armed rebellion (44th replaced "internal disturbance") | Failure of constitutional machinery | Threat to financial stability
  [7:6.1] Approval | 1 month, special majority | 2 months, simple | 2 months, simple
  [7:6.2] Duration | 6 months at a time, no maximum | 6 months at a time, max 3 years (beyond 1 year: National Emergency + EC certificate) | Until revoked
  [7:6.3] Used | 3 times – 1962, 1971, 1975 | Many times (AP 1973, Jai Andhra) | Never
[7:7] - **44th Amendment (1978):** Cabinet's **written advice**; **1/10th** of Lok Sabha members can seek a sitting to disapprove; **Arts 20-21** never suspended; **Art. 19** suspended automatically only in war/external aggression; **38th Amendment** had barred judicial review.
[7:8] - During a National Emergency Parliament can extend the **Lok Sabha/Assembly term by 1 year at a time**, at most **6 months** after it ends; a proclamation survives **30 days** after a new Lok Sabha's first sitting if the old one was dissolved.
[7:9] - **Only a National Emergency** affects Fundamental Rights; President's Rule and Financial Emergency do not.
[7:10] - **Financial Emergency:** financial directions, **salary cuts** (incl. judges), State **Money Bills reserved** for the President.
[7:11] - **1975 Emergency:** 25 June 1975 – **Samvidhan Hatya Diwas (25 June)**; withdrawn **21 March 1977**.
