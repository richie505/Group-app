=== Seventh Schedule – the Three Lists, Article 246 and Counts
# Where the division of powers sits
- The **Seventh Schedule** (with **Article 246**, Part XI) distributes **legislative powers/subjects** between the Union and the States in **three Lists** – it reflects the **federal structure**; it was part of the original Constitution (not added later) and has **three** Lists, not two.
- Other Schedules are distractors: **Sixth** = tribal areas of the North-East, **Eighth** = languages, **Ninth** = protected laws, **Eleventh** = Panchayats, **Fifth** = Scheduled Areas, **Third** = oaths.
| List | Who legislates | Original | Now (live entries) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **I – Union** | **Parliament – exclusive** (Art. 246(1)) | **97** | **98** |
| **II – State** | **State Legislature – exclusive "in normal circumstances"** (Art. 246(3)) | **66** | **59** |
| **III – Concurrent** | **Both** Parliament and State Legislatures (Art. 246(2)) | **47** | **52** |
- **Counts settled:** Laxmikanth 8th ed. gives **98 / 59 / 52** (entries now in force); NextGen gives **100 / 61 / 52** (it counts inserted numbers such as 2A, 92A, 92B and not all omissions). Both agree on **Concurrent = 52 (originally 47)**; old exam keys "47" and "97" are the **original** figures.
- The **Union List has more subjects than the State List** – one reason the Centre is called strong.
# Conflicts between Lists and laws
- Precedence: **Union List > Concurrent List > State List** when entries overlap.
- Concurrent List conflict (**Art. 254**): the **Union law prevails** – but **not always**: a State law **reserved for and assented to by the President** prevails in that State (Art. 254(2)), until Parliament legislates again.
- So "Union law **always** prevails" is **false**, and "**only States** can legislate on the Concurrent List" is **false**; under special circumstances (Arts 249, 250, 252, 253, 356) Parliament **can** legislate on the State List.
- **Central sector schemes** are mostly framed on Union List subjects.
- Entry numbering still ends at 97 / 66 / 47 because entries were inserted (e.g. **Union Entry 92A**, inter-State sales tax, by the **6th Amendment, 1956**) or omitted.

=== Federal vs Unitary Features
# Classification
| Federal features | Unitary (non-federal) features |
|---|---|
| **Dual polity** – two sets of governments | **Strong Centre** – more and weightier Union subjects, Centre's overriding Concurrent power, residuary power with Centre |
| **Written Constitution** | **Single Constitution** for Union and States (only ex-J&K had its own) |
| **Division of powers** (Seventh Schedule) | **Flexibility** – most provisions amended by Parliament alone; States not indestructible (Art. 3) |
| **Supremacy of the Constitution** | **Unequal representation** of States in the Rajya Sabha |
| **Rigid Constitution** – federal provisions need **ratification by half the States** | **Emergency provisions** |
| **Independent judiciary** | **Single citizenship**; **integrated judiciary** |
| **Bicameralism** – Rajya Sabha represents the States | **All India Services**; **integrated audit (CAG)**; **integrated election machinery** |
| | **Appointment of Governor** by the Centre; **veto over State Bills** (President's approval); Parliament's authority over the State List (Art. 249) |
- **Independence of the judiciary** is the **essential** federal feature (UPSC 2021) – it interprets the Constitution and settles Centre-State disputes.
- India is federal because of the **distribution of powers** between the Union and the States (UPSC 1994); the **Rajya Sabha** signifies the **federal structure**.
- "Not a federal feature" answers: **Office of the Governor** (APPSC G1 2018), **CAG** (APPSC G2), "**result of an agreement among the federating units**" (UPSC 2017).
- The **Rajya Sabha's unequal representation** is counted as a unitary feature by Laxmikanth, but UPSC 2017 listed it among the features of Indian federalism – it is never the "not a feature" answer.
- **Violating federal principles:** **Governor, CAG, Emergency powers (esp. Art. 356)**; **Central Services** serve only the Union and are not a violation – it is the **All India Services** (Art. 312) that Laxmikanth says "violate the principle of federalism".
- **Not violative of federalism:** the **three-List division** and **residuary power with Parliament** (Canadian model); President taking over a State under emergency **is** violative.
- Unitary features in a code: **Appointment of Governor, All India Services, President's approval on State Bills, constitutional crisis (emergency)**; written/rigid Constitution, supremacy, decentralisation are federal.
- **Governor** is appointed by the President (**Arts 155-156**); **All India Services** (**Art. 312**) are recruited through the **UPSC** and controlled by the Centre (Art. 312 has nothing to do with the Concurrent List).
- **Constitutional amendment affecting the federal structure** needs ratification by **at least half the States** (Art. 368(2) proviso).

=== India – a "Union of States" (Article 1)
- **Art. 1(1):** "**India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States**" – the Constitution's names for the country are **India and Bharat** only (not Hindustan, Bharatvarsh).
- The word "**Federation**" is **nowhere** used in the Constitution; India is described neither as a federation nor as a confederation.
- "**Union of States**" is taken from the **British North America Act, 1867** (Canada).
- **Ambedkar's two reasons** for "Union": (i) the federation is **not the result of an agreement** among the States (unlike the USA); (ii) the States have **no right to secede** – the Union is **indestructible**.
- The Union of India is an **indissoluble Union of States**; it is not formed at the will of States and cannot be dissolved by Parliament, State Assemblies or plebiscite.
- **Indestructible Union of destructible States:** Parliament can alter the area, boundaries or name of any State **without its consent** by a **simple majority** (Art. 3); USA = indestructible Union of indestructible States.
- **Secession:** the Constitution gives **no right of self-determination/secession** to any region; advocacy of secession is **not protected** by free speech – the **16th Amendment (1963)** allows restrictions in the interest of **sovereignty and integrity of India**.
- Territory of India = States + UTs + acquired territories; "**Union of India**" = the **States** only; both listed in the **First Schedule**.
- **Art. 2** = admission/establishment of new States not part of India; laws under Arts 2-3 that change the First and Fourth Schedules are **not** Art. 368 amendments (Art. 4); an Art. 3 Bill needs the **President's recommendation**.

=== Federalism – Meaning, Types and Federal Countries
- **Federalism:** two sets of governments **co-exist** with a **constitutional distribution of powers**, under a **written Constitution**, with an independent judiciary as umpire.
- **Unitary government:** one supreme central government; local units are its creations with delegated powers; **no constitutional division of powers**.
- **Confederation:** a loose league of **sovereign States** that keep the **right to secede**.
- A **federal polity** involves Union-State relations, inter-State relations, mechanisms for **cooperation** and mechanisms for **resolving disputes**.
# Unitary government – merits and demerits
| Merits | Demerits |
|---|---|
| **Quick (swift) decisions**, **uniformity of laws**, flexibility | Over-centralisation; local needs neglected |
| **Strong state** – firm crisis response | **Not suited to large, diverse countries** |
- "**Ideal for large countries**" is NOT a characteristic of unitary government; adaptability, wide participation and less authoritarianism are federal/democratic merits.
- **Power sharing** is justified because it reduces conflict, prevents majority imposition and consults all affected – it does **not** speed up decision-making.
# Coming-together vs holding-together
| Type | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Coming-together | Independent States pool sovereignty | **USA, Switzerland, Australia** |
| Holding-together | A large country divides power; stronger Centre | **India, Spain, Belgium** |
# Federal and unitary countries

| **USA**, Canada, Australia, **Argentina**, Switzerland, Germany, Russia, Brazil, **Belgium** (since 1993) | **Britain (UK)**, **France**, **Italy**, Japan, **China**, **Cuba**, **Sri Lanka** |

=== 42nd Amendment Transfers and Residuary Powers
# 42nd Amendment, 1976 – State List → Concurrent List
- **Five** subjects moved: **education**, **forests**, **weights and measures**, **protection of wild animals and birds**, **administration of justice** (constitution and organisation of all courts except the SC and HCs).
- Inserted **Entry 20A – population control and family planning**.
- **Education** was originally a **State** subject; it went to the **Concurrent List (Entry 25)** by the **42nd Amendment** – not the 24th, 25th or 44th.
- **Public order, police, agriculture** were **not** transferred.
- Union use of Concurrent entries is debated – **farm laws** (Entry 33), **NEP** and **NMC Act** (education).
# Residuary powers – Article 248
- **Residuary powers** = matters **not enumerated in any of the three Lists**; Parliament has **exclusive** power over them (**Art. 248** and **Union Entry 97**), including **residuary taxes** (gift tax, wealth tax, expenditure tax).
- Art. 248 is in **Part XI** (Chapter I, Legislative Relations); the **101st Amendment, 2016** made it "subject to **Article 246A**" (GST).
| Model | Residuary powers with |
|---|---|
| **India, Canada** | **Centre** |
| **USA, Australia** | **States** |
| **GoI Act, 1935** | **Governor-General** |
- Former **J&K** (Art. 370): residuary powers lay with the **State** until August 2019.

=== Switching to Unitary Mode – Emergency and Overriding Powers
- During an **emergency** the Centre becomes all-powerful and the federal structure **converts into a unitary one without a formal amendment** – unique among federations; so the Indian polity is described as **unitary** under the Emergency provisions.
- Only during a **National Emergency (Art. 352)** – declared in **any part** of the country – does the Union assume **full legislative and executive control**; President's Rule covers one State only, and ordinances or martial law (Art. 34) do not.
- Emergency provisions are in **Part XVIII (Arts 352-360)**; **three** kinds: National (352), State/President's Rule (356), Financial (360).
# Overriding powers
| Article | Power |
|---|---|
| 249 | **Rajya Sabha** resolution (**2/3 present and voting**) in national interest → Parliament legislates on a State subject (1 year, renewable) |
| 250 | During National Emergency Parliament legislates on the State List; laws lapse **6 months** after it ends |
| 252 | **Two or more States** request Parliament to legislate |
| 253 | Laws to implement **international agreements** |
| 354 | President may **modify revenue distribution** during a National Emergency – the order lasts till the **end of the financial year** in which the Emergency ends (not six months) |
# Emergencies compared
| Basis | 352 National | 356 President's Rule | 360 Financial |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ground | War, external aggression, **armed rebellion** (44th replaced "internal disturbance") | Failure of constitutional machinery | Threat to financial stability |
| Approval | **1 month**, special majority | **2 months**, simple | 2 months, simple |
| Duration | 6 months at a time, no maximum | 6 months at a time, **max 3 years** (beyond 1 year: National Emergency + EC certificate) | Until revoked |
| Used | **3 times – 1962, 1971, 1975** | Many times (AP **1973**, Jai Andhra) | **Never** |
- **44th Amendment (1978):** Cabinet's **written advice**; **1/10th** of Lok Sabha members can seek a sitting to disapprove; **Arts 20-21** never suspended; **Art. 19** suspended automatically only in war/external aggression; **38th Amendment** had barred judicial review.
- During a National Emergency Parliament can extend the **Lok Sabha/Assembly term by 1 year at a time**, at most **6 months** after it ends; a proclamation survives **30 days** after a new Lok Sabha's first sitting if the old one was dissolved.
- **Only a National Emergency** affects Fundamental Rights; President's Rule and Financial Emergency do not.
- **Financial Emergency:** financial directions, **salary cuts** (incl. judges), State **Money Bills reserved** for the President.
- **1975 Emergency:** 25 June 1975 – **Samvidhan Hatya Diwas (25 June)**; withdrawn **21 March 1977**.

=== Courts, Rajya Sabha and Asymmetric Federalism
# Supreme Court on federalism
| Case | Holding |
|---|---|
| **State of West Bengal v Union of India (1962)** | Not truly federal; States not sovereign |
| **Kesavananda Bharati (1973)** | Federal character is a **basic feature** |
| **S.R. Bommai (1994)** | Federalism = **basic structure**; Art. 356 open to **judicial review** |
| **MADA v SAIL (July 2024, 9 judges, 8:1)** | **Royalty is not a tax**; States can tax mineral rights |
- **Judicial review** (incl. of **executive orders**) reinforces the federal principle; it does not question policy wisdom.
# Representation and amendment
- Rajya Sabha seats: **Fourth Schedule**, by population (**UP 31**); US Senate **2 per State**.
- Parliamentary form: Council of Ministers **collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha** (Art. 75(3)); the Constitution is **neither wholly rigid nor wholly flexible**.
- **Article 368** is in **Part XX**; federal provisions need ratification by **half the State Legislatures**.
- **India vs USA:** India – single citizenship, integrated judiciary, three Lists, residue with Centre; USA – dual citizenship, dual courts, residue with States; both have a written Constitution, division of powers and a federal Supreme Court.
