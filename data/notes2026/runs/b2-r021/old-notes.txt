=== NOTES 2:21 | Basic Structure Doctrine – Kesavananda Bharati and After
## SUB 0 | Meaning and Nature of the Doctrine
[21:0:0] - Parliament may amend **any part** of the Constitution under **Article 368**, including Fundamental Rights and the Preamble.
[21:0:1] - But it **cannot alter, damage or destroy its core features** – the features that give the Constitution its identity.
[21:0:2] - Basic structure = what **cannot be amended** in exercise of the power under Art. 368 (APPSC G1 2018 key).
[21:0:3] - **Judge-made**: nowhere defined in the text; not added by any Act of Parliament or amendment.
[21:0:4] - **Not exhaustive**: the Supreme Court has never listed it fully; the list grows **case by case**.
[21:0:5] - Source of the theory = **judicial interpretation**.
[21:0:6] - Some features are so essential that they cannot be abrogated (Kesavananda Bharati, 1973; approved in **Minerva Mills**, 1980).
[21:0:7] - Fundamental Rights **can** still be amended, provided the basic structure is not damaged.
[21:0:8] "Basic Structure is explicitly defined in Part XX" = FALSE. "Incorporated by an Act of Parliament" = FALSE (APPSC G1 2024). It is not "the Preamble alone".
[21:0:9] "Fundamental Rights can never be amended after Kesavananda" = FALSE.

## SUB 2 | Evolution – Chronology of Cases
[21:2:0] # Before Kesavananda
[21:2:1] TABLE head: Year | Case / event | Contribution
  [21:2:1.0] 1951 | Shankari Prasad | Art. 368 includes power to amend FRs; an amendment is not "law" under Art. 13
  [21:2:1.1] 1965 | Sajjan Singh | Upheld the 17th Amendment; reaffirmed Shankari Prasad; Mudholkar dissent
  [21:2:1.2] 1967 | Golaknath | Challenged the 17th Amendment (not the 24th); overruled both earlier cases; FRs "transcendental and immutable"; amendment is "law" under Art. 13; Parliament cannot abridge FRs; 11 judges, 6:5; first used prospective overruling
  [21:2:1.3] 1971 | 24th Amendment | Parliament's reply – amended Arts. 13 and 368
[21:2:2] # Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973)
[21:2:3] - Decided **24 April 1973**; **13 judges** (largest bench ever); majority **7:6**; CJI S.M. Sikri.
[21:2:4] - **First propounded** the basic structure doctrine; **overruled Golaknath**; **upheld the 24th Amendment**.
[21:2:5] - Held the **Preamble** is part of the Constitution and amendable, subject to the basic structure.
[21:2:6] - Struck down the part of **Art. 31C** that stopped courts from asking whether a law carries out the DPSPs → **judicial review is basic**.
[21:2:7] # After Kesavananda
[21:2:8] TABLE head: Year | Case | Contribution
  [21:2:8.0] 1975 | Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain | First application; struck down the 39th Amendment clause (Art. 329A(4)) putting election disputes of PM (and President, VP, Speaker) beyond courts; free and fair elections basic
  [21:2:8.1] 1980 | Minerva Mills | Struck down 42nd Amendment's Art. 368(4), (5) and the widened Art. 31C; judicial review, limited amending power, FR–DPSP harmony
  [21:2:8.2] 1981 | Waman Rao | Doctrine applies from 24 April 1973 – cut-off for Ninth Schedule / Art. 31A laws
  [21:2:8.3] 1994 | S.R. Bommai | Art. 356 misuse; nine-judge bench; secularism and federalism basic
  [21:2:8.4] 2007 | I.R. Coelho | Nine judges; Ninth Schedule laws after 24 April 1973 reviewable
  [21:2:8.5] 2015 | NJAC case (SC Advocates-on-Record Assn.) | 99th Amendment (2014) and NJAC Act struck down, 4:1; judicial independence basic
[21:2:9] First propounded = **Kesavananda Bharati (1973)**, not Golaknath or Shankari Prasad (MPPSC 2025). First LIMITED FRs amendment power = **Golaknath (1967)**. First APPLIED = **Indira Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975)**.
[21:2:10] Benches: Golaknath 11 judges, 6:5 | Kesavananda 13 judges, 7:6 | NJAC 4:1 | Bommai and Coelho 9 judges

## SUB 5 | Ninth Schedule and Articles 31A, 31B, 31C
[21:5:0] # Origin and purpose
[21:5:1] - Created by the **1st Amendment (1951)** with **Art. 31B** (and 31A); assent by President **Rajendra Prasad**.
[21:5:2] - 1st Amendment also added **Art. 15(4)** and the grounds of public order, friendly relations with foreign States and incitement to an offence to **Art. 19(2)**.
[21:5:3] - Purpose: protect **land-reform / zamindari-abolition** laws from challenge for violating FRs.
[21:5:4] - Covers **both Central and State** laws.
[21:5:5] - Entries made **only by Parliament through a constitutional amendment**.
[21:5:6] - Example: **76th Amendment, 1994** placed **Tamil Nadu's 69% reservation** Act.
[21:5:7] - **13 Acts** at the start → **282** (more than 280) now.
[21:5:8] # Judicial review of entries
[21:5:9] TABLE head: Case | Year | Rule
  [21:5:9.0] Kesavananda Bharati | 1973 | Amendments, including inclusions, cannot destroy the basic structure
  [21:5:9.1] Waman Rao | 1981 | Cut-off 24 April 1973 (date of Kesavananda); later entries open to challenge
  [21:5:9.2] I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu | 2007 | No blanket immunity; "rights test / direct impact test" – post-1973 law invalid if it damages basic structure or abrogates the essence of Arts. 14, 19, 21
[21:5:10] - Sequence: **First Amendment (1951) → Kesavananda (1973) → Waman Rao (1981) → I.R. Coelho (2007)**.
[21:5:11] - I.R. Coelho = "**9th Schedule case**".
[21:5:12] # Saving articles
[21:5:13] TABLE head: Article | Saves | Inserted by
  [21:5:13.0] 31A | Estate acquisition, agrarian reform laws from Arts. 14, 19 | 1st Amendment, 1951

  [21:5:13.2] 31C | Laws implementing Art. 39(b), (c) from Arts. 14, 19 | 25th Amendment, 1971 (not the First); widened by 42nd

## SUB 7 | Supremacy Debate and Criticism
[21:7:0] - **Judicial review** = inherent power of constitutional courts to check laws and executive action; accepts **supremacy of the Constitution and courts** (APPSC G2 2017 key).
[21:7:1] - Basic structure rests on **supremacy of the Constitution**.
[21:7:2] - Indian Parliament is **not sovereign in the British sense**: written supreme Constitution, FRs, judicial review, federal division, limited amending power.
[21:7:3] - High Courts can void central laws: the **42nd Amendment's bar removed by the 43rd (1977)**.
[21:7:4] TABLE head: View | Argument
  [21:7:4.0] Criticism | No textual basis; "judicial rewriting" by unelected judges; open-ended, uncertain list; "judicial supremacy"
  [21:7:4.1] Defence | Shield against majorities; checks misuse of Art. 368; major contribution to constitutionalism
[21:7:5] # Latest developments
[21:7:6] - **18 June 2026**: 75 years of the 1st Amendment (Ninth Schedule); I.R. Coelho limit restated.
[21:7:7] - **2026**: free and fair elections as basic feature cited in debate on making **voting** more than a statutory right.
[21:7:8] - **129th Amendment Bill, 2024** (simultaneous polls), JPC: ex-CJI **B.R. Gavai** – no violation of basic structure; ex-CJIs **U.U. Lalit** and **Sanjiv Khanna** raised doubts.

=== NOTES 2:23 | Doctrines of Constitutional Interpretation
## SUB 0 | Federal Doctrines – Meaning and Field
[23:0:0] TABLE head: Doctrine | Meaning | Field
  [23:0:0.0] Pith and substance | Judge a law by its true nature; incidental trespass is fine | Union–State lists
  [23:0:0.1] Colourable legislation | What cannot be done directly cannot be done indirectly; "fraud on the Constitution" | Competence, not motive
  [23:0:0.2] Territorial nexus | State law valid outside the State if there is a sufficient nexus | Extra-territorial operation
  [23:0:0.3] Harmonious construction | Read conflicting provisions so both operate | FR vs DPSP; provision vs provision
  [23:0:0.4] Incidental and ancillary | An entry covers matters incidental to it; entries get the widest meaning | Entries
  [23:0:0.5] Parens patriae | State as legal protector of citizens unable to protect themselves | Welfare
[23:0:1] - Reconciling conflicting Central and Provincial laws on the same matter = **federal paramountcy** (Art. 254; APPSC G2 2017); **pith and substance** only decides which List a law falls in.
[23:0:2] - True character is read from the **whole Act, its object and its effect**, not just its title.

[23:3:0] - **Concurrent List**: Parliament's law prevails over a repugnant State law.

=== NOTES 2:24 | Separation of Powers and Checks and Balances
## SUB 0 | Constitutional Government
[24:0:0] - **Constitutional government = limited government**, limited by the terms of the Constitution (UPSC 2020, 2021); it defines, restrains and checks power to stop **executive tyranny**.
[24:0:1] - Best safeguard of liberty in a liberal democracy = **separation of powers** (UPSC 2021).
[24:0:2] - **Constitutionalism**: limits + rule of law + checks and balances + judicial review + FRs.
[24:0:3] - A **majoritarian** position cannot override constitutionalism.
[24:0:4] - **PIL** widens access to justice for the poor but is **not indispensable** for the rule of law, which existed before PIL via **Arts. 14, 32, 226**.
## SUB 1 | Montesquieu and the Indian Degree
[24:1:0] - **Montesquieu**: division of powers best protects liberty; "**The Spirit of Laws**" (**1748**); drew on the British constitution.
[24:1:1] - Parliamentary system = **fusion**: executive drawn from and accountable to the legislature; ministers must be legislators. Presidential = separation.
[24:1:2] - **Nominal head** President; **real executive** Council of Ministers.
[24:1:3] - **Ambedkar** preferred **responsibility over stability**; reasons: familiarity (1919/1935 Acts), diversity, avoid American-style conflict.
[24:1:4] - India: **no rigid separation**; implicit, not absolute; overlapping functions → checks and balances.
[24:1:5] - **Art. 50 (DPSP)**: separate judiciary from executive in public services. Judiciary insulated: **Arts. 50, 121, 211, 235**.
[24:1:6] - **Munro's Madras Regulations (1816):** reversed the Cornwallis separation – the **Collector** again got **magisterial and police powers**, and village heads got petty police and judicial powers.
