## SUB 0 | Part III at a Glance – Six Groups, Number, Location
[0:0] # Where and how many
[0:1] - **Location:** **Part III, Articles 12–35** of the Constitution; called the **"Magna Carta of India"** (not Part I, II or IV; not Arts. 14–32 only).
[0:2] - **Number:** originally **seven**, now **six** Fundamental Rights (groups) – the **Right to Property (Art. 31)** was deleted by the **44th Amendment, 1978** (Janata Party government) and is now a **legal/constitutional right under Art. 300A (Part XII)**.
[0:3] - **Borrowed from:** the **Bill of Rights of the USA**; the **Motilal Nehru Report (1928)** had demanded a bill of rights.
[0:4] - **Why "fundamental":** they are **guaranteed and specifically protected by the Constitution**, the fundamental law of the land – not rights granted by any ordinary law, nor merely natural-law rights.
[0:5] # The six groups
[0:6] TABLE head: Group | Articles | Contents
  [0:6.0] Right to Equality | 14–18 | 14 equality before law; 15 no discrimination (religion, race, caste, sex, place of birth); 16 public employment; 17 untouchability abolished; 18 titles abolished
  [0:6.1] Right to Freedom | 19–22 | 19 six freedoms (speech, assembly, association, movement, residence, profession); 20 conviction; 21 life and personal liberty; 21A education; 22 arrest and detention
  [0:6.2] Right against Exploitation | 23–24 | 23 traffic in human beings, forced labour; 24 child labour in factories
  [0:6.3] Right to Freedom of Religion | 25–28 | conscience, religious affairs, taxes, religious instruction
  [0:6.4] Cultural and Educational Rights | 29–30 | minorities' culture; minorities' institutions
  [0:6.5] Right to Constitutional Remedies | 32 alone | writs; Art. 226 (High Courts) is outside Part III
[0:7] - **Classification traps:** **Art. 15** (prohibition of discrimination) and **Art. 17** (untouchability) fall under the **Right to Equality** – not Freedom of Religion, Exploitation or Cultural rights (UPSC 1995, 2020; SSC 2018).
[0:8] - **Wrong pair:** "Right to protection of life – **Art. 20**" is wrong; life and liberty is **Art. 21** (Art. 20 = protection in respect of conviction). Art. 15 discrimination, Art. 19 association and Art. 32 remedies are correct pairs.
[0:9] - **Description:** "a pledge to our people and a pact with the civilised world" – **S. Radhakrishnan**.
[0:10] > Asked as: "How many FRs?" (six – APPSC Drug Inspectors, Polytechnic Lecturers; SSC CGL 2017), "Which Part?" (III – APPSC G2 2008; SSC 2008, 2017), "Which Articles?" (12–35; Equality 14–18; Freedom 19–22), CDS 2003 "originally seven, now six – both correct".

## SUB 1 | FR or Not? – Look-alike Rights
[1:0] # Not Fundamental Rights
[1:1] TABLE head: Right | Where it really is
  [1:1.0] Right to work / employment | DPSP Art. 41 (not an FR)
  [1:1.1] Equal pay for equal work | DPSP Art. 39(d)
  [1:1.2] Right to property | Art. 300A, legal right since the 44th Amendment (1978)
  [1:1.3] Right to vote and to be elected | Constitutional right (Art. 326) – UPSC 2017 official key; given effect by statute (RPA 1950 s.19, RPA 1951 s.62); NextGen calls it a statutory right
  [1:1.4] Right to information | Not listed in Part III; the Supreme Court reads it into Art. 19(1)(a) (RTI Act 2005 is a statute)
  [1:1.5] Right to protest, right to govern, right to citizenship, right of equal shelter | Not FRs (shelter is read into Art. 21 by courts, but no "right of equal shelter" is granted)
[1:2] # Look-alikes that ARE Fundamental Rights
[1:3] - **Education (6–14 years):** **Art. 21A**, inserted by the **86th Amendment, 2002**; the **RTE Act** was enacted in **2009**; both came into force on **1 April 2010**.
[1:4] - **Right to settle in any part of India** – Art. 19(1)(e); **gender equality** – Arts. 14, 15; **form associations**, **practise any profession** – Art. 19.
[1:5] - **Right to Equality, Freedom, against Exploitation, Freedom of Religion** – all FR groups (the "odd one out" is always the DPSP or statutory right).
[1:6] > NDA 2015: of education, work, associations and profession, only the **right to work** is not an FR. Voting age was lowered to **18** by the **61st Amendment (1988)**.

## SUB 2 | Citizens Only vs All Persons; Aliens
[2:0] # Who gets which right
[2:1] TABLE head: Citizens only | All persons (citizens + foreigners)
  [2:1.0] Art. 15 – no discrimination | Art. 14 – equality before law, equal protection of laws
  [2:1.1] Art. 16 – public employment | Art. 20 – protection in respect of conviction
  [2:1.2] Art. 19 – six freedoms (speech, movement, trade and profession…) | Art. 21 / 21A – life and liberty, education
  [2:1.3] Art. 29 – minorities' language and culture | Art. 22 – arrest safeguards (not enemy aliens)
  [2:1.4] Art. 30 – minorities' institutions | Arts. 23–24 – exploitation; Arts. 25–28 – religion; Art. 32 – remedies
[2:2] - **Five citizens-only Articles: 15, 16, 19, 29, 30** (Raj. PCS 2024; APPSC G1 2023: denied to aliens = Arts. **29–30, 19, 16** – **not 23–24**).
[2:3] - A **British citizen** (any foreigner) in India cannot claim **freedom of trade and profession** or **freedom of speech and expression**, free movement or residence (all Art. 19) – but gets equality before law, life and liberty, religious freedom (UPSC 1999).
[2:4] - **Non-citizens** get the **right to life and liberty** and **constitutional remedies** – not freedom of expression or of association (Art. 19).
[2:5] - **Aliens** are not given the citizens-only FRs – they are the category to whom FRs are "not given" (bankrupt or sick persons and political sufferers keep all their FRs).
[2:6] - Also citizens only: **contesting elections** (not an FR at all).
[2:7] - **Aliens:** the Constitution **does distinguish friendly and enemy aliens** – **enemy aliens** lose even the arrest-and-detention safeguards of **Art. 22(1)-(2)**, by **Art. 22(3)**.
[2:8] - **Companies** (legal persons) can claim the all-persons rights, but **not Art. 19**.
[2:9] ~~ Sources: NextGen Polity p.172-173 (citizens-only Arts. 15, 16, 19, 29, 30; all persons 14, 20, 21, 23, 25-28); Rocket Polity Part 1 p.38-39; app notes 2:8 sub 2
## SUB 3 | Features and Nature of Fundamental Rights
[3:0] # Features
[3:1] - **Justiciable:** a person can move the **Supreme Court directly (Art. 32)** and **High Courts (Art. 226)**; FRs are **above ordinary laws** (Art. 13).
[3:2] - **Not absolute but qualified:** the State can impose **reasonable restrictions** by law; the **courts judge reasonableness**; **Art. 19** lists **11 grounds** of restriction in all.
[3:3] - **Claimed against the State:** most FRs are available against **arbitrary State action** and **limit the power of the State – even Parliament**; some (15(2), 17, 23, 24) also bind private persons.
[3:4] - **Not sacrosanct:** Parliament can curtail them **only by a constitutional amendment** (not an ordinary law), without destroying the **basic structure**; they can be **suspended only as the Constitution prescribes** (Arts. 358-359).
[3:5] - **Negative and positive:** many are **negatively worded** (14, 15(1), 16(2), 18(1), 20, 22(1), 28(1)); **21A, 29, 30** confer privileges; **15, 17, 18, 20, 24** are absolute limits on legislative power.
[3:6] - **Both natural and legal**; they protect **individuals and minority groups** from arbitrary State action (both statements correct).
[3:7] - **Britain** has **no written FRs**; it relies on conventions and the rule of law.
[3:8] # Statement traps
[3:9] - **Wrong:** "FRs are **unamendable**", "**absolute**", "partly justiciable and absolute", "not limited by the Constitution", "all FRs are suspended in an emergency".
[3:10] - **Right:** "not absolute", "some only for citizens", "drawn from the American Constitution", "justiciable", "six in number", "granted by the Constitution".
[3:11] - **CDS 2004:** FRs are fundamental because they are protected and enforced by the Constitution, can be suspended only as it prescribes, and are enforceable against the State (I, II, III) – **not** because they cannot be amended.
[3:12] - **A–R (UPPSC 2015):** the Constitution is the longest (A true) and the FR chapter follows the American model (R true), but R does not explain A.
[3:13] - **Right to die:** **Gian Kaur (1996)** – no right to die (suicide) under Art. 21; **Common Cause (2018)** – right to **die with dignity** (passive euthanasia, living will) is part of Art. 21.
[3:14] - **India is not a fully federal constitution** – it is federal with a unitary bias ("quasi-federal").
[3:15] - **Golden Triangle:** **Arts. 14, 19 and 21** (Maneka Gandhi 1978; Minerva Mills 1980) – not 14-20-21, 14-15-16, 19-20-21 or 14-21-32.
[3:16] ~~ Sources: NextGen Polity p.169-173 (features), p.200 (Golden Triangle 14, 19, 21); Rocket Polity Part 1 p.38-40, Part 1 p.59 (Maneka Gandhi: golden triangle); sci.gov.in (Common Cause v. UoI, 9 Mar 2018); app notes 2:8 sub 1
## SUB 4 | Limits on FRs – Restrictions, Armed Forces (Arts. 33-35), Emergency (358-359)
[4:0] # Who can restrict
[4:1] - **Reasonable restrictions** are imposed by **law** made by **Parliament** (and State legislatures within their field) – not by the President, Prime Minister or Supreme Court; the courts only **judge** their reasonableness.
[4:2] # Articles 33–35
[4:3] TABLE head: Article | Provision
  [4:3.0] 33 | Parliament alone may restrict or abrogate FRs of the armed forces, forces charged with maintenance of public order (police), intelligence/counter-intelligence staff, and persons in telecommunication systems set up for those forces – including non-combatants (barbers, cooks, carpenters). Examples: Army Act 1950, Air Force Act 1950, Navy Act 1957
  [4:3.1] 34 | Restrictions while martial law is in force; Parliament may pass an indemnity Act; "martial law" is not defined in the Constitution
  [4:3.2] 35 | Only Parliament (not States) legislates on 16(3), 32(3), 33, 34 and punishments for Part III offences – for uniformity
[4:4] - **CDS 2015 trap:** staff of **communication systems set up in the country in general** are **not** covered by Art. 33 – they enjoy FRs at par with other citizens; the armed forces, public-order forces and **their** communication staff are the restricted categories.
[4:5] # Emergency
[4:6] - **Art. 358:** when a National Emergency is declared on **war or external aggression**, **Art. 19 (Right to Freedom) is suspended automatically** (not on armed rebellion).
[4:7] - **Art. 359:** the **President** may by order suspend the right to move courts for **specified FRs** – **never Arts. 20 and 21** (**44th Amendment, 1978**).
[4:8] ~~ Sources: NextGen Polity p.172 (Art. 19 suspension), p.223-225 (Arts. 33-35, 358-359); Rocket Polity Part 1 p.40; app notes 2:8 sub 6
## SUB 5 | Article 13 – Laws Void, Meaning of "Law", Amendments
[5:0] # Clauses
[5:1] TABLE head: Clause | Provision
  [5:1.0] 13(1) | Pre-constitution laws inconsistent with FRs are void to the extent of inconsistency
  [5:1.1] 13(2) | The State shall not make any law that takes away or abridges FRs; such post-constitution laws are void to that extent
  [5:1.2] 13(3)(a) | "Law" includes ordinance, order, bye-law, rule, regulation, notification, custom or usage having the force of law
  [5:1.3] 13(4) | Art. 13 does not apply to Art. 368 amendments – added by the 24th Amendment, 1971
[5:2] - **Basis of judicial review** (with Arts. 32 and 226); it gives **constitutional provisions (FRs) precedence over laws** of Parliament and State legislatures.
[5:3] - 13(1) refers to laws in force before the **commencement of the Constitution** (26 Jan 1950), not "before Independence".
[5:4] - **UPSC 2026:** "law" in Art. 13 includes **custom or usage** – so the view that custom is included is correct and the view excluding it is wrong (only Y correct).
[5:5] # Are amendments "law"?
[5:6] TABLE head: Case / Amendment | Year | Position
  [5:6.0] Shankari Prasad | 1951 | Amendment is not "law" under Art. 13; Parliament can amend FRs (first such case)
  [5:6.1] Golaknath | 1967 | FRs have a transcendental position – Parliament cannot abridge FRs; prospective overruling
  [5:6.2] 24th Amendment | 1971 | Added Art. 13(4) in reply
  [5:6.3] Kesavananda Bharati | 1973 | FRs amendable, but the basic structure cannot be destroyed
[5:7] - **Ninth Schedule:** added by the **1st Amendment (1951)** with **Arts. 31A and 31B**; **I.R. Coelho (2007)** – laws placed in it after 24 Apr 1973 are open to basic-structure review.
[5:8] ~~ Sources: NextGen Polity p.179-182 (Art. 13 clauses, 13(4), Golaknath); Rocket Polity Part 1 p.38; app notes 2:8 sub 4, sub 9
## SUB 6 | Doctrines under Article 13
[6:0] TABLE head: Doctrine | Meaning | Case
  [6:0.0] Severability | Law is void only to the extent of inconsistency; the consistent, severable part remains valid | A.K. Gopalan (1950); R.M.D.C. (1957); Motor General Traders v. State of AP (1984)
  [6:0.1] Eclipse | A pre-constitution law inconsistent with an FR is not wiped out – it is dormant (overshadowed) and revives when the conflict is removed | Bhikaji Narain Dhakras v. State of MP (1955); State of Gujarat v. Ambica Mills (1974) – extended to post-constitution laws for non-citizens
  [6:0.2] Waiver | A person cannot waive an FR | Basheshar Nath v. CIT (1959)
  [6:0.3] Prospective overruling | A ruling operates only for the future | Golaknath (1967)
  [6:0.4] Progressive interpretation | Constitution as a living document | Maneka Gandhi (1978) widened Art. 21; SaveLIFE Foundation v. UoI (2026) – right to trauma care under Art. 21
  [6:0.5] Absolute liability | Hazardous industry liable without exceptions | M.C. Mehta (1987) – Oleum gas leak
  [6:0.6] Territorial nexus | State law can operate outside the State if there is sufficient connection | State of Bombay v. R.M.D.C. (1957)
  [6:0.7] Pith and substance | Encroachment on another List – look at the true nature of the law | Federal doctrine (with aspect, colourable legislation, incidental and ancillary powers, repugnancy)
[6:1] - **Eclipse vs severability:** eclipse = law **sleeps and revives**; severability = **bad part cut off, rest lives**. Eminent domain and pleasure are unrelated doctrines.
[6:2] ~~ Sources: NextGen Polity p.180-182 (doctrines; Motor General Traders 1984; Ambica Mills 1974); app notes 2:8 sub 5
## SUB 7 | Article 12 – Meaning of "the State"
[7:0] # Definition
[7:1] - **Government and Parliament of India**; **Government and Legislature of each State**; **all local authorities** (municipalities, panchayats, district boards); **other authorities** within India or under the control of the Government of India (LIC, ONGC, electricity boards).
[7:2] - **Ambiguity – courts:** whether the **judiciary** is "other authority" is the grey area; in its judicial function it is **not** "State" (**Naresh Mirajkar v. State of Maharashtra, 1967**).
[7:3] - **Private bodies:** a writ does not ordinarily lie merely because a private body performs a public activity, unless the State is involved.
[7:4] TABLE head: Case | Year | Holding
  [7:4.0] Rajasthan SEB v. Mohan Lal | 1967 | Statutory bodies are "other authorities"
  [7:4.1] R.D. Shetty v. International Airport Authority | 1979 | Laid down tests for an instrumentality or agency of the State
  [7:4.2] Ajay Hasia v. Khalid Mujib | 1981 | Society running a Regional Engineering College = State; test is creation and control, not legal form
  [7:4.3] Pradeep Kumar Biswas | 2002 | State if financially, functionally and administratively dominated by government
[7:5] ~~ Sources: NextGen Polity p.174-175, p.178-179 (Naresh 1967; other authorities); app notes 2:8 sub 3
