## SUB 0 | Nitrogen Cycle
[0:0] # Reservoir and Processes
[0:1] - Main reservoir = **atmosphere (N₂ about 78% of air)**, NOT soil (APPSC G2 2025).
[0:2] - Plants take nitrogen as **nitrate**; atmospheric N₂ is not directly usable.
[0:3] TABLE head: Process | What happens | Agents
  [0:3.0] Fixation | N₂ → ammonia/nitrate (enzyme nitrogenase) | Rhizobium, Azotobacter, cyanobacteria (Anabaena, Nostoc), lightning, industrial Haber process
  [0:3.1] Ammonification | Organic N in dead matter → ammonia | Decomposer bacteria and fungi
  [0:3.2] Nitrification | Ammonia → nitrite → nitrate | Nitrosomonas (to nitrite), Nitrobacter (to nitrate)
  [0:3.3] Denitrification | Nitrate → N₂ gas, returned to air | Pseudomonas, Thiobacillus
[0:4] - Fixation, ammonification and nitrification **add** usable N to soil; denitrification **removes** it.
[0:5] # Legumes and Root Nodules
[0:6] - **Rhizobium** – symbiotic, in **legume root nodules** (pulses, pea); **Rhizobium japonicum** – soybean.
[0:7] - **Leghaemoglobin**: **pink/red** pigment in nodules (alfalfa, soybean); binds oxygen to keep it low, since **nitrogenase is destroyed by oxygen**.
[0:8] - **Boron** is necessary for nodule formation.
[0:9] - Pea family (Fabaceae/Leguminosae): groundnut, horse-gram, soybean; N-fixing plants: alfalfa, chickpea, clover.
[0:10] # Biofertilisers
[0:11] TABLE head: Organism | Nature | Crop / role
  [0:11.0] Rhizobium | Symbiotic, legume nodules | Pulses, pea
  [0:11.1] Azotobacter, Azospirillum | Free-living N fixers | Cereals, millets, non-leguminous upland crops
  [0:11.2] Blue-green algae (cyanobacteria) | Photosynthetic N fixers; heterocysts | Rice / paddy
  [0:11.3] Azolla–Anabaena | Water fern Azolla + Anabaena in its leaves | Water-logged rice fields (Azolla pinnata)
  [0:11.4] Frankia | Actinomycete, non-legumes | Alnus, Casuarina
  [0:11.5] VAM (mycorrhiza) | Fungus–root association | Phosphorus and water uptake
  [0:11.6] PSB | Bacillus megaterium, Pseudomonas striata | Solubilise phosphate
[0:12] Azotobacter is NOT partnered with Anabaena; symbiotic fixation in legume nodules is by Rhizobium, not cyanobacteria; VAM does not denitrify.
[0:13] - **Insectivorous plants** grow in **nitrogen-deficient** (often waterlogged) soils and trap insects for nitrogen.
[0:14] - **Uptake forms:** plants take nitrogen as **inorganic nitrates/nitrites** (also ammonium) and some **organic** compounds – **never directly from the air**.
[0:15] - **Rain** raises soil **nitrogen**: lightning fixes N₂ into nitrogen oxides that rain carries down as nitrates.

## SUB 1 | Carbon Cycle
[1:0] TABLE head: Adds CO₂ | Removes CO₂
  [1:0.0] Respiration, decomposition, fossil-fuel combustion, volcanic activity, deforestation | Photosynthesis (plants, algae, some bacteria), dissolving in oceans, carbonate sedimentation, silicate weathering
[1:1] - Reservoirs and sinks: atmosphere, oceans, forests, soils; long-term – carbonate rocks and fossil fuels. Forests = vital carbon sinks.
[1:2] Photosynthesis does NOT add CO₂ – it removes it.
[1:3] - **Where the extra CO₂ goes:** much is removed by ocean uptake and **photosynthesis by phytoplankton**; it does not escape to the outer stratosphere, and polar ice only archives old air (UPSC 2011: 2 only).
[1:4] - **Deep Carbon Observatory (DCO, 2009-2019):** global research programme on the role of carbon in Earth, including field studies of **deep microbial life** (both statements correct, UPPSC 2019).

## SUB 2 | Biogeochemical Cycles – Basics and Types
[2:0] - Nutrients are recycled indefinitely between organisms, air and the Earth's crust; driven by energy flow; **all cycles are linked to the water cycle**.
[2:1] - Organisms need at least **27 elements** (15 metals). Macronutrients: potassium, sodium, magnesium, calcium, sulphur, phosphorus, chlorine.
[2:2] TABLE head: Type | Cycles | Main reservoir
  [2:2.0] Gaseous | Carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, water | Atmosphere / ocean
  [2:2.1] Sedimentary | Phosphorus, sulphur, calcium, potassium, iron | Earth's crust – soil and rocks
[2:3] Hydrogen (H₂) is NOT a gaseous cycle in exam lists.
[2:4] APPSC 2025: Removing decomposers blocks mineral movement – nutrients locked in dead matter never return to the soil.
[2:5] # Phosphorus Cycle
[2:6] - **No atmospheric phase**; natural reservoir = **rock (phosphates)**; weathering of rocks is the main source; much ends in **ocean sediments**.
[2:7] - P is used in membranes, nucleic acids, energy transfer, shells, bones, teeth.
[2:8] - **Definition:** movement of nutrient elements through the biotic and abiotic components = **biogeochemical (nutritional) cycle**.
[2:9] - **Soil's major nutrients** for cultivation: **nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium (NPK)**.
[2:10] ~~ Sources: NextGen Rocket Environment p.52; NextGen Environment Part 1.2 p.8; PMF Environment Part 2 p.16, 27-28
## SUB 3 | Ecological Succession – Terms and Types
[3:0] # Terms
[3:1] TABLE head: Term | Meaning
  [3:1.0] Succession | Gradual, orderly replacement of one community by another in an area over time
  [3:1.1] Pioneer species | First hardy colonisers – lichens, mosses
  [3:1.2] Sere / seral stage | Whole sequence = sere; each step = seral stage (seral communities in between)
  [3:1.3] Climax community | Final stable community in balance with climate (forest, grassland, coral reef)
  [3:1.4] Seasonal succession | Cyclic, driven by environmental rhythms; NOT caused by disturbance
[3:2] # Primary vs Secondary
[3:3] TABLE head: Primary | Secondary
  [3:3.0] Area that never had life – lava, bare rock, volcanic island; no soil | After fire, flood, logging; soil, seeds, roots remain
  [3:3.1] Pioneers: lichens and mosses | Faster than primary
[3:4] - On a surface without soil, **lichens and mosses** survive; **ferns need soil**.
[3:5] - Grasslands do not turn into forest because of **water limits and dry-season fire**.
[3:6] # Seres by Starting Medium
[3:7] TABLE head: Sere | Starts on
  [3:7.0] Hydrosere | Water (pond, lake)
  [3:7.1] Xerosere | Dry land
  [3:7.2] Lithosere | Bare rock (sub-type of xerosere)
  [3:7.3] Psammosere | Sand (sub-type of xerosere)
  [3:7.4] Halosere | Saline soil or water
[3:8] - **Sub-climax:** a disturbance that holds a sere at an intermediate stage gives a **sub-climax** community.
[3:9] ~~ Sources: NextGen Environment Part 1.1 p.14 and Part 1.2 p.1-2; PMF Environment Part 1 p.56-61
## SUB 4 | Succession – Phases, Sequences and Theories
[4:0] # Clements' Phases (1916)
[4:1] TABLE head: Phase | What happens
  [4:1.0] 1. Nudation | Bare area forms (erosion, landslide, volcano, fire)
  [4:1.1] 2. Invasion | Seeds/spores arrive; ecesis (successful establishment), aggregation
  [4:1.2] 3. Competition and co-action | Unfit species eliminated
  [4:1.3] 4. Reaction | Organisms modify soil, water, light, temperature – most important phase
  [4:1.4] 5. Stabilisation | Climax community
[4:2] Invasion comes right after nudation; ecesis belongs to the invasion phase.
[4:3] # Sequences
[4:4] - **Xerosere**: crustose lichens → foliose lichens → mosses → herbs and grasses → shrubs → forest. **Lichens** (algae + fungi) start succession on bare rock.
[4:5] - Pioneer trees (birch, oak) need high light; shade-tolerant species form the climax.
[4:6] - **Hydrosere**: **small phytoplankton** (pioneers in water) → submerged rooted (Chara) → floating (Lemna, Pistia, water lilies) → reed-swamp → sedge-meadow → woodland → forest; ends in wet woodland (**fen carr**).
[4:7] - Hydroseres are better studied – **lake sediments keep a permanent record**.
[4:8] # Ecologists and Climax Theories
[4:9] TABLE head: Person | Contribution
  [4:9.0] F.E. Clements | Phases of succession (1916); monoclimax – one climax per region, set by climate
  [4:9.1] Tansley | Polyclimax; coined "ecosystem"
  [4:9.2] Whittaker | Climax-pattern
  [4:9.3] E.P. Odum (1971) | Succession = orderly community change within a unit area
  [4:9.4] H.C. Cowles | Succession on sand dunes
  [4:9.5] Elton / Lindeman | Food chains, pyramid of numbers / 10% law

## SUB 5 | Functions of the Environment
[5:0] # Four Functions
[5:1] TABLE head: Function | Meaning
  [5:1.0] Supplies resources | Both renewable AND non-renewable raw materials (first function)
  [5:1.1] Assimilates waste | Biodegradation – microorganisms break organic waste into nutrients for reuse
  [5:1.2] Sustains life | Genetic diversity, biodiversity, ozone layer UV protection
  [5:1.3] Aesthetic services | Scenery, recreation
[5:2] APPSC 2022: "Environment provides renewable resources only" is the INCORRECT statement.
[5:3] "Assimilating wealth" is NOT a vital function of the environment.
[5:4] # Function → Description (APPSC 2023 match)
[5:5] TABLE head: Function | Description
  [5:5.0] Waste decomposition | Breakdown of organic waste into simpler compounds
  [5:5.1] Cultural heritage | Source of traditional practices, knowledge and identity
  [5:5.2] Recreation and aesthetics | Scenic and leisure value
  [5:5.3] Medicinal resources | Supply of drugs and herbal remedies
[5:6] - **Ecosystem functions**: ecological succession, nutrient cycling, energy flow through food chains, **homeostasis** (maintenance of ecological balance).
[5:7] - Nutrients are recycled; **energy flows through and is not recycled**.
[5:8] - **NOT a function:** "recycling" is not one of the four listed functions (APPSC ACF 2022); "disorganises waste" and "helps get rid of water pollution" are wrong wordings too.
[5:9] - **Order:** the **first** function is **production / supply of raw materials (resources)**; waste recycling by microbes = **biodegradation function**.

## SUB 6 | Ecosystem Services and Valuation
[6:0] # Millennium Ecosystem Assessment (MEA, 2005, UN)
[6:1] TABLE head: MEA category | Examples
  [6:1.0] Provisioning | Tangible goods – food, fresh water, timber, fibre, medicinal resources
  [6:1.1] Regulating | Climate, flood, disease regulation, water purification, pollination, waste decomposition
  [6:1.2] Cultural | Recreation, aesthetics, cultural heritage, spiritual value
  [6:1.3] Supporting | Basis of all other services – soil formation, nutrient cycling, primary production
[6:2] Pollination and waste decomposition = regulating; nutrient cycling and soil formation = supporting. UPSC 2012 official key grouped **nutrient cycling and crop pollination** as the supporting option (pollination is strictly regulating).
[6:3] - **Cultural services** are hardest to price in money, so their value is underestimated.
[6:4] # Forest Services
[6:5] TABLE head: Economic products | Ecological services
  [6:5.0] Timber, bamboo, fuelwood, fodder, NTFPs | Carbon sequestration, soil and water conservation, groundwater recharge, flood moderation, air purification
[6:6] - **AP angle**: AP forests give intangible services – carbon sink, water-flow regulation, soil and moisture conservation. AP has the **second-largest mangrove ecosystem** in India (Godavari and Krishna estuaries).
[6:7] # Valuation Frameworks
[6:8] TABLE head: Report / framework | By / year | Role
  [6:8.0] MEA | 2005, UN | Four service categories
  [6:8.1] Costanza et al. | 1997 | First valued global services at about US$33 trillion a year
  [6:8.2] TEEB | Launched 2007, Potsdam (G8+5); hosted by UNEP | "Making nature's values visible" in decision-making
  [6:8.3] EnviStats India | MoSPI, annual | Natural capital accounting under the UN SEEA framework
[6:9] - Later values: coral reefs US$375 billion–9.9 trillion a year; Amazon US$2.2–3.4 trillion a year.
[6:10] - Debate: pricing nature aids decisions but undervalues cultural and spiritual services.
[6:11] - **TEEB:** global initiative hosted by **UNEP** (not IMF / WEF) – draws attention to the **economic benefits of biodiversity** and helps decision-makers **recognise, demonstrate and capture** the value of ecosystems (UPSC 2016: 2 and 3 only).
[6:12] - **Trees:** planting trees helps **maintain ecological balance**; trees reduce air pollution – causing it is not a function of a tree.

## SUB 7 | Human Disruption of Cycles
[7:0] TABLE head: Factor | Effect
  [7:0.0] Fossil fuels + deforestation | Raise atmospheric CO₂ → climate change
  [7:0.1] Chemical pesticides | Alter soils and microbial networks; may weaken resilience (not always increase it)
  [7:0.2] Nitrogen overload | Human activity has at least doubled nitrogen available to plants
  [7:0.3] Fertiliser runoff | Algal blooms; decay uses up dissolved oxygen → dead zones
  [7:0.4] N fertiliser in soil | Microbes release nitrous oxide, a greenhouse gas
[7:1] - **Prosopis juliflora** (invasive) called an "ecological first responder" – a pioneer spreading where hydrology and soil chemistry have changed (7 May 2026).
[7:2] - **Reactive nitrogen sources (UPSC 2019):** agricultural soils release **nitrogen oxides**, cattle release **ammonia**, the poultry industry releases reactive nitrogen compounds – all three correct.

## SUB 8 | Water Cycle Regulation – Wetlands and Forests
[8:0] - Water carries elements through the ecosystem; cycles would stop if water did not cycle.
[8:1] - **Wetlands as sponges**: store floodwater, recharge aquifers, keep dry-season flow; also control erosion, retain sediment, support nutrient cycling and biodiversity.
[8:2] - **Wetlands as "kidneys" of the Earth**: aquatic plants absorb heavy metals and excess substances.
[8:3] - **Microclimate regulators**: stored water evaporates and holds heat, steadying local temperature and humidity.
[8:4] - **Forests**: regulate water flow, conserve soil and moisture, recharge groundwater, moderate floods.
[8:5] ~~ Sources: app notes 5:74 sub 5; NextGen Environment Part 2.1 p.1
