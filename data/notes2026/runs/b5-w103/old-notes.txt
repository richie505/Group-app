[103:0:3] # Single-use plastic (SUP) ban – 1 July 2022
[103:0:4] - India's measure against plastic pollution: **banning identified single-use plastics** (not all plastic, not all food containers).
[103:0:5] - **19 items**: ear buds with plastic sticks, balloon sticks, plastic flags, candy and ice-cream sticks, **thermocol for decoration**, plates, cups, cutlery, straws, cigarette-packet films, PVC banners < 100 microns. NOT banned: **HDPE milk bottles**. The ban covers only **~11%** of plastic waste (CSE).
[103:0:6] - Enforcement: **SPCBs/PCCs and local bodies**, with a National Level Task Force and State Special Task Forces.
[103:0:7] - **NGT (2019)** fined **25 States/UTs ₹1 crore** each for missing the deadline for plastic waste management action plans.
[103:0:8] # EPR for plastic packaging
[103:0:9] - **EPR:** the producer's duty for environmentally sound management of its product **till end of life** – collection and recycling after use.
[103:0:10] - **PIBOs** = Producers, Importers and Brand Owners; recyclers generate tradable EPR certificates on the **CPCB portal**.

[103:0:14] - **Bisphenol A (BPA):** key building block of **polycarbonate** (and epoxy resins); leaches from bottles and food containers; endocrine disruptor.
[103:0:15] - **Contain plastic – all three:** **cigarette butts** (cellulose acetate filter), **eyeglass lenses**, **car tyres** (synthetic rubber) (UPSC 2025).
[103:0:16] - A bottle that keeps its shape with hot water is taken to be **thermosetting** plastic (CDS 2019 key); polyethylene is the polymer made from ethylene gas.
[103:0:17] - Plastic waste: **toxic fumes** when burnt, lasts long, **cannot be composted**.
[103:0:18] - **Microplastics: < 5 mm**; nurdles = primary pellets 1–5 mm. **Microbeads** (cosmetics) are a concern because they **harm marine ecosystems** (UPSC 2019); banned in cosmetics. **Synthetic-rubber tyre wear** is a big source of ocean microplastics – tyres of **natural rubber** cut it. FSSAI found microplastics in all tested sugar and salt.
[103:0:19] - **Plastic pollution** = accumulation of non-biodegradable waste in the oceans that threatens marine life.
[103:0:20] - **Plastic-eating fungus** *Aspergillus tubingensis*: found at a waste dump in **Islamabad, Pakistan** (2017).
[103:0:21] - **IIT Kharagpur** made eco-friendly food packaging from **cucumber peels**. **India's largest charkha** made of used plastic waste was inaugurated in **Noida** (Gandhi 150).

## SUB 2 | Disposal and Treatment Methods
[103:2:0] TABLE head: Method | Process | Suited to
  [103:2:0.0] Open dumping | Untreated, uncovered, unsegregated waste on land | Not viable – flies, rats, leachate
  [103:2:0.1] Landfill | Most common and economic method in India; most urban household waste ends here | Residual waste; last resort
  [103:2:0.2] Sanitary landfill | Waste compacted in cells and covered with earth every day; liners (clay/plastic) + leachate collection protect groundwater; adopted as a substitute for open-burning dumps | Only residual inert waste; costly
  [103:2:0.3] Composting (aerobic) – Indore method | Microbes (bacteria, fungi) oxidise organics; material turned at intervals; 2–4 weeks | Wet/organic waste
  [103:2:0.4] Bangalore method (anaerobic) | Pits with alternate layers of MSW and night soil; 4–6 months | Organic waste
  [103:2:0.5] Vermicomposting | Earthworms break waste down (vermiculture) | Household organic waste
  [103:2:0.6] Incineration | Controlled burning at high heat in oxygen; cuts volume ~90%; steam for power; emits pollutants, toxic ash | Hazardous, infectious/biomedical
  [103:2:0.7] Pyrolysis | Thermo-chemical decomposition (heating) without oxygen → gas, oil, char (carbon-based fuels) | Mixed waste; costly
  [103:2:0.8] Plasma gasification | Extreme thermal process (electricity + very high temperature), no combustion → syngas (H2 + CO); inorganics → slag | All organic wastes
  [103:2:0.9] RDF | Shredded, dehydrated high-calorific dry MSW | Fuel in cement kilns and thermal plants
[103:2:1] - **Pyrolysis and plasma gasification = waste-to-energy technologies** (UPSC 2019); compost and vermicompost give manure, not energy.
[103:2:2] - **Compost:** biodegradable waste decomposed in pits; rich in organic matter and nutrients; earthworms and microbes **speed up** (not slow down) decomposition. In composting both aerobes and anaerobes degrade organic matter. **Vermiculture** = the science of **raising earthworms**.
[103:2:3] - **Biomining** uses bacteria, **fungi** or plants to extract metals from ores and solid wastes; composting also uses fungi.
[103:2:4] - Urban India uses **composting, incineration and landfilling – "all the above"**.
[103:2:5] - **Other pollution solutions:** **Oilzapper** = bacterial mix for oil spills and oily sludge; **bioremediation** uses organisms to remove pollutants; **UV purification** kills microbes in drinking water; **hydrogen fuel cells** emit only **water vapour**.
[103:2:6] - **Better method to reduce the solid-waste problem: recycling** (not burning, sea dumping or compressing). **Composting household waste** cuts methane and helps reduce global warming; **burning household waste** does not reduce GHG emissions.
[103:2:7] TABLE head: Waste | Disposal method
  [103:2:7.0] Organic | Composting
  [103:2:7.1] E-waste | Recycling
  [103:2:7.2] Hazardous / biomedical | Incineration (biomedical at a common treatment facility)
  [103:2:7.3] Broken glass pieces | Landfilling
  [103:2:7.4] Non-recyclable high-calorific dry waste | RDF / co-processing in cement kilns
[103:2:8] - **Trade-offs of the four main methods (APPSC G2 2025 match):**
[103:2:9] TABLE head: Method | Pro / con
  [103:2:9.0] Landfilling | Low cost, but long-term environmental impact
  [103:2:9.1] Incineration | Reduces volume, but emits pollutants
  [103:2:9.2] Composting | Useful by-products (manure, biogas), but needs land
  [103:2:9.3] Recycling | Conserves resources, but needs infrastructure
[103:2:10] > Exam angle: match method → function (incineration = burning; pyrolysis = waste to fuel; plasma = extreme heat; composting = biological decay); "most common method in India" = landfilling.

## SUB 3 | Biodegradable vs Non-biodegradable; Kinds of Waste
[103:3:0] # Definitions
[103:3:1] - **Solid waste:** discarded or abandoned material in **solid, liquid, semi-solid or containerised gaseous** form; includes urban, agricultural, biomedical and radioactive wastes.
[103:3:2] - **Liquid waste (sewage):** fluids from washing, flushing or manufacturing. **Sullage** = waste water from **kitchens** and bathrooms (grey water), not from toilets (black water/sewage), factories or hospitals.
[103:3:3] - **E-waste (ITU):** discarded electrical and electronic equipment and parts **not meant for reuse**. **Biomedical waste:** waste from **diagnosis, treatment or immunisation of humans or animals** (blood, sharps, cultures, discarded medicines).
[103:3:4] - **Garbage** = putrescible kitchen/market waste; **rubbish** = household/commercial waste other than garbage and ashes.
[103:3:5] # Biodegradable vs non-biodegradable
[103:3:6] - **Biodegradable:** decomposed by micro-organisms (mainly **bacteria**) into simpler harmless substances. **Aerobic** decomposition → **compost**; **anaerobic** → **methane (biogas)**.
[103:3:7] - **Non-biodegradable:** mainly inorganic; cannot be broken down by microbes, so it accumulates; **recycling** is the ideal option for it.
[103:3:8] TABLE head: Biodegradable | Non-biodegradable
  [103:3:8.0] Sewage, faecal matter, urine, domestic garbage, food/kitchen waste, vegetable and fruit peels, cow dung, paddy husk | Plastic, polythene bags, plastic cups, DDT, insecticides/pesticides, asbestos
  [103:3:8.1] Paper, wood, grass, leaves, plant waste, cellulose, starch, proteins | Tin, aluminium foil, metal scrap, mercury, lead
  [103:3:8.2] Wool (natural keratin fibre), cotton, leather | Glass bottles, China clay cups, polystyrene cups
  [103:3:8.3] Natural rubber | Synthetic rubber/tyres, nylon, Teflon, PVC
  [103:3:8.4] Polymers: polylactic acid (PLA), PHBV, polyglycolic acid, Nylon-2-Nylon-6 | Polyethylene, polystyrene, polycarbonate
[103:3:9] - **Polythene** is a **polymer of ethylene (C2H4)** – carbon and hydrogen only; polythene bags cannot be destroyed by bacteria because they are synthetic **polymers**.
[103:3:10] - Biodegradable polymers are eco-friendly because microbes degrade them; non-biodegradable plastics persist and break into **microplastics**.
[103:3:11] - **Biodegradable plastics** (e.g. PLA) are a better alternative, but some need much energy and resources to make and can still pollute on disposal – both statements true (TSPSC 2023).
[103:3:12] - **Industrial wastes:** **thermal power plants** give fly ash (non-biodegradable) – they produce the most non-biodegradable waste; **gypsum** comes from **fertiliser** plants, not food processing. Food processing, paper, cotton, textile and sugar mills give mostly non-hazardous, biodegradable waste.
[103:3:13] TABLE head: Litter | Time to degrade
  [103:3:13.0] Paper | 10–30 days
  [103:3:13.1] Woollen items | about 1 year
  [103:3:13.2] Tin, aluminium and other metal items | 100–500 years
  [103:3:13.3] Plastic bags | up to 1 million years
[103:3:14] # Categories
[103:3:15] TABLE head: Category | What it holds
  [103:3:15.0] Municipal (MSW) | Household + commercial: organics, plastics, paper, metals, street and market waste
  [103:3:15.1] Agricultural | Crop residues, paddy husk, cattle dung (biodegradable; dung → biogas under GOBARdhan)
  [103:3:15.2] Construction & demolition | Mostly inert – concrete, bricks, dirt, rocks, debris
  [103:3:15.3] Hazardous | Paints, batteries, pesticides, chemicals, fluorescent lamps, aerosol cans
  [103:3:15.4] Biomedical / E-waste / Radioactive | Separate rules (see later sub-sections)
[103:3:16] - **Hazardous waste – four classic criteria:** **ignitability, corrosivity, reactivity, toxicity** (NOT biodegradability). **Household hazardous waste:** **paints and batteries**, bulbs, aerosol cans, tyres.
[103:3:17] - **Healthcare waste (WHO):** **~85% general non-hazardous, ~15% hazardous** (infectious, chemical, radioactive).
[103:3:18] - **Land pollution:** its significant cause is the **disposal of solid waste** (not rainfall or planting trees).

## SUB 4 | 3R, Circular Economy and Industrial By-products
[103:4:0] TABLE head: Term | Meaning
  [103:4:0.0] 3R | Reduce, Reuse, Recycle ("Regain" is NOT one of the 3Rs)
  [103:4:0.1] Top priority | Reduction at source – practising reduce, reuse, recycle cuts waste at source (not disposables, open dumping or street burning)
  [103:4:0.2] Reuse | Using the item again in the same form
  [103:4:0.3] Recycling | Converting waste materials into other usable materials
  [103:4:0.4] 5R | Adds Refuse and Repurpose (or Recover)
  [103:4:0.5] Least preferred | Landfilling
  [103:4:0.6] RRR Centre | Permanent in every city under SBM-U 2.0
[103:4:1] - **Circular economy:** minimises waste by **reusing and recycling materials** (not using products till they break, landfilling or banning manufacture). **Unplanned waste generation** is NOT sustainable development.
[103:4:2] - **Zero waste management:** **segregation at source, separate collection of each kind, community involvement** – all of the above. **Indore** is the model zero-waste city.
[103:4:3] - **Legacy waste:** old MSW in dumpsites; **biomining** sorts it into recyclables, RDF, bio-soil and inert fill.
[103:4:4] TABLE head: Waste | Source industry | Reuse
  [103:4:4.0] Fly ash | Coal thermal plants | Bricks, roads, replaces part of Portland cement (up to ~35%); contains toxic elements (arsenic, lead, mercury) – not only SiO2 and CaO; use mandatory within 300 km
  [103:4:4.1] Steel slag | Steel plants | Road base, agricultural soil, cement (all three – UPSC 2020); Surat steel-slag road
  [103:4:4.2] Red mud | Alumina refining | Bricks, tiles
  [103:4:4.3] Phosphogypsum | Phosphoric acid / phosphatic fertiliser plants | Gypsum substitute, soil conditioner
  [103:4:4.4] Chromium sludge | Tanneries | – (hazardous)
[103:4:5] - **Green rural roads (UPSC 2020):** **copper slag, cold-mix asphalt, geotextiles** are preferred; hot-mix asphalt and Portland cement are not.

## SUB 5 | Solid Waste Management Rules and Indian Data
[103:5:0] TABLE head: Rules | Key points
  [103:5:0.0] MSW (Management and Handling) Rules 2000 | ULBs collect, segregate, store, transport, process, dispose; primary goal promoting segregation
  [103:5:0.1] SWM Rules 2016 (8 Apr 2016) | Replaced the MSW Rules 2000; apply beyond municipal areas (urban agglomerations, census towns, industrial townships, railways, airports, defence, pilgrimage places); 3 streams – wet (biodegradable), dry, domestic hazardous; brand owners with non-biodegradable packaging must set up a collect-back system (producer responsibility for packaging life cycle); user fees and spot fines; exact criteria for siting landfills and waste-processing facilities (landfill 100 m from river, 200 m from pond, 500 m from highways/habitations, 20 km from airports); industries within 100 km of an RDF plant replace 5% fuel with RDF
  [103:5:0.2] SWM Rules 2026 (under EPA 1986; in force 1 Apr 2026; replaced 2016) | 4 streams: wet, dry, sanitary, special care (green, blue, red, black bins); landfills only for non-recyclable inert residue; higher landfill fee for unsegregated waste; legacy dumps mapped with time-bound biomining; Environmental Compensation = polluter pays
[103:5:1] - SWM 2016 did NOT require five categories, did NOT bar moving waste between districts, and was NOT limited to ULBs and industrial townships.
[103:5:2] - 2016: used diapers/napkins wrapped and put in the **dry** bin; 2026: separate **sanitary** stream. **Special care** = domestic hazardous (batteries, bulbs, medicines, paint cans). Paper, plastics, metals, glass → **dry**.
[103:5:3] - **Bulk Waste Generators** (2026): process wet waste on site or hold an **EBWGR** certificate.
[103:5:4] - In 2016 MoEFCC notified **six sets of waste rules** (solid, plastic, e-waste, biomedical, hazardous, C&D); **CPCB** (with SPCBs) is the nodal body for standards and monitoring of waste treatment.
[103:5:5] - **Supreme Court:** clean environment part of **Art. 21** (Feb 2026); **18 Aug 2026** – **generators**, not only sanitary workers, answerable for waste at the doorstep. Draft "SWM Rules 2024" (proposed 1 Oct 2025) were not the notified rules.
[103:5:6] - **Collection chain:** door-to-door → segregated transport → **transfer station → Material Recovery Facility (MRF) → processing/recycling** → residual landfill.

## SUB 6 | E-waste
[103:6:0] # Rules
[103:6:1] - **EPR in Indian law was first introduced by the e-Waste (Management and Handling) Rules 2011** (UPSC 2019; not the BMW Rules 1998, Recycled Plastic Rules 1999 or Hazardous Wastes Rules 2016). Rocket Environment p.45 credits the Plastic Waste (M&H) Rules 2011 – a source slip; UPSC's answer is the e-waste rules.
[103:6:2] - **E-Waste (Management) Rules 2016:** 21 products; PROs, deposit-refund scheme, e-waste exchange.
[103:6:3] - **E-Waste (Management) Rules 2022:** notified by **MoEFCC in November 2022**; in force **1 April 2023** (not Nov 2022); **106 EEE items incl. solar PV** waste; do NOT apply to packaging plastics or radioactive waste (separate rules).
[103:6:4] - Collection of e-waste is the **producer's** responsibility (EPR); only **registered** producers, manufacturers, refurbishers and recyclers may deal; producers register on the **CPCB portal** and buy **digital EPR certificates** from registered recyclers; annual targets on past sales.
[103:6:5] - **Battery Waste Management Rules 2022** replaced the 2001 rules.
[103:6:6] # Magnitude
[103:6:7] - India = **3rd-largest** e-waste generator (after **China, USA**); about **2.2 million tonnes (2025)**. **Mumbai** = largest e-waste-producing city (ASSOCHAM); Maharashtra the top State.
[103:6:8] - **3.54 lakh tonnes** collected and processed in **FY 2020-21** (of 13.46 lakh t generated); 11.59 lakh t recycled in FY 2024-25.
[103:6:9] - **Global E-waste Monitor** (**ITU and UNITAR**) 2024: **62 Mt** in 2022, only **22.3%** formally recycled; 82 Mt by 2030.
[103:6:10] # Recycling chain and hazards
[103:6:11] - Chain: consumer → collection centre / producer take-back → **authorised dismantler → authorised recycler** → recovered materials. Citizens should **sell or give old electronics to authorised recyclers** (not burn, bin or dump them).
[103:6:12] - **78%** processed informally; informal hubs **Seelampur (Delhi), Moradabad (UP)**. "Invisible e-waste": vapes, USB cables, LED accessories.
[103:6:13] - **R2 (Responsible Recycling) Code of Practices:** environmentally responsible practices in the **electronics recycling industry** (UPSC 2021).
[103:6:14] - Old computers release **beryllium, cadmium, chromium, mercury, lead**, PCBs, PBBs – NOT **heptachlor** (an insecticide) or **plutonium** (radioactive) (UPSC 2013).

=== NOTES 5:104 | Carbon Emissions, Carbon Footprint, Carbon Sequestration, Carbon Credits and Carbon Trading
## SUB 0 | Carbon Sinks, Blue Carbon, REDD+ and Carbon Monitoring
[104:0:0] # Sinks
[104:0:1] - **Carbon sink:** a reservoir that accumulates and stores carbon. **Natural sinks: forests, soil, oceans** (chlor-alkali plants are NOT sinks). Forests act as sinks when they grow in density or area.
[104:0:2] - **Blue carbon:** carbon captured by **oceans and coastal ecosystems** – **mangroves, seagrass, salt marshes** (all three are blue-carbon ecosystems). Forest/soil carbon is not "blue"; fossil carbon is in petroleum and gas.
[104:0:3] - **Best sink per unit area = blue carbon ecosystems:** they store several times more carbon per area than tropical rainforests (seagrass ~3 times). Glaciers are not sinks.
[104:0:4] - **Tropical peatlands:** most carbon **per hectare** of any terrestrial ecosystem.
[104:0:5] - **Carbon fertilisation:** **increased plant growth** because more atmospheric CO2 raises photosynthesis (~**70%** of Earth's recent greening). Not warming, not ocean acidity.
[104:0:6] - **Bhutan** = carbon-negative country. Net zero = emissions balanced by removals.
[104:0:7] # Forest carbon stock (ISFR)
[104:0:8] TABLE head: Report | Figure
  [104:0:8.0] ISFR 2019 – India | 7,124.6 million tonnes
  [104:0:8.1] ISFR 2019 – Andhra Pradesh (incl. TOF patches > 1 ha) | ~220 Mt (805 Mt CO2e) = 3.08% of India's forest carbon
  [104:0:8.2] ISFR 2023 (Dec 2024) – India | 7,285.5 million tonnes, up 81.5 Mt over 2021; total sink ~30.43 bn t CO2e
[104:0:9] # IPCC carbon pools (5)
[104:0:10] - Above-ground biomass, below-ground biomass, dead wood, litter, soil organic carbon. **Atmospheric oxygen is NOT a pool.**
[104:0:11] # REDD+ and forest-carbon finance
[104:0:12] - **REDD+** = Reducing Emissions from **Deforestation and Forest Degradation** (+ conservation, sustainable management, enhancement of stocks).
[104:0:13] - **UN-REDD** (2008; **UNDP, FAO, UNEP**): proper design contributes to **biodiversity protection, forest resilience and poverty reduction – all three** (UPSC 2016).
[104:0:14] - **Forest Carbon Partnership Facility (World Bank):** global partnership of governments, businesses, civil society and indigenous peoples; **helps countries with REDD+** finance and technical aid. It does **not** fund universities/scientists for forestry research (UPSC 2015: I and III).
[104:0:15] - **Khasi Hills Community REDD+ Project** (East Khasi Hills, **Meghalaya**): **India's first community REDD+ project, Plan Vivo certified**; run by Khasi **himas**; ~27,000 ha (UPSC 2026).
[104:0:16] # Carbon monitoring
[104:0:17] - **National Carbon Project** – under the **ISRO Geosphere-Biosphere Programme**: assesses **carbon pools, fluxes and net carbon balance** of India's terrestrial biomes and builds **remote-sensing spatial databases** (statements I and III). NOT "space technology for sequestration".

## SUB 1 | Carbon Sequestration, CCS and Carbon Removal
[104:1:0] - **Carbon sequestration:** capturing and long-term storing of atmospheric CO2 – in plants (afforestation), soil, oceans or geological formations. **Afforestation = capturing and storing CO2.**
[104:1:1] - **CCS (Carbon Capture and Storage), "also known as carbon sequestration":** capture at a stationary point source (power plant, cement plant) → transport → storage (**3 steps**); store only. APPSC key for "technology that captures and stores CO2 from industrial processes" = **carbon sequestration**.
[104:1:2] - **CCUS:** capture, then **use or store**; supports coal gasification and the hydrogen economy.
[104:1:3] TABLE head: Method | Meaning
  [104:1:3.0] Geological storage | Abandoned/uneconomic coal seams, depleted oil and gas reservoirs, deep saline formations – all three (UPSC 2017)
  [104:1:3.1] Enhanced rock weathering | Finely ground basalt spread on farmland binds CO2 as carbonate
  [104:1:3.2] Ocean alkalinity enhancement | Adding lime/alkaline minerals to seawater
  [104:1:3.3] Mine storage | Industrial CO2 pumped into abandoned mines as carbonated water
  [104:1:3.4] Direct Air Capture (DAC) | CO2 straight from ambient air anywhere; a sequestration method; CO2 used in plastics, food/beverage processing; with hydrogen gives synthetic aviation fuel (UPSC 2025: all three)
  [104:1:3.5] BECCS | Bioenergy + CCS → net-negative emissions
[104:1:4] - UPSC 2023: basalt on farms, lime in oceans, CO2 into abandoned mines – **all three** are discussed for carbon capture and sequestration.
