=== Ecosystem – Concept, Biotic and Abiotic Components, Producers and Decomposers
# Concept
- **Ecosystem:** a community of organisms **together with the non-living environment** in which they live, interacting as one functional unit (pond, ocean, forest, even an **aquarium**). Term coined by the British botanist **Sir Arthur G. Tansley in 1935**.
- **Two components:** **biotic** (producers, consumers, decomposers) and **abiotic** (energy, water, air, soil, nutrients, climate). "Evolution" is a process, not a component.
- An ecosystem is a **natural, open system**: energy and matter enter and leave; it has its own **productivity**; it is **not a closed system with fixed boundaries** (UPSC/APPSC "NOT correct" item).
- The **biosphere** (narrow zone where **lithosphere, hydrosphere and atmosphere** meet) is treated in most books as an **open system** – energy enters as sunlight and leaves as heat (UPPSC RO/ARO 2017 key: A true, R false). Strictly, the Earth is open for energy but nearly **closed for matter**.
- All components are **interdependent**: a change in one population affects the rest – so "components are not interdependent" is false, while "human activities affect the environment" is true.
- **Functions:** energy flow (one-way), nutrient cycling, production, **self-regulation (homeostasis)**; the **food chain** is the **dynamic part** of an ecosystem (APPSC G1 2008), while producers, consumers and decomposers form its structure.
# Abiotic components
| Type | Factors |
|---|---|
| Energy | Sunlight – primary source and driving force |
| Climatic | Light, temperature, rainfall, humidity, wind |
| **Edaphic** | **Soil** – texture, pH, minerals, drainage |
| Topographic | Altitude, slope, aspect |
| Materials | CO₂, water, nitrates, phosphates; humus and other organic compounds |
- **Abiotic factor:** any **non-living** component that affects organisms or their environment – air, water, light are abiotic; plants, bacteria and animals are biotic.
- **Liebig's Law of the Minimum:** growth limited by the nutrient in shortest supply. **Shelford's Law of Tolerance:** both too little and too much limit.
- **Stenothermal** organisms tolerate only a **narrow** temperature range; **eurythermal** a wide range (likewise stenohaline / euryhaline for salinity).
- **Cold-blooded (poikilothermic)** animals – **frogs**, snakes, lizards, fish, crocodiles – take the temperature of their surroundings.
# Producers (autotrophs) – trophic level I
- **Autotrophs** make their own food; their **carbon and energy needs are met by photosynthesis** (NCERT X): they take in **CO₂ and water** (not oxygen) and, with **sunlight and chlorophyll**, convert them into **carbohydrates**, which give the plant energy.
- **Photosynthesis** turns **light energy into chemical energy**; it needs light – normally daytime sunlight, but it also runs under moonlight or artificial light, so it is not tied to clock time (State PSC key "day or night").
- On land the major producers are **herbaceous and woody plants**; in water, **phytoplankton** / microscopic **algae** and seaweeds.
- **Chemoautotrophs:** bacteria of deep-sea vents / active volcanoes oxidise **hydrogen sulphide**; many **archaea** (methanogens) use energy from chemical compounds – they too are producers.
- Some **bacteria** (green sulphur bacteria) photosynthesise with **invisible infrared** light.
- **Producers act as intermediaries between the biotic and abiotic components** (official UPPSC key; many books also call **decomposers** the link, because they return nutrients to the abiotic pool).
- **Chlorella** (a green alga) has the highest efficiency of converting solar energy into chemical energy among the usual options.
- **Oxygen:** most of Earth's oxygen comes from **algae** / marine phytoplankton – ocean phytoplankton and photosynthetic bacteria give **about 50%** of the world's oxygen, more than rainforests; oxygen-rich surface water holds far **less** oxygen than air (UPSC 2025: only statement II correct).
- **Cactus** photosynthesises in its green **stem** (leaves reduced to spines).
# Consumers and decomposers
| Component | Examples |
|---|---|
| Primary consumers (herbivores) | Cow, deer, goat, rabbit, caterpillar, **zooplankton** |
| Secondary consumers | Wolves, frog, fox |
| Tertiary / top carnivores | Lion, tiger, hawk, eagle |
| Omnivores | **Man** (primary and secondary consumer), bear |
| **Decomposers** (saprotrophs) | **Bacteria and fungi** – not viruses |
| **Detritivores** | **Earthworms, millipedes, woodlice**, nematodes – not jellyfish or seahorses |
- **Fungi are heterotrophs** (mostly saprotrophs that absorb food from dead matter; some parasitic or symbiotic).
- **Parasitic nutrition:** obtaining food from another living organism **without killing it**.
- **Decomposition:** breakdown of dead organic matter by **microorganisms (bacteria, fungi)** into simple inorganic nutrients; biodegradable waste is converted into useful compost by **bacteria**.
- Steps: **fragmentation → leaching → catabolism → humification → mineralisation**; fully decomposed litter = **humus**. Fast when detritus is rich in nitrogen and sugars and climate warm and moist; slow with lignin, chitin, cold or anaerobic conditions.
- If **decomposers are removed**, **mineral (nutrient) movement is blocked** – energy flow would continue for a while, but nutrients are not recycled.
- Macroconsumers = phagotrophs; microconsumers = saprotrophs (osmotrophs). Free-living **Azotobacter** fixes nitrogen.
- **Protists** are unicellular **eukaryotes**, not prokaryotes; cyanobacteria are photosynthetic prokaryotes.
# Types of ecosystem
| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| Natural terrestrial | Forest, grassland, desert, tundra, mountain |
| Natural aquatic | Pond, **lake**, river, wetland, estuary, ocean |
| **Artificial (man-modified)** | **Crop field**, aquarium, park, village; **city** = extreme example |
- A **lake / pond** is the classic **self-sufficient (complete, self-regulating) natural ecosystem**; an aquarium, farmland or polyhouse depends on human inputs.

=== Food Chains, Trophic Levels, Food Webs and Biomagnification
# Food chain
- **Food chain:** the order in which organisms **feed upon each other** – producer → herbivore → carnivore; energy passes along it. It runs **across species**, not within one species' population, and it does **not** show the **numbers** eaten (that is a pyramid / food web) – UPSC 2013: only statement 1 correct.
| Chain | Starts with | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Grazing** | **Living green plants** (autotrophs pass energy to herbivores) | **Grass → grasshopper → frog → snake** (→ eagle); grass → goat → human |
| **Detritus (DFC)** | **Dead organic matter** | Leaf litter → earthworm → chicken → hawk |
| Pond / aquatic | Phytoplankton | → zooplankton → small fish → large fish |
| Marine | **Diatoms** | → **crustaceans** → **herrings** (UPSC 2014) |
| Grassland | Grass | → grasshopper or rabbit → snake or hawk |
- "Grazing food chain begins with **dead leaves**" is **false** (that is the detritus chain); "autotrophs capture energy and transfer it to herbivores" is true (APPSC G2 Mains 2025: A false, R true).
- In water the grazing chain is the main energy conduit; on land more energy flows through the detritus chain.
# Trophic levels
| Level | Organisms |
|---|---|
| **I** | **Producers** – green plants, **phytoplankton, seaweeds, chemosynthetic bacteria** (vents, active volcanoes), algae |
| II | **Primary consumers / herbivores** – deer, ants, caterpillar, goat, **zooplankton** |
| III | Secondary consumers – frog, fox; a man eating goat flesh |
| IV | Tertiary / top carnivores – snake (in grass → grasshopper → frog → snake), hawk, lion |
| None single | Decomposers and detritivores recycle all levels |
- The **highest trophic level** is held by **carnivores** (top carnivores), not herbivores or decomposers.
- **Man** is an omnivore – **primary as well as secondary consumer**; eating goat flesh makes him a **secondary consumer**.
- **Producers outnumber primary consumers** (energy and numbers fall at each level).
- A species can occupy more than one level; chains rarely exceed **4–5 levels**.
- Missing-level question: "tiger, grass, snake, frog" has **no herbivore – second level missing**.
# Food web and stability
- **Food web:** network of interlinked food chains; **more complex → more stable** (alternative feeding paths).
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Resistance | Withstand a disturbance |
| **Resilience** | **Recover** after a disturbance |
| Homeostasis | Self-regulation up to a limit through feedback |
- Diverse systems (rainforest, coral reef) are more stable than tundra, desert or monoculture; overall the **ocean (marine ecosystem) is the most stable ecosystem** – it stays unchanged over long periods.
- Warming and ocean acidification weaken calcifying plankton and corals, disrupting marine food webs.
# Bioaccumulation vs biomagnification
| Bioaccumulation | Biomagnification (biological magnification) |
|---|---|
| Build-up of a pollutant inside one organism over its life | **Rising concentration of toxicants at each higher trophic level** – maximum in the topmost level |
- Only **persistent, fat-soluble, non-biodegradable** pollutants (DDT, mercury, PCBs) biomagnify – an increase in **toxicants**, not in algae, insects or weeds.
- DDT (a **chlorinated hydrocarbon**): food crop → rat → snake → **hawk** – highest in the hawk; grasshopper → toad → **snake** – highest in the snake; causes **eggshell thinning** and seabird crashes.
- **Methylmercury** – Minamata disease (Japan).
- Pollutant types: biodegradable = sewage; non-biodegradable = DDT, plastic, asbestos; primary = CO, SO₂, NOx; secondary = O₃, **PAN**, smog.
- **Trophic level:** the functional position (feeding step) an organism occupies in a food chain.

=== Environment, Ecology and Levels of Organisation
# Environment
- **Environment:** the sum total of living and non-living components, influences and events surrounding an organism; **biotic and abiotic factors interact**; it is never static. Composite: **physical** (land, water, air, nutrients), **biological** (organisms) and **cultural** elements – "all of the above" definitions hold.
- Term "environment" first used in English by **Thomas Carlyle (1828)**.
| Category | Includes |
|---|---|
| Physical | Lithosphere, hydrosphere, atmosphere; nutrients, water, air |
| Biotic | Plants, animals, microbes |
| Cultural | Man-made – social, economic, political institutions |
- Renewable resources come from **both** biotic and abiotic parts (sunlight, wind, water) – "only biotic elements give renewable resources" is false.
- **Primary functions:** provide resources, **assimilate wastes**, habitat for biodiversity – "generating synthetic chemicals" is NOT a function.
- Every **habitat is an environment**, but an environment may exist without life.
# Ecology
- **Ecology** (Greek *oikos* = home + *logos* = study): study of the **relationship between organisms (plants, animals) and their environment**; term coined by **Ernst Haeckel in 1866** ("Ökologie"); also called **bionomics** – synonymous with ecology, stressing natural systems that influence human systems.
| Branch / term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Autecology** | Ecology of a **single species** in relation to its environment |
| Synecology | Ecology of communities |
| **Deep ecology** | Term of the Norwegian philosopher **Arne Naess (1973)** |
| **Ecological transition** | Concept of **John W. Bennett** (*The Ecological Transition*, 1976) |
| **Ecad** | An organism (plant) **modified by its environment** – a habitat form without genetic change |
| Variation | Differences among individuals of the same species |
| Bionomics | = ecology ("management / laws of life") |
# Levels of organisation
- Order from smallest to largest: **organism → population → community → ecosystem → (landscape) → biome → biosphere** (UPPSC 2017: population – community – ecosystem – landscape).
| Level | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Population | Individuals of one species in an area |
| Community | All populations of different species in an area |
| Ecosystem | Community + its non-living environment; basic functional unit of ecology |
| Biome | Large region defined by climate + dominant vegetation (rainforest, tundra, desert) |
| **Biosphere** | Narrow **zone of contact of lithosphere, hydrosphere and atmosphere** where life exists; all ecosystems together |
- **Biodiversity** measured as **alpha, beta, gamma**; "biological diversity" coined by **Raymond F. Dasmann**; hotspot concept – **Norman Myers** (≥1,500 endemic vascular plants, ≥70% habitat lost).
- **Sere** = whole sequence of communities in succession; **habitat fragmentation** = continuous habitat broken into isolated patches; **anthrome** = human-altered terrestrial biome.

=== Energy Flow, Productivity and the Ten Percent Law
# Energy flow
- **Unidirectional**: **sunlight → producers → herbivores → carnivores**; energy is not cycled. **Solar energy** is the primary source and **driving force** of an ecosystem.
- **Law of conservation of energy** (first law of thermodynamics): energy can be neither created nor destroyed but **can be transformed** from one form to another – light → chemical energy in photosynthesis. Second law: each transfer loses energy as **heat**.
- Under 50% of sunlight is **PAR**; plants fix about **2–10% of PAR**; green plants capture only about **1% of the sunlight** falling on their leaves (UPPSC 2025, SSC 2011).
- **Total primary production efficiency** = share of incoming **solar energy captured and used in photosynthesis** – about **1–2%**.
# Ten percent law
- **Raymond Lindeman (1942)**: only about **10%** of the energy (as food) passes to the next trophic level; about **90%** is lost (respiration, excretion, locomotion, heat) – energy **decreases** at each transfer, hence 4–5 levels.
- 1% = solar energy captured by plants; 10% = energy passed between trophic levels.
# Productivity
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **GPP** (**Gross Primary Production**) | Total rate of organic matter (solar energy) fixed by producers in photosynthesis |
| NPP | GPP − producer respiration; available to consumers |
| **Primary productivity** | Rate at which **solar energy is stored in green plants** as food (autotroph level) |
| **Secondary productivity** | Rate of **formation of new organic matter by consumers** (heterotroph level) |
- Primary production in an **aquatic ecosystem** depends on **light penetration, nutrients and temperature** (all of these), with phytoplankton as producers.
- Highest NPP per unit area: **tropical rainforest**; estuaries, coral reefs, swamps, mangroves also high. Lowest: **deserts and open ocean** (ocean total still large by area).
- Decreasing productivity: **mangroves > grasslands > lakes > oceans** (UPSC 2013); ecotones (mangroves, estuaries) beat their surroundings.

=== Habitat, Niche and Population
# Habitat vs niche
| Habitat | Niche |
|---|---|
| Physical place where an organism lives – its "address" | **Functional role** of a species in its habitat – its "profession" |
| One habitat holds many species | **Unique** to each species – no two species have identical niches |
- **Ecological niche** describes not only the physical space occupied but also the **functional role** in the community (UPSC 2013); it covers the range of conditions tolerated, resources used and role played. Concept enunciated by **Grinnell**.
- **Fundamental niche:** full range of biotic and abiotic conditions and resources a species could use **free from interference of other species**; **realised niche**: actual range used under competition.
- **Niche breadth:** specialists (**koala** – only eucalyptus) vs generalists.
- **Resource partitioning:** competing species coexist by using different parts of a shared resource.
- **Terrestrial** organisms live on land; **hydrophytes** are aquatic plants (not necessarily rootless).
# Population attributes
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Natality** | **Birth** of individuals; rate expressed per unit population per unit time, or as **individuals produced per female per unit time** |
| Mortality | Deaths per unit time |
| **Density** | **Individuals per unit area** (or volume) |
| Others | Age structure, sex ratio, dispersal (NOT dominant vegetation) |
| J-curve | Exponential growth, unlimited resources |
| S-curve | Logistic growth levelling at carrying capacity K |
- Age pyramids: **triangular = expanding; bell = stable; urn = declining**.
- **Carrying capacity:** the **maximum population an environment can sustain** for ever **without being degraded**. It is **dynamic** – it changes with resources, climate and technology. Conditions: resource extraction must stay **below** regeneration, and **waste within the absorption capacity** (UPPSC 2025: only 2 correct).
| r-selected | K-selected |
|---|---|
| Many small offspring, little care, short life, unstable habitats – insects, weeds | Few large offspring, much care, long life, near K – elephants, humans |

=== Ecological Pyramids
- Show trophic levels; **base = producers**, apex = top consumers; **three types** – numbers, biomass, energy.
| Pyramid | Upright | Inverted |
|---|---|---|
| Numbers | Grassland, pond | **Tree ecosystem**; parasitic chain |
| Biomass | Forest, grassland | **Pond, marine** (small, fast-turning phytoplankton crop) |
| Energy | **Always** | **Never** |
- **Biomass** = total (dry) weight of organisms per unit area; **standing crop** = living biomass in a trophic level per unit area **at a given time** – a quantity, not a rate.
- Limitations: assume a simple chain, ignore food webs; a species cannot be shown at two levels; **saprophytes and decomposers get no place**.
- Energy decreases upward mainly through **heat loss**.
> Exam angle: own MCQs and APPSC G2 Mains 2025 – energy pyramid never inverted; biomass inverted in pond/sea; numbers inverted in a tree.

|---|---|---|
| Mutualism | +/+ | Lichen, fig–fig wasp, mycorrhiza |

=== Ecotones, Ecogeographic Rules and Plant Adaptations
- **Ecotone:** transition zone where two communities meet – mangroves (marine–terrestrial), estuary, **grassland (forest–desert)**, river bank; a uniform forest stand is NOT an ecotone.
- **Edge effect:** more species and higher density of some species in the ecotone; they are **edge species**.
| Rule | Trait |
|---|---|
| Bergmann | Body size – colder = larger |
| Allen | Colder = shorter ears and limbs |
| Jordan | Fish vertebrae – colder = more |
| Gloger | Colouration – warm-humid = darker |
| Group | Habitat | Adaptation – example |
|---|---|---|
| **Xerophytes** | Dry | Thick cuticle, sunken stomata, spines, CAM, **green photosynthetic stem** – Opuntia (cactus) |
| **Hydrophytes** | Water | Aerenchyma, reduced roots – Hydrilla, lotus |
| Halophytes | Saline | **Pneumatophores, vivipary** – Rhizophora |
| Phreatophytes | Groundwater | Deep roots |
| Lithophytes | Rocks | – |
- **Cuscuta (dodder)**: stem parasite with haustoria. Desert flora do NOT have broad thin leaves.

=== Terrestrial Ecosystems and Biomes
- **Natural**: forests, grasslands, oceans, lakes, deserts. **Artificial**: crop fields, parks; **city** = extreme example. Estuary, marine, wetlands are aquatic, not terrestrial.
- **Rainforest**: highest land species richness; fast decay → poor soil. **Taiga**: slow decay, thick litter, acidic soil. **Mature forests** give little net oxygen (respiration balances photosynthesis).
- Hot desert: **Thar**, Sahara. Cold desert: Ladakh, **Gobi**.

| Pampas | Argentina |
| Steppe | Eurasia |
| Veld | South Africa |

| Banni | Kutch – threat **Prosopis juliflora** |
