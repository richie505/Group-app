=== Winter Season, Retreating and North-East Monsoon, Western Disturbances
# Retreating monsoon (October–November/December)
- Caused by the **southward movement of the Sun's vertical rays**; the NW low disappears, the **subtropical westerly jet returns south of the Himalayas**, the TEJ disappears.
- Withdrawal is gradual (about three months): from the extreme **north-west first** – NextGen/NCERT: from the beginning of **October**; PMF/IMD: from **mid-September** – out of the peninsula by October and from the south-east tip by **December**.
- **Fine weather and clear skies**; land temperature rises again → hot, humid "**October heat**".
- **Tropical cyclones** are the hallmark: they form mostly in the **Bay of Bengal** (rarely the Arabian Sea), and their frequency peaks in **October–November**; they bring heavy rain to the east coast → **bimodal** rainfall in the south.
- The retreating winds blow from land **towards the Bay of Bengal**, pick up moisture there and rain on the Coromandel coast.
- About **13%** of annual rainfall comes in this season.
> "Declining monsoon is indicated by clear sky, pressure change over the Bay of Bengal and rising temperature on land" – all three (BPSC).
# North-east monsoon and Tamil Nadu
- The winter high over NW India sends winds NW → SE down the plains; over the Bay of Bengal they become the **north-east monsoon** and pick up moisture.
- **Tamil Nadu / Coromandel coast** gets its **main rain in October–December (Nov–Dec)** from the NE monsoon; South Arcot and Ramanathapuram get **over 50%** of their rain then. Also **Rayalaseema, Nellore, south coastal AP, SE Karnataka, SE Kerala**; **not the Gujarat coast**.
- **Why Tamil Nadu is dry in the SW monsoon:** it lies in the **rain shadow** of the Western Ghats for the Arabian Sea branch, and the Bay branch blows **parallel to the coast**. So **Chennai** gets the least SW-monsoon rain among Kolkata, Mangalore, Chennai, Delhi.
- States **not** fed mainly by the June–September monsoon: **Tamil Nadu** (NE monsoon) and **Jammu & Kashmir** (western disturbances).
# Western disturbances
- **Temperate (extra-tropical) cyclones from the Mediterranean**, carried east by the westerlies through Iran–Afghanistan–Pakistan into **north-west India**.
- Give **10–15 cm** of winter rain to the **north-western plains** – **beneficial for rabi crops (wheat)** – and **snowfall** in J&K, Himachal Pradesh, Uttarakhand.
- Winter rain in the NW plains **decreases from west to east** (and from north to south).
- **Northern plains' winter rain = western disturbances**, not the NE monsoon (which is active in winter but is not the cause) and not the retreating monsoon.
- They are **not** part of the monsoon system (CDS: "not associated with monsoon climate" = western disturbances).
# Winter season (December/January–February)
- **High pressure over NW India** (NCERT: the **1019 mb** isobar passes through north-west India, **1013 mb** in the far south) → **anticyclonic**, dry, outblowing winds; cold northern plains, cold waves, fog; **no loo or thunderstorms**.
- **Anticyclonic conditions** form because the land is **cold and the air dense** – not because of winter rain (UPSC 2001: A true, R false).
- Winter is the **driest season (~2%)**: most of India is dry under the **north-east trade winds**, which blow from land to sea.
- **Dry winters** (Koeppen Cwg, "monsoon with dry winter"): **Bengal / Ganga plain**; J&K and Punjab–Haryana get western-disturbance rain, Tamil Nadu gets NE-monsoon rain.
- **South India:** maritime – **low diurnal and annual range**, high temperature all year, **no extremes**; the 20 °C January isotherm runs from the Tapi mouth to the Mahanadi delta.

=== Monsoon – Meaning, Characteristics and Controls of Indian Climate
# Meaning and characteristics
- India has a **tropical monsoon** climate; "monsoon" from Arabic **Mausim** = season; **Al Masudi** (Arab geographer) described the seasonal rhythm of winds.
- Monsoons are **seasonal (secondary/periodic) winds** that **reverse direction** with the season – **not primary (planetary) winds**.
- They are confined to the tropics, roughly **20°N–20°S** (textbook statement).
- The distribution of SW-monsoon rain is governed mainly by **relief**.
- Monsoon rain is **not steady**, **unevenly (not equally) distributed**, has **breaks** and is **uncertain/variable**.
- India is a "**thirsty land**" despite abundant rain: rain is **concentrated in a few months**, **runs off rapidly** and **evaporates quickly** – all three.
- Main cause of the monsoon: the **temperature (pressure) contrast between the land and the Indian Ocean**.
- **Early maritime links** of India with South-East Asia across the Bay of Bengal were helped by the **monsoon winds** (UPSC 2011).
# Controls (factors) of Indian climate
- **Latitude** – the **Tropic of Cancer** crosses the middle of India; the south is tropical, the north sub-tropical. India lies about **8°4'–37°6'N**, so it is **not wholly between the tropics** (Jharkhand PCS: A true, R false).
- **Himalayas** – block cold Central Asian winds, trap the monsoon and give India its **climatic distinctiveness**.
- **Distance from the sea (continentality)**, **altitude**, **relief**, **pressure and winds**, **upper-air circulation (jet streams)**, **ocean currents**, **cyclones/western disturbances**.
- CDS 2015: **location, thermal contrast, upper-air circulation and the Himalayas** are all major factors.
- **Amritsar and Shimla** lie on nearly the same latitude; Shimla is cooler because of **altitude** (~2,200 m vs ~230 m). Likewise **Ooty vs Coimbatore**, **Agra (16 °C) vs Darjiling (4 °C)** in January.
- At a similar latitude **Mumbai** is moderate while **Nagpur** is extreme – **distance from the sea**. In **Kerala and the Andaman Islands** the day–night range is only about **7–8 °C**.
> A–R (NDA 2011): "Monsoon winds are driven by the size of the Asian landmass" and "upper-air circulation is associated with the monsoon" – both true, R not the explanation of A.

=== Onset, Advance, Branches and Breaks of the South-West Monsoon
# Onset and advance
- Season: **June–September** (IMD); also stated as **mid-June to mid-September**; gives **~75%** of annual rain.
- Sudden onset = "**burst**" in the first week of June; rain in Kerala called **Varsha**.
| Stage | Normal date |
|---|---|
| Andaman & Nicobar Islands (first Indian territory) | ~25 May |
| **Kerala** (first state, Malabar coast) | **1 June** (2026: 4 June) |
| Bhopal / Delhi | ~15 June / ~25 June |
| Whole country | **8 July** (IMD revised normal) |
- IMD declares the Kerala onset after **10 May** when **60% of 14 stations** get **2.5 mm** on two consecutive days (5-day **pentads** used).
- Order of onset: **Kerala → West Bengal → Uttar Pradesh → Rajasthan** (CDS 2010).
- Duration: **> 120 days** in the SW peninsula, **< 75 days** in west Rajasthan; the monsoon reaches **west Rajasthan last**.
# Arabian Sea branch
- Strikes the **Western Ghats** and Malabar first; heaviest rain at **Agumbe, Hulikal** on the crest; the **windward west coast** gets very heavy **orographic** rain because the **Western Ghats** lie across the winds.
- **Mumbai** (windward) gets far more than **Pune** (leeward); the eastern side of the Ghats is a **rain-shadow** area.
- Enters central India via the **Narmada–Tapi** valleys; one arm runs **parallel to the Aravallis** → scanty rain in Rajasthan.
- The **Chhattisgarh Basin** is **not much affected** by the Arabian Sea branch (it gets Bay branch rain).
- The **Palghat Gap** lets only part of the Arabian Sea branch through to Tamil Nadu – the **majority** of monsoon winds do **not** enter through it.
# Bay of Bengal branch
- Strikes Myanmar and the **funnel-shaped Garo–Khasi–Jaintia hills** → orographic rain in **Meghalaya** (Mawsynram, Cherrapunji).
- Deflected **westward by the Himalayas** along the Ganga plain → rain **decreases east to west**; **Jharkhand** and the east get their rain from the **SW monsoon**.
> APPSC FRO: "Kerala is the first state to get the SW monsoon in early June" and "the Bay branch causes heavy rain in Meghalaya" – **both correct**.
# Breaks
- A **break** occurs when the **monsoon trough (ITCZ) shifts north to the Himalayan foothills**: the plains stay dry, the hills get heavy rain, and temperature rises.
- One or two breaks a season; most often in the **second week of August**.
- Rajasthan **does** get some SW monsoon rain (little), so "Rajasthan gets no SW monsoon rain" is false.

=== Hot Weather Season, Local Storms and the Six Traditional Seasons
# Hot weather (pre-monsoon) season – March to May
- Northward march of the Sun; over **42.5 °C** in the north and NW; **Ganganagar** recorded about **50 °C** (1935).
- **Heat waves** in north India: **March to June** (peak May–June); IMD plains threshold **40 °C**.
- **Dust storms (Andhi)** – commonest in **May**, in Rajasthan, Haryana, Punjab, Delhi, western UP.
- Pre-monsoon rain (~**10%** of annual) is **convectional**: strong heating → rising air **cools adiabatically** → thunderstorms (CDS 2009 A–R: both true, R explains A).
| Local storm / wind | Region | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Mango showers** | **Kerala and coastal Karnataka** (PMF adds Tamil Nadu, AP) | Help mangoes ripen |
| **Blossom / cherry blossom showers** | Karnataka, Kerala coffee areas | **Coffee** flowering |
| **Kaal Baisakhi / Nor'westers** | **West Bengal**, Assam, Odisha, NE | Violent evening storms; useful for **tea, jute, rice** |
| Bardoli Chheerha | Assam | Assam's name for the Nor'westers |
| **Loo** | N and NW plains (Punjab to Bihar) | **Hot, dry wind** – not rain |
| Karaburan | Central Asia | Hot dusty wind – not Indian |
- Tornadoes and hailstorms peak in **March–May**; hailstorms are rare in the monsoon; most hail-prone: **Vidarbha and Marathwada**. Deadliest tornado: **Belda, 24 March 1998**.

=== Theories of the Monsoon Mechanism, ENSO and Monsoon Research
| Theory / driver | Core idea |
|---|---|
| **Thermal** (differential heating) – **Edmund Halley, 1686** | Land heats faster than sea → summer low over land draws moist sea winds; reversed in winter |
| **Dynamic / ITCZ shift** (1950s) | ITCZ moves north over the Ganga plain as the **monsoon trough**; SE trades cross the equator and turn south-westerly |
| **Jet streams** | **STJ** shifts **north of the Himalayas** → onset; returns **south** → withdrawal. **TEJ** (South China Sea → **East Africa**) coincides with the SW monsoon; **Somali jet** + **Somalia Current** strengthen it |
| **Tibetan Plateau** (~5,000 m) | Summer **heat island** → upper-air **anticyclone**, stronger TEJ; heavy snow cover weakens it |
| **ENSO / IOD / MJO** | **El Nino weakens** (delayed, weak monsoon, drought); La Nina strengthens; **positive IOD** can offset El Nino; MJO repeats every 30–60 days |
- **Mascarene High** (near **Madagascar**) drives the cross-equatorial flow; **dust aerosols** over the Indo-Gangetic Plain help pull the monsoon inland.
- **Gilbert Walker** (1920s) described the **Southern Oscillation**: low pressure over the Indian Ocean (Darwin) + high over the Pacific (Tahiti) = good monsoon.
- **MONEX** (Monsoon Experiment) **1979** under the **WMO** confirmed the role of the shifting ITCZ (an earlier Indo-Soviet experiment was held in 1973).
- **El Nino**: a warm current off **Peru** every **3–7 years** replacing the cold Peruvian current; it **causes drought-like conditions in parts of India**.
