=== NOTES 4:38 | Indian Climate – Monsoon Mechanism, Theories and Seasons
## SUB 0 | Monsoon – Meaning and Characteristics
[38:0:0] - "Monsoon" from Arabic **Mausim** = season.
[38:0:1] - **Seasonal (secondary)** winds that **reverse direction** with the season – sea → land in summer, land → sea in winter; NOT primary (planetary) winds.
[38:0:2] - Confined to the tropics between **20°N and 20°S**.
[38:0:3] - Distribution is controlled mainly by **relief**; rainfall is **not steady**, **unequally distributed** and variable.
[38:0:4] - Sudden onset = "**burst**" (first week of June).
[38:0:5] - **Breaks**: the monsoon trough shifts to the **Himalayan foothills** → plains dry, hills get heavy rain.
[38:0:6] - India is a "**thirsty land**" despite heavy rain: almost all rain comes in June–September and runs off or evaporates, leaving shortage in the other 8 months.
[38:0:7] APPSC: "Monsoons are primary winds" – FALSE. "Monsoon rain is equally distributed" – NOT a feature.
[38:0:8] # Controls of Indian Climate
[38:0:9] - Main controls: **latitude, altitude, pressure and wind system, distance from the sea (continentality), ocean currents, relief**.
[38:0:10] - Altitude: **Ooty** (~2,240 m) is far cooler in summer than **Coimbatore** (~411 m).
[38:0:11] - Continentality: at a similar latitude, **Mumbai** has moderate temperatures while inland **Nagpur** has hot summers and cold winters.
[38:0:12] - **Island and coastal climate:** In **Kerala** and the **Andaman Islands** the difference between day and night temperatures is hardly **7–8 °C**; in the **Thar desert** a 50 °C day may fall to **15–20 °C** at night. Interior places such as **Delhi, Kanpur and Amritsar** have sharp seasonal contrasts.
[38:0:13] - **Altitude example (NCERT):** **Agra** and **Darjiling** are on the same latitude, but the January temperature is about **16 °C** in Agra and only **4 °C** in Darjiling.
## SUB 1 | Theories of the Monsoon Mechanism
[38:1:0] TABLE head: Theory / driver | Core idea
  [38:1:0.0] Thermal / differential heating – Sir Edmund Halley, 1686 | Land heats faster than sea → summer low over land draws moist sea winds; reversed in winter
  [38:1:0.1] Dynamic / ITCZ shift | ITCZ moves north over the Ganga plain as the monsoon trough; SE trades cross the equator and turn south-westerly
  [38:1:0.2] Jet streams | Subtropical westerly jet (STJ) shifts north of the Himalayas → onset; returns south → withdrawal. Tropical Easterly Jet (TEJ) (South China Sea → East Africa) coincides with the SW monsoon
  [38:1:0.3] Tibetan Plateau heating | Plateau (~5,000 m) = summer heat island → low pressure, upper-air anticyclone, stronger TEJ
  [38:1:0.4] ENSO / IOD / MJO | El Nino weakens, La Nina aids; positive IOD can offset El Nino; an unfavourable MJO phase stalls the monsoon
[38:1:1] - **Al Masudi** (10th c.) noted the seasonal winds.
[38:1:2] - **Dust aerosols** over the Indo-Gangetic Plain heat the mid-troposphere and help pull the monsoon inland.
[38:1:3] - Winter jet blows north of the Himalayas, parallel to the Tibetan highlands; the Tibetan highland **splits it** into north and south branches.
[38:1:4] Onset = STJ north of the Himalayas; withdrawal = STJ back south of the Himalayas (not north).
[38:1:5] # Mascarene High and Somali Jet
[38:1:6] TABLE head: Term | Role
  [38:1:6.0] Mascarene High | Permanent high in the south Indian Ocean (near Madagascar) → drives cross-equatorial flow to the subcontinent
  [38:1:6.1] Somali jet | Low-level jet along the East African coast; with the Somalia Current it strengthens monsoon winds

[38:3:2] # Tornadoes and Hailstorms
[38:3:3] - **Tornadoes**: mainly **eastern/NE India** (WB, Odisha, Jharkhand, Bihar, Assam), **March–May** with the Nor'westers. Deadliest: **24 March 1998, Belda** (WB–Odisha border), ~250 deaths. Others: New Delhi (1978), Berhampore (1993), Kendrapara (2009).
[38:3:4] - **Hailstorms**: mainly **pre-monsoon (March–May)** and winter; rare in the monsoon; **not** mainly Oct–Dec. Most prone: **Maharashtra – Vidarbha and Marathwada**; also MP, Chhattisgarh, Punjab, Haryana, Rajasthan, UP, HP.

## SUB 5 | Branches of the Sw Monsoon

## SUB 7 | Retreating Monsoon, Ne Monsoon and Western Disturbances
[38:7:0] TABLE head: System | Origin | Effect
  [38:7:0.0] Retreating monsoon (Oct–Nov) | Sun moves south; NW low vanishes; TEJ disappears | Clear skies, "October heat" (hot, humid); Bay cyclones → bimodal rain in the south
  [38:7:0.1] NE monsoon (Oct–Dec) | Retreating winds pick up Bay moisture | Coromandel (Tamil Nadu), Rayalaseema, Nellore, Prakasam
  [38:7:0.2] Western disturbances | Mediterranean temperate cyclones via Iran–Afghanistan–Pakistan | NW India 10–15 cm winter rain for rabi; snow in J&K, HP, Uttarakhand
[38:7:1] - **Tamil Nadu coast is dry in the SW monsoon** – rain-shadow side and winds parallel to the coast; its winter rain comes from the **NE monsoon**, not western disturbances.
[38:7:2] - **Northern plains winter rain** = western disturbances; the NE monsoon is active then but is not the cause.
