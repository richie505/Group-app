## SUB 1 | Seasonal Share and Regional Gradients of Rainfall
[1:0] TABLE head: Season | Share of annual rain
  [1:0.0] South-west monsoon (June–Sept) | ~75% – the main source for almost all India
  [1:0.1] Retreating / NE monsoon | ~13%
  [1:0.2] Summer (pre-monsoon) | ~10%
  [1:0.3] Winter | ~2% (driest season)
[1:1] - **Northern plains:** rain **decreases from east to west** (the Bay stream loses moisture): Kolkata > Patna > Prayagraj > Delhi > Bikaner. In the **Ganga basin** it also falls **away from the Himalayan foothills**, i.e. **north to south**.
[1:2] - **Duration of the monsoon decreases from south to north** (UPSC 2012: both statements true).
[1:3] - **Peninsula:** rain **decreases from west to east** – away from the **Western Ghats**, not away from the Eastern Ghats.
[1:4] - **Rain-shadow** (leeward) areas: **east of the Western Ghats** – **Pune, Bengaluru**, interior Maharashtra (Vidarbha), Karnataka, Telangana, Rayalaseema; **Leh** behind the Himalaya. **Mangaluru, Mumbai** are **windward**.
[1:5] - The **western coastal plains** get more rain than the plateau because they lie on the **windward side of the SW monsoon** (orographic rain on the Western Ghats).
[1:6] - **Tropical deserts** lie in the **trade-wind belt on the western sides of continents**; the **Eastern Himalaya** gets most rain from the **SW (Bay) monsoon**, not from north-east winds.
[1:7] > A–R (IAS 1994): "monsoon rain decreases westward in the Ganga plain" is true; the reason given in the key ("winds go higher") differs from the textbook cause – progressive **loss of moisture**.
[1:8] ~~ Sources: NextGen Indian Geography 07 p.1-2, p.4-5 (book p.85-86, 88-89); PMF Indian Physical Geography 03 p.7, p.32; app notes 4:39 (sub 1, 2)
## SUB 2 | Drought – Types, Extent and Management
[2:0] TABLE head: Type | Based on
  [2:0.0] Meteorological | Rainfall deficit (lack of rain)
  [2:0.1] Agricultural | Soil moisture
  [2:0.2] Hydrological | Reduced streamflow, reservoirs, groundwater
  [2:0.3] Socio-economic | Demand exceeds supply
  [2:0.4] Flash drought | Sudden heat + no rain
[2:1] # Extent
[2:2] TABLE head: Category | Rainfall | Share
  [2:2.0] Drought-prone in all | – | ~68% of cultivable/agricultural land (nearest option: 70%)
  [2:2.1] Chronically drought-prone | < 750 mm | ~33%
  [2:2.2] Drought-prone | 750–1,125 mm | ~35%
[2:3] - Most frequent drought: **western India – Rajasthan, Gujarat, parts of Maharashtra**; also Marathwada, Rayalaseema, south Prakasam, north Karnataka.
[2:4] - **IMD:** an **all-India drought year** when the season's rainfall is **more than 10%** below normal (and over 20% of the area is affected).
[2:5] - **West Bengal plateau** (Purulia, Bankura): occasional drought with low, erratic rain on porous **lateritic** soil.
[2:6] - **Manual for Drought Management 2016** (Ministry of Agriculture; revised 2020) – multi-indicator declaration (rainfall deficit, vegetation, soil moisture, water).
[2:7] - **Disaster Management Act = 2005** (not 2006). DPAP 1973–74; the Green Revolution reduced famine relief. Kautilya's Arthashastra prescribes famine measures.

## SUB 4 | Flood-prone Areas
[4:0] - Largest floods: the **Ganga and Brahmaputra** basins (heavy monsoon rain, silt).
[4:1] - About **40 million ha ≈ 12%** of India's land is prone to **floods and river erosion** (Rashtriya Barh Ayog; NDMA; NIDM Annual Report 2023–24).
[4:2] - **Brahmaputra** = "sorrow of Assam" (not Bihar – that is the Kosi); cloudburst flash floods rising in the Himalaya and NE; ~37,760 sq km flood-prone area protected in West Bengal.
