=== Economic Systems – India's Mixed Economy
# Types of economy
| Type | Who owns / who decides | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Capitalist (market, free) | **Production and distribution in the hands of private individuals**; prices by market forces; **economic inequality** is a feature | USA |
| Socialist | **All productive resources owned and controlled by the government**; collective ownership, equitable distribution | Former USSR |
| Command (controlled, planned) | A **central plan replaces the market**; the government decides what, how and for whom | Former USSR, Cuba, North Korea |
| Traditional | **Custom and tradition** decide what, how and for whom | – |
| Gandhian | Decentralised, self-contained villages, cottage industries | **Gandhian Plan 1944 – S.N. Agarwal** |
| Mixed | **Coexistence of public and private sectors** | India, UK |
| Dual | Modern sector beside a traditional sector | Lewis model |
# India = mixed economy
- **After Independence India followed the path of a mixed economy** – public and private sectors coexist under State regulation; it is neither purely capitalist, socialist, Gandhian nor free (asked about 12 times: APPSC ASO 2017, AEs 2009, G2, Constable 2019, CDS 2019, UPPSC).
- **Meaning:** co-existence of **public and private sectors** – NOT co-existence of small and large industries, labour- and capital-intensive industries, national and foreign companies, agriculture and industry, or military and civilian rule.
- **How the State regulates** the private sector: **taxes, subsidies and credit**; a high level of public investment in infrastructure is a precondition for private-sector growth.
- **Socialistic pattern of society** (Avadi 1955, IPR 1956) was to come about **through the mixed economy** (APPSC JL 2004).
- The Indian model of development protects the interest of **both State and Person** (UP 2013).
- A **pragmatic middle path** between liberal-capitalist, Soviet and Gandhian models; growth by democratic means with a social order based on justice.
- **"Commanding heights"** under the State: **heavy industry, infrastructure and finance**; private sector in consumer goods under regulation.
- **Joint sector** = **one enterprise jointly owned** by government and private investors (a mixed economy is the whole system).
- **Public sector** = government ownership or control; **private sector** = private ownership or control.
# Planned, market, open and closed
- **Centrally planned economy:** the government makes all important decisions on production, exchange and consumption (UPPSC 2002 ✓); in a **market system** prices are set by demand and supply, **not agreed by the government** ✗ → **Only I**.
- **Closed economy:** **neither exports nor imports** take place (UPSC 2011); open economy trades with others.
> UPPSC 2024: "Indian economy is underdeveloped" ✓ (a developing economy with underdeveloped features) and "mixed" ✓; "Keynes is the Father of Economics" ✗ → **only 1 and 2**.

=== Features of a Developing (Underdeveloped) Economy
# The label
- India is a **developing economy** (APPSC G2 2003; UPPSC 1995, 2011) – not developed, backward or stagnant; it still shows **underdeveloped** features.
- India is categorised as a "developing country" mainly because of **low per capita income** – not the service-sector share, quality of manpower or lack of industrial growth.
# Features of the Indian economy
- **Low per capita income and unemployment** – the salient features; **massive unemployment**.
- **Predominance of agriculture:** the Indian economy is **primarily an agricultural economy**; **industrial dominance** marks a developed economy, not India (UP 2004: **I, III and IV**).
- **Low capital formation** (needs a high savings rate) and **low productivity of labour**.
- **Inequitable distribution of income, high dependency ratio, slower rate of increase in national income** – underdevelopment features (CGPSC 2017 key: all four listed items, incl. change in banking).
- **NOT a feature: lack of natural resources** – India has resources; they are under-utilised.
- **Indicators of a developed country:** high literacy, low death rate, high per capita income – a **high proportion of labour in the primary sector is NOT** one.
- **Dualistic economy** = **coexistence of a modern and a traditional economy** (not inequality, population growth or resource waste).
- Agriculture of underdeveloped countries has **surplus labour**; their trade = primary exports, manufactured imports.
# Causes of underdevelopment
- **Most important cause:** co-existence of **under-utilised manpower and under-utilised natural resources**.
- Recorded causes: rapid population growth with high dependency ratio, low human development (HDI rank about **130**), high poverty – **NOT mainly the working efficiency of the population** (APPSC G2 Mains 2017).
- **Vicious circle of poverty (Ragnar Nurkse):** low income → low saving → low investment → low productivity → low income; the vicious circle argument points out the **supply-side connection between productivity and income**.
- **Labour-surplus economy:** surplus farm labour whose marginal productivity is near zero.
- **Disguised unemployment:** more workers than needed; marginal productivity of the extra workers **zero**; removing them would **not** reduce output; mainly in **agriculture** and the unorganised sector.
- Capital formation in a developing economy can rise only with an adequate **savings rate**.

=== India at Independence and the Colonial Legacy
- **Colonial rule** (from **Plassey, 1757**) turned India from a textile exporter into a **raw-material supplier and captive market** – India was **NOT a supplier of manufactured goods** under British rule; the economy **stagnated** (UPPSC 2013).
- **Paul Bairoch:** India made about **24.5% of the world's manufactures in 1750** (APPSC G2 2017) → about **1.7% by 1900** (deindustrialisation).
- **Angus Maddison:** India's share of world GDP **22.6% (1700) → 3.8% (1952)**.
- **First National Income Committee (1951):** 1948-49 national income **₹8,710 crore**; per capita **₹225**.
- **Agriculture:** about **72% of the workforce (1951)**; about half of GDP.
- **Weak industrial base:** capital goods only **one-fourth** of industrial output.
- **Literacy below 16%**; **life expectancy 32 years** (1947).

=== Basic Concepts of Economics and Economists
| Name | Remember for |
|---|---|
| **Adam Smith** (Scottish) | **Father of (Modern) Economics** / of capitalism – *Wealth of Nations* (1776); not Keynes |
| J.M. Keynes | *General Theory*; modern macroeconomics; **paradox of thrift** – if all save more, income falls and total saving may not rise |
| **Joan Robinson** | ***The Accumulation of Capital* (1956)** |
| W. Arthur Lewis | Dual-sector model: surplus labour from the subsistence to the modern sector |
| **A.L. Bowley** | Averages = "**statistical constants which enable us to comprehend in a single effort the significance of the whole**" |
- **"Economics"** comes from the **Greek** *oikonomia* (oikos = household, nomos = management).
# Micro concepts asked
- **Fixed cost** does not vary with output – **expenditure on machines**; raw material, labour wages and traffic costs vary.
- **Perfect substitutes** = completely interchangeable goods; perfect complements are used together.
- **Giffen good:** inferior good; income and substitution effects oppose – demand can be inversely or positively related to price; if the **income effect is stronger**, demand moves **with** price → **both** statements true.
- **Total and marginal product:** TP rising at an increasing rate → MP rises; at a diminishing rate → MP falls; TP maximum → **MP zero**; TP falling → **MP negative** (not positive).
- **Law of increasing returns = law of decreasing cost.**
- **Marginal revenue:** MR > 0 → TR rises; **MR = 0 → TR maximum**; MR < 0 → TR falls (all three true).
- **Price elasticity of demand** depends on the **nature of the good** and the **availability of close substitutes**.
- **Economic models** simplify reality, represent a whole or part of a theory and show cause and effect; they can be expressed in words, diagrams, tables or equations – **not only equations**.
- **Unit root** is a concept of **time-series models** (Dickey-Fuller test): a series with a unit root is non-stationary – not of cross-section or structural-form models.

=== Ownership, Institutional Sectors and Savings
| Sector | Ownership |
|---|---|
| Public | Government (general government + public corporations) |
| Private | Private corporations + households |
| Joint | Government + private investors in one firm |
| Cooperative | **Owned by its members** |
- **Largest share of national income** in India's mixed economy: **private sector**.
- GVA is compiled for **four institutional sectors**: general government, public corporations, private corporations, households – **cooperatives are not a separate one**.
- **Gross domestic savings:** the **household sector** contributes the largest share (APPSC G1 2007) – household > private corporate > public.
- **SI 2017 match:** gross domestic savings (2011) **31.1%**; gross capital formation (2011) **36.6%**; unemployment rate (2004-05) **8.36%**; population growth (2001-11) **1.64%** a year → **A-IV, B-I, C-II, D-III**.
- **Private corporate investment:** **12.3% of GDP (FY23) → 11.2% (FY24)**; its share of GFCF at a decadal low of **33%** (Aug 2026).

=== Dual Economy – Organised vs Unorganised; Regional and Demographic Features
- **Organised sector:** formal, regulated work with **social security**; **unorganised:** informal, largely **cash-based**.
- Unorganised sector: **over 80% of workers**, about **45% of output**.
- **Census 2011:** population **121.09 crore**; **68.8% rural, 31.2% urban**; sex ratio **940** (933 in 2001).
- **Work participation rate 39.8%** (Census 2011) – higher in less developed States such as AP and Chhattisgarh because **subsistence activities need more manual work**.
- **Regional imbalance:** inter-state Gini of per capita income **0.230 (1994-95) → 0.261**; Telangana's PCI = **1.35 times** AP's (2024-25).
- **Demography:** TFR **1.9**, below the **replacement level 2.1**; median age about **36 by 2036**; dependency ratio expected to fall from **65% to 54%** over a decade; **demographic dividend peaks around 2030**.
