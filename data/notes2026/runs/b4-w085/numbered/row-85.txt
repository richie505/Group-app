## SUB 3 | Factors of Location, Weber and Other Theorists
[3:0] # Factors
[3:1] TABLE head: Factor | Pull | Example
  [3:1.0] Raw material (weight-losing) | Near ore or coalfield | Iron and steel, sugar, cement
  [3:1.1] Power | Near cheap power | Aluminium
  [3:1.2] Market | Near consumers or ports | Japan's market-based steel at the coast
  [3:1.3] Labour, transport, capital, policy | Skilled labour, ports, government support | Northern Italy, Pearl River Delta
[3:2] - **Steel**: about **4 tonnes** of inputs (iron ore, coking coal, limestone, dolomite) → **1 tonne** of steel, so plants sit on coalfields or ore fields.
[3:3] # Weber's Least-cost Theory (1909)
[3:4] - **Alfred Weber**: least-cost approach – firm locates where total **transport + labour + agglomeration** costs are least (OPSC 2018); Christaller = central places, not industry.
[3:5] TABLE head: Term | Meaning
  [3:5.0] Locational triangle | 2 raw-material sources + 1 market; least-transport-cost point lies inside it
  [3:5.1] Material Index (MI) | Weight of localised raw materials / weight of finished product
  [3:5.2] Isodapanes | Lines of equal extra transport cost
[3:6] - **MI > 1** → locate at raw material; **MI < 1** → at market.
[3:7] TABLE head: Material type | Meaning | Example
  [3:7.0] Ubiquities | Available everywhere | Water, air
  [3:7.1] Pure localised | Lose no weight | Cotton
  [3:7.2] Gross (weight-losing) | Lose weight in processing | Iron ore, coal, sugarcane
[3:8] - Cotton is non-weight-losing, so textile mills are NOT tied to raw-cotton sites.

## SUB 4 | Footloose, Sunrise, Umland and Agglomeration
[4:0] - **Footloose**: not tied to raw material or a fixed place; light, high-value, low transport cost, low pollution – **diamond cutting**, electronics, software, watch-making.
[4:1] - **Sunrise industries**: new, fast-growing sectors – **information technology**, electronics, **hospitality**/tourism; iron and steel and textiles are old ("sunset") industries.
[4:2] - **Umland**: the **area surrounding a town**, linked to it by economic and cultural activities; term first used in **1883**.
[4:3] - **Agglomeration economies**: cost savings when firms cluster – shared infrastructure, specialised suppliers, skilled labour pool. They do NOT tie footloose industries to raw materials.
[4:4] - **Industrial inertia**: a firm stays put after the original advantage is gone (Ruhr, Pittsburgh, Lancashire).

## SUB 5 | Industrial Revolution and the Shift to Asia
[5:0] - **Industrial Revolution** began in **England (Britain)** c. **1750** – coal and iron, canals and rail, indented coast and rivers.
[5:1] - **NCERT trap (NDA 2011):** from the 1780s it was the **cotton** industry, not iron, that symbolised British industrialisation.
[5:2] - **Steel**: **China 1st** (over 50% of world steel); **India 2nd**.
[5:3] - **Cotton textiles**: **China** is the leading producer.
[5:4] - **Shipbuilding (2025 new orders)**: China first (about 60–66%), South Korea second (about 20%), Japan third.
[5:5] - India: Rourkela = first public-sector plant (German help); VISL Bhadravati = oldest public-sector steel plant in the south.
[5:6] - **Shift to Asia**: old Western regions (Ruhr, Lorraine, US Rust Belt, English Midlands) declined; China and India lead.
[5:7] - **Asian Tigers** (newly industrialising): South Korea, Taiwan, Singapore, Hong Kong.
[5:8] - Revised Index of Core Industries, base **2022-23**; iron ore added → **nine** industries (July 2026).
[5:9] - India steel: 168.4 MT crude steel (2025-26); capacity about 220 MT; target **300 MT by 2030**.
