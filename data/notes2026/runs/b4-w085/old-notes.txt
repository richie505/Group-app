## SUB 3 | Industrial Revolution and the Shift to Asia
[85:3:0] - **Industrial Revolution** began in **England (Britain)** c. **1750** – coal and iron, canals and rail, indented coast and rivers.
[85:3:1] - **NCERT trap (NDA 2011):** from the 1780s it was the **cotton** industry, not iron, that symbolised British industrialisation.
[85:3:2] - **Steel**: **China 1st** (over 50% of world steel); **India 2nd**.
[85:3:3] - **Cotton textiles**: **China** is the leading producer.
[85:3:4] - **Shipbuilding (2025 new orders)**: China first (about 60–66%), South Korea second (about 20%), Japan third.
[85:3:5] - India: Rourkela = first public-sector plant (German help); VISL Bhadravati = oldest public-sector steel plant in the south.
[85:3:6] - **Shift to Asia**: old Western regions (Ruhr, Lorraine, US Rust Belt, English Midlands) declined; China and India lead.
[85:3:7] - **Asian Tigers** (newly industrialising): South Korea, Taiwan, Singapore, Hong Kong.
[85:3:8] - Revised Index of Core Industries, base **2022-23**; iron ore added → **nine** industries (July 2026).
[85:3:9] - India steel: 168.4 MT crude steel (2025-26); capacity about 220 MT; target **300 MT by 2030**.

## SUB 4 | Footloose, Sunrise, Umland and Agglomeration
[85:4:0] - **Footloose**: not tied to raw material or a fixed place; light, high-value, low transport cost, low pollution – **diamond cutting**, electronics, software, watch-making.
[85:4:1] - **Sunrise industries**: new, fast-growing sectors – **information technology**, electronics, **hospitality**/tourism; iron and steel and textiles are old ("sunset") industries.
[85:4:2] - **Umland**: the **area surrounding a town**, linked to it by economic and cultural activities; term first used in **1883**.
[85:4:3] - **Agglomeration economies**: cost savings when firms cluster – shared infrastructure, specialised suppliers, skilled labour pool. They do NOT tie footloose industries to raw materials.
[85:4:4] - **Industrial inertia**: a firm stays put after the original advantage is gone (Ruhr, Pittsburgh, Lancashire).

## SUB 5 | Factors of Location, Weber and Other Theorists
[85:5:0] # Factors
[85:5:1] TABLE head: Factor | Pull | Example
  [85:5:1.0] Raw material (weight-losing) | Near ore or coalfield | Iron and steel, sugar, cement
  [85:5:1.1] Power | Near cheap power | Aluminium
  [85:5:1.2] Market | Near consumers or ports | Japan's market-based steel at the coast
  [85:5:1.3] Labour, transport, capital, policy | Skilled labour, ports, government support | Northern Italy, Pearl River Delta
[85:5:2] - **Steel**: about **4 tonnes** of inputs (iron ore, coking coal, limestone, dolomite) → **1 tonne** of steel, so plants sit on coalfields or ore fields.
[85:5:3] # Weber's Least-cost Theory (1909)
[85:5:4] - **Alfred Weber**: least-cost approach – firm locates where total **transport + labour + agglomeration** costs are least (OPSC 2018); Christaller = central places, not industry.
[85:5:5] TABLE head: Term | Meaning
  [85:5:5.0] Locational triangle | 2 raw-material sources + 1 market; least-transport-cost point lies inside it
  [85:5:5.1] Material Index (MI) | Weight of localised raw materials / weight of finished product
  [85:5:5.2] Isodapanes | Lines of equal extra transport cost
[85:5:6] - **MI > 1** → locate at raw material; **MI < 1** → at market.
[85:5:7] TABLE head: Material type | Meaning | Example
  [85:5:7.0] Ubiquities | Available everywhere | Water, air
  [85:5:7.1] Pure localised | Lose no weight | Cotton
  [85:5:7.2] Gross (weight-losing) | Lose weight in processing | Iron ore, coal, sugarcane
[85:5:8] - Cotton is non-weight-losing, so textile mills are NOT tied to raw-cotton sites.
[85:5:9] # Other theorists
[85:5:10] TABLE head: Theorist | Idea
  [85:5:10.0] Alfred Weber | Least cost
  [85:5:10.1] Edgar Hoover | Transport-cost structure, terminal costs; favours ports and break-of-bulk points
  [85:5:10.2] Walter Isard | Substitution principle
  [85:5:10.3] August Losch | Maximum-profit / market-area approach
  [85:5:10.4] P. Sargant Florence | Location Quotient

## SUB 1 | Locational Factors of Steel Plants
[87:1:0] - Raw materials are **weight-losing**, so plants sit on the **Chota Nagpur** coal-ore crescent (Jharkhand, N. Odisha, Chhattisgarh, W. Bengal).
[87:1:1] - **Near coalfields:** Bokaro, Durgapur, Kulti-Burnpur; **near iron ore:** Bhadravati, Bhilai, Rourkela.
[87:1:2] - **Far from coalfields:** **Bhadravati** (Kemmangundi ore; charcoal, then Sharavati/Jog hydel power).
[87:1:3] - **Midway** between ore (Noamundi, Gorumahisani) and coal (Jharia): **Jamshedpur**.
[87:1:4] - **Not on a riverside:** **Bhilai** (Tandula dam water); Bokaro (Damodar), Jamshedpur (Subarnarekha–Kharkai), Bhadravati (Bhadra) are.
[87:1:5] - **West-coast plants** (Ratnagiri, Mangalore, Dolvi, Hazira): exam reason = **high-grade iron ore of Goa** plus ports for imported coking coal and export.
[87:1:6] - **Coking coal** is the input India must **import** (UPSC 2015) – domestic coking coal is **high-ash**; not saltpetre or rock phosphate.
[87:1:7] - Plants put away from raw materials for regional balance: **Salem, Visakhapatnam, Vijayanagar**.
[87:1:8] - **Bokaro–Rourkela combine**: Bokaro gets iron ore from Rourkela and sends back coal.
[87:1:9] ~~ Sources: PMF Human & Economic Geography 05 p.11, 16-22, 25; app notes 4:87 sub 2
[87:1:10] - **Bhilai** gets iron ore from **Dalli-Rajhara**, coal from **Korba** and Kargali, water from **Tandula dam**.
[87:1:11] - Coastal plants (Visakhapatnam, Hazira, Dolvi) import **coking coal mainly from Australia** (also Indonesia, USA, Russia); iron ore imports from Australia and Brazil.
[87:1:12] - **Jharia** (Jharkhand) is a **coalfield**, not a steel centre; Jamshedpur, Durgapur and Bhilai have steel plants.

=== NOTES 4:88 | India – Cotton Textiles, Jute, Sugar, Cement, Paper, Fertiliser and Petrochemical Industries
## SUB 0 | Cotton Textiles – Oldest Industry, Centres, Nicknames and Location
[88:0:0] # History and status
[88:0:1] - **Oldest / ancient industry of India:** **cotton textiles** (muslin, calico, chintz were famous worldwide) – older than jute, tea or sugar, which are modern mill industries (UPPSC 2004 twice).
[88:0:2] - **First cotton mill:** **1818, Fort Gloster near Kolkata (Calcutta)** – a commercial failure (UPUDA 2013; Jharkhand 2013).
[88:0:3] - **First successful mill:** **1854, Mumbai**, by Parsi entrepreneur **C.N. Davar** (local capital); growth surged after the American Civil War (1860s-70s).
[88:0:4] - **Only major industry in Indian hands at the end of the 19th century:** **cotton textiles** – jute, mining and tea were British-owned; Indian-owned steel began only with TISCO (1907).
[88:0:5] - **Largest organised modern industry of India:** cotton textiles; three tiers – handspun khadi, handloom/powerloom, and the capital-intensive mill sector (powerlooms now make most cloth).
[88:0:6] # Location and dispersal
[88:0:7] - Began in the **black-soil cotton belt of Maharashtra and Gujarat**; **Bombay–Ahmedabad** = the most prominent cotton textile region (APPSC G-I 2003).
[88:0:8] - **Dispersed** all over India because raw cotton is a **pure raw material that does not lose weight** in manufacture, so mills followed markets, ports, power and cheap labour (Kanpur, Kolkata, Coimbatore) (RAS 2013 A-R: both correct).
[88:0:9] - **Tamil Nadu – mill-made yarn:** hydel power, **rich pool of skilled/cheap labour**, local cotton; **black cotton soil is NOT predominant** in TN (UPSC 2010: only II).
[88:0:10] - **Largest number of mills:** **Tamil Nadu** (most produce yarn, Coimbatore has nearly half) – older books and keys say **Maharashtra** (Mumbai the largest centre).
[88:0:11] - **Not a cotton region:** **Dhanbad–Jamshedpur** = coal and steel; Mumbai–Pune, Madurai–Coimbatore, Indore–Ujjain are cotton regions (NDA 2017).
[88:0:12] - **Raw cotton exports:** India's largest buyers are **Bangladesh and China**, not South Korea.

## SUB 1 | Sugar Industry – Producers, Belts, North–South Shift and Policy
[88:1:0] # World and India
[88:1:1] - **Largest producer:** **Brazil**; India **2nd** (~19%) and **largest consumer**; India overtook Brazil only in **2021-22** (first time in 16 years). Brazil is also the largest exporter.

## SUB 5 | Jute – Hooghly Belt, Retting and Andhra Mills
[88:5:0] - **First jute mill:** **Rishra, near Kolkata, 1855**; jute is India's 2nd textile industry after cotton.
[88:5:1] - **Location:** ~**90%** of capacity in a **100 km x 3 km belt along the Hooghly** – most mills in the **Hugli basin**, **West Bengal** (APPSC G-I 2024).
[88:5:2] - **Why Hooghly:** raw jute of the delta, **soft/standing water for retting**, NW-1 and **Kolkata port**, coal from Raniganj, cheap labour, capital.
[88:5:3] - **Retting:** soaking jute stems in water so microbes loosen the fibre – the process that separates fibre from the stem.
[88:5:4] - **Mills:** WB 71 of 97 (2026); **AP 2nd** – Chittivalasa, Nellimarla; **Bhajrang Jute Mill, Guntur** – shut down, a long labour/management dispute.
[88:5:5] - **Bhatpara** = jute centre (Titagarh = paper). **National Jute Policy 2005**; **JUTE-ICARE** = certified seed + microbial retting with **SONA**.
[88:5:6] - **Largest exporter:** Bangladesh. **Jute Packaging Materials Act 1987:** 100% foodgrains, 20% sugar in jute bags.

=== NOTES 4:90 | Classification of Basic Industries – Agro, Mineral, Forest, Fuel and Manpower-based
## SUB 0 | Classification by Raw Material – Agro, Mineral, Forest, Animal, Marine
[90:0:0] TABLE head: Class | Features | Examples
  [90:0:0.0] Agro-based | Farm produce; near raw material (perishable, weight-losing) | Cotton textile, jute, sugar, vanaspati, cigarettes, tea, paper (bagasse, bamboo)
  [90:0:0.1] Mineral-based | Capital-intensive; near mines | Iron and steel, aluminium, cement, copper smelting
  [90:0:0.2] Forest-based | Timber + minor forest produce | Pulp and paper (largest), furniture, plywood, matchsticks, lac, rayon
  [90:0:0.3] Animal-based | Animal produce | Dairy, leather, wool
  [90:0:0.4] Marine-based | Sea produce | Seafood and coastal aquaculture processing
[90:0:1] - **Cement is NOT agro-based** (limestone = mineral) (APPSC G-I 2008).
[90:0:2] - **Rayon = "artificial silk"**, made from **wood pulp** (cellulose) (APPSC G-I 2002).
[90:0:3] - **Pollutant–industry:** **bleaching liquor – paper and pulp** ✓; cellulose fibres are not a pharma pollutant, drugs/antibiotics not a mining pollutant (SSC 2017: only A).
[90:0:4] - **Leather:** UP State Leather Development and Marketing Corporation – **Agra** (printed key). AP: Nellore – leather, auto parts.
[90:0:5] ~~ Sources: OnlyIAS NCERT Human Geography Part 7 p.13; app notes 4:90 sub 0, 2
## SUB 1 | Paper, Pulp and Timber – World Leaders
[90:1:0] - **Pulp and paper "most developed":** **USA and Canada** (softwood, hydel power, water); **Canada** exports wood pulp to the USA, Europe, Asia. The APPSC 2002 question was keyed both ways (Canada; **Mexico**).
[90:1:1] - **Soft timber and wood pulp (when asked):** **Canada** largest producer and exporter. **Now (FAO 2022):** pulp – producer **USA (23.5%)**, exporter **Brazil (29%)**; sawn wood exporter **Russia (20%)**, Canada 2nd.
[90:1:2] - **Pulp woods:** **poplar** (woody), also eucalyptus, bamboo; bagasse and rice straw are non-woody. Recycled fibre 71-75% of raw material.
[90:1:3] - Paper is **capital-intensive**; top paper exporters Finland, Sweden, Canada; China = largest furniture exporter; rayon led by China, India, Indonesia. India per capita paper use ~16 kg (world 57).
[90:1:4] ~~ Sources: PMF Human & Economic Geography Part 5 p.86, p.91; app notes 4:90 sub 7
[90:1:5] - **Timber seasoning:** **kiln seasoning** = heated air in a closed chamber (quick, controlled); air seasoning = open, shaded stacks; chemical seasoning = salt solutions.
## SUB 2 | Fuel-based and Manpower-based Industries; Water and Coal Use
[90:2:0] TABLE head: Class | Basis | Examples
  [90:2:0.0] Fuel-based (energy-based) | Coal, petroleum, gas, nuclear | Thermal power, oil refining, petrochemicals, gas-based fertilisers
  [90:2:0.1] Manpower-based (labour-intensive) | High human labour | Handloom, handicrafts, leather, garments
[90:2:1] - **Maximum industrial water consumer = thermal power** (~**88%**); engineering ~5%, paper ~2.3%, textiles ~2% (UPSC 2013).
[90:2:2] - **Maximum coal use = energy (power) production**, not steel, railways or ships.
[90:2:3] - AP: Mallavalli (Krishna) Mega Food Park; Prakasam, Kurnool – mineral-based.
