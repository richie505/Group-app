## SUB 0 | Economic Systems – India's Mixed Economy
[0:0] # Types of economy
[0:1] TABLE head: Type | Who owns / who decides | Example
  [0:1.0] Capitalist (market, free) | Production and distribution in the hands of private individuals; prices by market forces; economic inequality is a feature | USA
  [0:1.1] Socialist | All productive resources owned and controlled by the government; collective ownership, equitable distribution | Former USSR
  [0:1.2] Command (controlled, planned) | A central plan replaces the market; the government decides what, how and for whom | Former USSR, Cuba, North Korea
  [0:1.3] Traditional | Custom and tradition decide what, how and for whom | –
  [0:1.4] Gandhian | Decentralised, self-contained villages, cottage industries | Gandhian Plan 1944 – S.N. Agarwal
  [0:1.5] Mixed | Coexistence of public and private sectors | India, UK
  [0:1.6] Dual | Modern sector beside a traditional sector | Lewis model
[0:2] # India = mixed economy
[0:3] - **After Independence India followed the path of a mixed economy** – public and private sectors coexist under State regulation; it is neither purely capitalist, socialist, Gandhian nor free (asked about 12 times: APPSC ASO 2017, AEs 2009, G2, Constable 2019, CDS 2019, UPPSC).
[0:4] - **Meaning:** co-existence of **public and private sectors** – NOT co-existence of small and large industries, labour- and capital-intensive industries, national and foreign companies, agriculture and industry, or military and civilian rule.
[0:5] - **How the State regulates** the private sector: **taxes, subsidies and credit**; a high level of public investment in infrastructure is a precondition for private-sector growth.
[0:6] - **Socialistic pattern of society** (Avadi 1955, IPR 1956) was to come about **through the mixed economy** (APPSC JL 2004).
[0:7] - The Indian model of development protects the interest of **both State and Person** (UP 2013).
[0:8] - A **pragmatic middle path** between liberal-capitalist, Soviet and Gandhian models; growth by democratic means with a social order based on justice.
[0:9] - **"Commanding heights"** under the State: **heavy industry, infrastructure and finance**; private sector in consumer goods under regulation.
[0:10] - **Joint sector** = **one enterprise jointly owned** by government and private investors (a mixed economy is the whole system).
[0:11] - **Public sector** = government ownership or control; **private sector** = private ownership or control.
[0:12] # Planned, market, open and closed
[0:13] - **Centrally planned economy:** the government makes all important decisions on production, exchange and consumption (UPPSC 2002 ✓); in a **market system** prices are set by demand and supply, **not agreed by the government** ✗ → **Only I**.
[0:14] - **Closed economy:** **neither exports nor imports** take place (UPSC 2011); open economy trades with others.
[0:15] > UPPSC 2024: "Indian economy is underdeveloped" ✓ (a developing economy with underdeveloped features) and "mixed" ✓; "Keynes is the Father of Economics" ✗ → **only 1 and 2**.
[0:16] ~~ Sources: NextGen Indian Economy Part 1 - APPSC 02 p.7-8 (types of economy); NextGen Indian Economy Part 1 - APPSC 04 p.29-30; Rocket Indian Economy #57 p.85; app notes 3:0 sub 0
## SUB 1 | Features of a Developing (Underdeveloped) Economy
[1:0] # The label
[1:1] - India is a **developing economy** (APPSC G2 2003; UPPSC 1995, 2011) – not developed, backward or stagnant; it still shows **underdeveloped** features.
[1:2] - India is categorised as a "developing country" mainly because of **low per capita income** – not the service-sector share, quality of manpower or lack of industrial growth.
[1:3] # Features of the Indian economy
[1:4] - **Low per capita income and unemployment** – the salient features; **massive unemployment**.
[1:5] - **Predominance of agriculture:** the Indian economy is **primarily an agricultural economy**; **industrial dominance** marks a developed economy, not India (UP 2004: **I, III and IV**).
[1:6] - **Low capital formation** (needs a high savings rate) and **low productivity of labour**.
[1:7] - **Inequitable distribution of income, high dependency ratio, slower rate of increase in national income** – underdevelopment features (CGPSC 2017 key: all four listed items, incl. change in banking).
[1:8] - **NOT a feature: lack of natural resources** – India has resources; they are under-utilised.
[1:9] - **Indicators of a developed country:** high literacy, low death rate, high per capita income – a **high proportion of labour in the primary sector is NOT** one.
[1:10] - **Dualistic economy** = **coexistence of a modern and a traditional economy** (not inequality, population growth or resource waste).
[1:11] - Agriculture of underdeveloped countries has **surplus labour**; their trade = primary exports, manufactured imports.
[1:12] # Causes of underdevelopment
[1:13] - **Most important cause:** co-existence of **under-utilised manpower and under-utilised natural resources**.
[1:14] - Recorded causes: rapid population growth with high dependency ratio, low human development (HDI rank about **130**), high poverty – **NOT mainly the working efficiency of the population** (APPSC G2 Mains 2017).
[1:15] - **Vicious circle of poverty (Ragnar Nurkse):** low income → low saving → low investment → low productivity → low income; the vicious circle argument points out the **supply-side connection between productivity and income**.
[1:16] - **Labour-surplus economy:** surplus farm labour whose marginal productivity is near zero.
[1:17] - **Disguised unemployment:** more workers than needed; marginal productivity of the extra workers **zero**; removing them would **not** reduce output; mainly in **agriculture** and the unorganised sector.
[1:18] - Capital formation in a developing economy can rise only with an adequate **savings rate**.
[1:19] ~~ Sources: NextGen Indian Economy Part 1 - APPSC 04 p.29-30; NextGen Indian Economy Part 1 - APPSC 02 p.43 (vicious circle); Rocket Indian Economy #36 p.53, #33 p.49; app notes 3:0 subs 1-3
## SUB 2 | Basic Concepts of Economics and Economists
[2:0] TABLE head: Name | Remember for
  [2:0.0] Adam Smith (Scottish) | Father of (Modern) Economics / of capitalism – *Wealth of Nations* (1776); not Keynes
  [2:0.1] J.M. Keynes | *General Theory*; modern macroeconomics; paradox of thrift – if all save more, income falls and total saving may not rise
  [2:0.2] Joan Robinson | *The Accumulation of Capital* (1956)
  [2:0.3] W. Arthur Lewis | Dual-sector model: surplus labour from the subsistence to the modern sector
  [2:0.4] A.L. Bowley | Averages = "statistical constants which enable us to comprehend in a single effort the significance of the whole"
[2:1] - **"Economics"** comes from the **Greek** *oikonomia* (oikos = household, nomos = management).
[2:2] # Micro concepts asked
[2:3] - **Fixed cost** does not vary with output – **expenditure on machines**; raw material, labour wages and traffic costs vary.
[2:4] - **Perfect substitutes** = completely interchangeable goods; perfect complements are used together.
[2:5] - **Giffen good:** inferior good; income and substitution effects oppose – demand can be inversely or positively related to price; if the **income effect is stronger**, demand moves **with** price → **both** statements true.
[2:6] - **Total and marginal product:** TP rising at an increasing rate → MP rises; at a diminishing rate → MP falls; TP maximum → **MP zero**; TP falling → **MP negative** (not positive).
[2:7] - **Law of increasing returns = law of decreasing cost.**
[2:8] - **Marginal revenue:** MR > 0 → TR rises; **MR = 0 → TR maximum**; MR < 0 → TR falls (all three true).
[2:9] - **Price elasticity of demand** depends on the **nature of the good** and the **availability of close substitutes**.
[2:10] - **Economic models** simplify reality, represent a whole or part of a theory and show cause and effect; they can be expressed in words, diagrams, tables or equations – **not only equations**.
[2:11] - **Unit root** is a concept of **time-series models** (Dickey-Fuller test): a series with a unit root is non-stationary – not of cross-section or structural-form models.
[2:12] ~~ Sources: NextGen Indian Economy Part 1 - APPSC 04 p.30 (Adam Smith); Rocket Indian Economy #36 p.53 (economists); en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_root (Dickey & Fuller 1979); NCERT Class 12 Introductory Microeconomics ch.2-4; app notes 3:0 sub 3
## SUB 3 | India at Independence and the Colonial Legacy
[3:0] - **Colonial rule** (from **Plassey, 1757**) turned India from a textile exporter into a **raw-material supplier and captive market** – India was **NOT a supplier of manufactured goods** under British rule; the economy **stagnated** (UPPSC 2013).
[3:1] - **Paul Bairoch:** India made about **24.5% of the world's manufactures in 1750** (APPSC G2 2017) → about **1.7% by 1900** (deindustrialisation).
[3:2] - **Angus Maddison:** India's share of world GDP **22.6% (1700) → 3.8% (1952)**.
[3:3] - **First National Income Committee (1951):** 1948-49 national income **₹8,710 crore**; per capita **₹225**.
[3:4] - **Agriculture:** about **72% of the workforce (1951)**; about half of GDP.
[3:5] - **Weak industrial base:** capital goods only **one-fourth** of industrial output.
[3:6] - **Literacy below 16%**; **life expectancy 32 years** (1947).
[3:7] ~~ Sources: NextGen Indian Economy Part 1 - APPSC 04 p.30; P. Bairoch, "International Industrialization Levels from 1750 to 1980" (J. European Econ. Hist. 1982) via en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_history_of_India; app notes 3:0 sub 3, 3:1 sub 6

## SUB 5 | Dual Economy – Organised vs Unorganised; Regional and Demographic Features
[5:0] - **Organised sector:** formal, regulated work with **social security**; **unorganised:** informal, largely **cash-based**.
[5:1] - Unorganised sector: **over 80% of workers**, about **45% of output**.
[5:2] - **Census 2011:** population **121.09 crore**; **68.8% rural, 31.2% urban**; sex ratio **940** (933 in 2001).
[5:3] - **Work participation rate 39.8%** (Census 2011) – higher in less developed States such as AP and Chhattisgarh because **subsistence activities need more manual work**.
[5:4] - **Regional imbalance:** inter-state Gini of per capita income **0.230 (1994-95) → 0.261**; Telangana's PCI = **1.35 times** AP's (2024-25).
[5:5] - **Demography:** TFR **1.9**, below the **replacement level 2.1**; median age about **36 by 2036**; dependency ratio expected to fall from **65% to 54%** over a decade; **demographic dividend peaks around 2030**.

## SUB 7 | Micro Basics – Demand, Utility, Elasticity and Consumer Choice
[7:0] # Definitions and laws
[7:1] - **Lionel Robbins** (1932): economics studies the relationship between **unlimited wants (ends) and scarce means** with alternative uses (scarcity definition).
[7:2] - **Behavioural economics** applies **psychological insights** into human behaviour to explain economic decisions (Kahneman, Thaler).
[7:3] - **Saturation point:** total utility is at its maximum and **marginal utility is zero** (beyond it MU turns negative).
[7:4] - **Consumer equilibrium (two goods):** MUx/Px = MUy/Py – if the MU of the last unit of X is twice that of Y, equilibrium needs the **price of X to be twice the price of Y**.
[7:5] - **Budget line:** a point **below (inside)** the budget line costs less than income – the consumer is **not spending all income**; a point on the line spends it all.
[7:6] # Demand
[7:7] - **Normal goods:** demand rises with income (positive income effect); **inferior goods:** demand falls as income rises.
[7:8] - The demand curve **slopes downward** and is usually drawn **convex to the origin**.
[7:9] - **Demand for a good rises** when the **price of a substitute rises**; it falls when the price of a complement rises or (for an inferior good) when income rises; a fall in its own price raises the quantity demanded (UPSC key: **1 and 4 only**).
[7:10] - **Supply curve of a farmer:** everything is held constant **except the price of the commodity itself** (that is the variable on the curve).
[7:11] # Elasticity
[7:12] TABLE head: Case | Meaning
  [7:12.0] Zero price elasticity | Quantity demanded does not change at all whatever the price change (vertical demand curve)
  [7:12.1] Zero cross elasticity | Purchases of one good do not change when the other's price changes – unrelated (independent) goods
  [7:12.2] Positive / negative cross elasticity | Substitutes / complements
  [7:12.3] Tangent demand curves | A straight-line and a curved demand curve tangent at a point have the same elasticity there
[7:13] - **Consumer surplus** (Marshall) – what consumers would pay minus what they pay; used in **tax policy** (burden of commodity taxes), pricing of public utilities.
[7:14] ~~ Sources: OnlyIAS NCERT Economy 03 p.14-21 (NCERT Class XII Introductory Microeconomics ch.2: budget line, normal and inferior goods, elasticity); NextGen Indian Economy Part 1 - APPSC 02 p.47; L. Robbins, An Essay on the Nature and Significance of Economic Science (1932); D. Salvatore, Schaum's Outline of Microeconomic Theory ch.2-4
## SUB 8 | Micro Basics – Production, Costs and Factor Pricing
[8:0] # Production
[8:1] - **Law of diminishing returns:** adding more units of a variable factor to a fixed factor → **marginal product eventually diminishes**.
[8:2] - **Constant returns to scale** need **all inputs** raised in the same proportion; raising **labour alone by 10%** with capital fixed raises output by **less than 10%** (diminishing returns).
[8:3] - **CES production function:** the **elasticity of substitution** between inputs is **constant** (Cobb-Douglas is the special case with elasticity 1).
[8:4] # Costs
[8:5] TABLE head: Cost | Meaning / example
  [8:5.0] Explicit cost | Money actually paid to buy or hire factors of production
  [8:5.1] Implicit / opportunity cost | Return forgone on own resources – e.g. the interest he could earn by lending out his own funds
  [8:5.2] Fixed cost (farm) | Does not vary with output – land rent, depreciation, interest on fixed capital (seeds, fertiliser, hired labour are variable)
  [8:5.3] AFC | Falls continuously (rectangular hyperbola) – the only cost curve that is not U-shaped (AVC, ATC, MC are U-shaped)
[8:6] - **LAC falls as output expands** because of **economies of scale**.
[8:7] - **Increasing cost industry:** factor prices rise as the industry uses more factors (prices and quantities move in the **same direction**).
[8:8] - **Cash** = notes, coins, bank balances and cash equivalents; long-term **investments** are not cash.
[8:9] # Factor pricing
[8:10] - **Value of marginal product (VMP) of labour** = **marginal product of labour × price** of output.
[8:11] - **Quasi-rent** (Marshall) = total revenue − total variable cost = fixed cost + profit → **greater than the firm's total profit**.
[8:12] ~~ Sources: OnlyIAS NCERT Economy 03 p.28-35 (NCERT Class XII Microeconomics ch.3: production function, returns, cost curves); D. Salvatore, Schaum's Outline of Microeconomic Theory ch.6-7, 13; A. Koutsoyiannis, Modern Microeconomics ch.3-4 (CES, quasi-rent)
## SUB 9 | Micro Basics – Market Structures, Oligopoly and Game Theory
[9:0] # Competition and monopoly
[9:1] - **Perfect market:** **large number of buyers and sellers**, homogeneous product, free entry and exit, perfect knowledge.
[9:2] - **Monopolistic competition and monopoly** share a **downward-sloping demand curve**; in long-run equilibrium the monopolistic competitor **never** produces at the lowest point of LAC (tangency on the falling part → excess capacity).
[9:3] - A pure monopolist keeps **pure profits in the long run** because of **blocked entry**.
[9:4] - When a competitive industry becomes a monopoly, part of **consumer surplus** goes to the monopolist as **economic profit** (the rest is deadweight loss).
[9:5] - **Contestable markets** (Baumol): even a few firms behave competitively **if entry is absolutely free and exit entirely costless**.
[9:6] # Duopoly models
[9:7] TABLE head: Model | Each firm assumes the rival keeps … | Note
  [9:7.0] Cournot | Its quantity (output) constant | Interdependence not recognised; stable solution
  [9:7.1] Bertrand | Its price constant | Price war down to competitive price
  [9:7.2] Edgeworth | Its price constant | Price oscillates – no stable solution
[9:8] # Game theory
[9:9] - **Zero-sum game:** one player's gain = the other's loss.
[9:10] - **Maximin:** choose the strategy that **maximises the minimum gain** (minimax = minimise the maximum loss).
[9:11] ~~ Sources: OnlyIAS NCERT Economy 03 p.47-60 (perfect competition, non-competitive markets); NextGen Indian Economy Part 1 - APPSC 02 p.47, p.55 (monopolistic competition); D. Salvatore, Schaum's Outline of Microeconomic Theory ch.9-11; A. Koutsoyiannis, Modern Microeconomics ch.9 (Cournot, Bertrand, Edgeworth)
## SUB 10 | Micro Basics – Welfare Economics, General Equilibrium and Linear Programming
[10:0] # Pareto and general equilibrium
[10:1] - **Pareto's welfare analysis** relates to the production function and **utility** (no one can be made better off without making someone worse off).
[10:2] - **General equilibrium of production and exchange** (2 persons, 2 goods): **MRT(xy) = MRS(xy) of A = MRS(xy) of B**.
[10:3] - **Consumption (exchange) contract curve** = locus of general-equilibrium points of exchange.
[10:4] - **Grand utility-possibility curve** = locus of Pareto optimality in **both production and consumption**.
[10:5] - **Social welfare function** needs an **ethical (value) judgement** – Pareto optimality alone cannot rank its points.
[10:6] - **Productive efficiency** = **factor-price (allocative) efficiency + technical efficiency**.
[10:7] # Shadow prices and LP
[10:8] - **Shadow (accounting) prices** = imputed values of costs and benefits, used in public-sector project appraisal as a **sound basis for allocation decisions**.
[10:9] - In **linear programming** the shadow price = **change in the objective function per unit change in a constraint**; LP assumes linearity, i.e. **constant returns** – increasing returns to scale is **not** an assumption.
[10:10] ~~ Sources: D. Salvatore, Schaum's Outline of Microeconomic Theory ch.14-15 (general equilibrium, welfare); A. Koutsoyiannis, Modern Microeconomics ch.22-23; Little-Mirrlees / UNIDO project appraisal (shadow prices)
