## SUB 0 | Functions of the Environment, Absorptive and Carrying Capacity
[0:0] TABLE head: Function | Meaning
  [0:0.0] Supplies resources (first function) | Both renewable (trees, fish – replaced naturally or inexhaustible) and non-renewable (fossil fuels – exhausted with use)
  [0:0.1] Assimilates waste | Absorbs and neutralises wastes
  [0:0.2] Sustains life | Genetic and biodiversity
  [0:0.3] Aesthetic services | Scenery and amenity
[0:1] - **Renewable resources** = those that **can be replaced naturally over time or are inexhaustible** – used without the resource becoming depleted.
[0:2] - **Absorptive capacity** = ability of the environment to absorb degradation = the **maximum amount of pollutants an ecosystem can process and neutralise without harmful effects**.
[0:3] - **Carrying capacity** (NCERT): the environment works without interruption as long as **resource extraction is not above the rate of regeneration** and **wastes are within the assimilating capacity** of the environment.
[0:4] - **Crisis**: when **extraction > regeneration** and waste > absorptive capacity, the environment **cannot sustain life** – "we are today at the threshold of environmental crisis".
[0:5] - **Recycling** is NOT a function of the environment; "supplies renewable resources only" is wrong.
[0:6] ~~ Sources: NextGen Indian Economy Part 1 - APPSC 03 p.52 (functions, renewable resources, absorptive capacity); NCERT Indian Economic Development Class 11 ch.9; app notes 3:19 sub 0
## SUB 1 | Resource Depletion and Causes of Environmental Degradation
[1:0] - **Resource depletion** = use faster than regeneration; **overuse of natural resources → resource depletion** (not conservation or sustainability).
[1:1] - Best illustration of depletion with long-term impact: **overfishing in a coastal area** harming the whole marine ecosystem and livelihoods. A forest managed **sustainably for timber while keeping biodiversity** is NOT depletion.
[1:2] TABLE head: Resource | Cause of depletion
  [1:2.0] Groundwater | Deep bore tubewells for irrigation
  [1:2.1] Topsoil | Brick production
  [1:2.2] Biodiversity habitats | Expansion of agriculture
[1:3] - **Causes vs conservation practices:**
[1:4] TABLE head: Human-driven causes | NOT causes (conservation)
  [1:4.0] Deforestation (primary cause) | Sustainable agriculture
  [1:4.1] Chemical-intensive farming | Renewable energy production
  [1:4.2] Groundwater over-extraction, dams | Wildlife conservation
  [1:4.3] Road and urban expansion, industrial emissions, overfishing | –
[1:5] - **Deforestation** is driven by urbanisation, agricultural expansion and timber extraction, and **reduces** biodiversity – "more biodiversity" is false.
[1:6] - India's **two threats**: **poverty-induced degradation** + **pollution from affluence** and a rapidly growing industrial sector.
[1:7] - Green Revolution: HYV monoculture and chemical fertilisers cut soil fertility; **HYV irrigation needs** depleted the water table. **Urea ≈ 56%** of fertiliser use; low-carbon alternative **green urea** (green hydrogen + captured CO2).
[1:8] - India's low EPI score: **urban particulate pollution and coal-based power**.
[1:9] ~~ Sources: NextGen Indian Economy Part 1 - APPSC 03 p.53 (two threats); NCERT Indian Economic Development Class 11 ch.9; app notes 3:19 sub 1-2
## SUB 2 | Green Economy, Green GDP and Green Accounting
[2:0] - **Green economy** (UNEP) = **low-carbon, resource-efficient and socially inclusive**; reduces environmental risks; focuses on **fostering green industries and creating green jobs**. **Green jobs** preserve or restore environmental quality.
[2:1] - **Green GDP** = conventional GDP with the **environmental consequences** of growth factored in = GDP **net of natural-resource depletion and degradation**. The term **Green GNP** emphasises **sustainable development** (not mere growth of GNP or per capita income).
[2:2] - **Uttarakhand** = first State to adopt green GDP, as **Gross Environment Product (GEP)**.
[2:3] TABLE head: Person / body | Contribution
  [2:3.0] Sir Partha Dasgupta | Chaired the Expert Group on Green National Accounts (set up 2011, report 2013)
  [2:3.1] Paul Samuelson | NEW (Net Economic Welfare)
  [2:3.2] Nordhaus and Tobin | MEW (Measure of Economic Welfare)
  [2:3.3] World Bank | Green Index; 2013 cost-of-degradation estimate
  [2:3.4] MoSPI | Environment accounts on UN SEEA; EnviStats India (annual)
[2:4] - **SEEA** Central Framework adopted by the UN Statistical Commission in **2012**; MoSPI ecosystem accounts since 2018.
[2:5] - EnviStats India – six components: conditions and quality; resources and use; **residuals**; **extreme events**; **human settlements and health**; protection and management.
[2:6] ~~ Sources: NextGen Indian Economy Part 1 - APPSC 04 p.38 (green GDP; NEW – Samuelson); Ramesh Singh, Indian Economy ch.2 (green GDP, NEW, MEW); PMF Environment 02 (green GDP); MoSPI EnviStats India (mospi.gov.in); app notes 3:19 sub 5-6
## SUB 3 | Environmental Kuznets Curve and the Growth vs Environment Debate
[3:0] TABLE head: Feature | Fact
  [3:0.0] X-axis | Per capita income
  [3:0.1] Y-axis | Environmental degradation
  [3:0.2] Shape | Inverted U – pollution rises at low income, falls after a turning point
  [3:0.3] Origin | Simon Kuznets' curve: income vs income inequality; for environment first shown by Grossman and Krueger (1991)
[3:1] - **Growth vs environment debate:**
[3:2] TABLE head: View | Argument
  [3:2.0] Trade-off view | Environmental rules slow growth; "grow first, clean up later"
  [3:2.1] Necessity view | Degradation raises health costs and lowers labour efficiency
[3:3] - Conventional GDP **ignores depletion** of natural resources, so growth can hide the loss of natural capital.
[3:4] ~~ Sources: Grossman and Krueger (1991, NBER); Ramesh Singh, Indian Economy ch.2; app notes 3:19 sub 3-4
## SUB 4 | Cost of Degradation and Land Degradation Data
[4:0] - **World Bank (2013)**: India's annual cost of environmental degradation ≈ **5.7% of GDP** (**Rs 3.75 lakh crore**, 2009) – through higher **healthcare spending** and lower **labour productivity**.
[4:1] - Global: about one-third of land transformed by humans; **60%+ of rivers** dammed or diverted; about **40%** of land degraded.
[4:2] TABLE head: ISRO-SAC Desertification and Land Degradation Atlas (2021) | Degraded area
  [4:2.0] 2003-05 | 94.53 Mha (28.76%)
  [4:2.1] 2011-13 | 96.40 Mha (29.32%)
  [4:2.2] 2018-19 | 97.85 Mha (29.77%)
[4:3] - Leading process: **water erosion (10.98%)**, then vegetation degradation (8.91%); desertification rose in **28 of 31** States/UTs.
[4:4] - **21.76 Mha** restored in 2011-2020; **Telangana first (4.18 Mha)**. Most desertification-vulnerable AP districts: **Anantapur, Kurnool, Kadapa**.
[4:5] ~~ Sources: World Bank, India: Diagnostic Assessment of Select Environmental Challenges (2013); ISRO-SAC Desertification and Land Degradation Atlas of India 2021 (sac.gov.in); app notes 3:19 sub 8
