=== NOTES 2:21 | Basic Structure Doctrine – Kesavananda Bharati and After
## SUB 0 | Kesavananda Bharati (1973) – Where the Doctrine Was Propounded
[21:0:0] - The **basic structure doctrine** was **first propounded** in **Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973)** – not in Golaknath (1967), Sajjan Singh (1965), Shankari Prasad (1951), Champakam Dorairajan, Gopalan, Maneka Gandhi, Minerva Mills, Bommai or Indra Sawhney. Where Kesavananda is not among the options, the answer is "**None of the above**".
[21:0:1] - Decided **24 April 1973** by **13 judges** (the largest bench ever), majority **7:6**, headed by **CJI S.M. Sikri**.
[21:0:2] - Held: Parliament under **Art. 368** may amend **any part** of the Constitution, **including Fundamental Rights**, but **cannot alter, damage or destroy its basic structure**.
[21:0:3] - **Overruled Golaknath** and **upheld the 24th Amendment** (1971), which had amended Arts. 13 and 368.
[21:0:4] - Held the **Preamble** to be **part of the Constitution** and amendable, but its objectives embody the basic structure – the Preamble is regarded as **part of the basic structure** (Kesavananda, not Berubari, Gopalan or the Privy Purse case).
[21:0:5] - Struck down the part of **Art. 31C** (25th Amendment) that barred courts from asking whether a law really gives effect to the Directive Principles – so **judicial review** of amendments stays.
[21:0:6] - Basic features named by the judges included **supremacy of the Constitution, republican and democratic government, secularism, separation of powers, federalism, unity and sovereignty, parliamentary system, welfare state, dignity of the individual**.
[21:0:7] - Landmark for "**the basic structure of the Constitution, as defined in the Preamble, cannot be changed**" (CDS 2010) – not for religion in politics, untouchability or the non-suspension of Art. 21.
[21:0:8] TABLE head: Person | Contribution
  [21:0:8.0] Dietrich Conrad | German jurist; argued in India (1965) that the amending power has implied limits, drawing on Germany's Basic Law
  [21:0:8.1] Justice Mudholkar | Dissent in Sajjan Singh (1965) – first judicial reference to "basic features"
  [21:0:8.2] CJI S.M. Sikri | Headed the 13-judge Kesavananda bench
[21:0:9] - Country the idea is traced to: **Germany**.
[21:0:10] > Exam angle: "Which case propounded the basic structure doctrine?" is asked again and again (×18 PYQs) – Kesavananda Bharati, 1973.

## SUB 1 | What the Basic Structure Means
[21:1:0] - Basic structure = the **core features** of the Constitution that **cannot be amended or altered by Parliament** in exercise of the power under **Art. 368** (APPSC G1 2018) – "certain features are so essential that they **cannot be abrogated**" (IAS 1994).
[21:1:1] - It is **not** "what can be amended by simple majority", **not** "Directive Principles only" or "Fundamental Rights only", **not** "the order of Articles cannot be altered" and **not** "amendments only on procedural matters".
[21:1:2] - Source of the theory = **judicial interpretation** – **judge-made**, nowhere defined in the text; **not** inserted by any **Act of Parliament** or amendment (APPSC G1 2024 statement I false).
[21:1:3] - **Not exhaustive**: the Supreme Court has never fully listed it; the list grows **case by case**.
[21:1:4] - What limits Parliament's power to amend the Constitution = the **basic structure** (not FRs as such, customs or distribution of powers).
[21:1:5] - Present position: Parliament can amend **any part** of the Constitution, **including FRs**, **without affecting the basic structure** (APPSC G1 2024 statement II true); FRs can still be amended after Kesavananda.
[21:1:6] - **Parliament's limited amending power** is itself a basic feature (**Minerva Mills, 1980**; statement III true).
[21:1:7] - As a principle of judicial review, the doctrine **accepts the supremacy of the Constitution and of the courts** (the American principle of judicial supremacy, accepted "to a limited extent"). It goes beyond "**procedure established by law**", under which a court only checks whether a law is within the authority's powers.

  [21:1:8.3] Kumar Padma Prasad (1992) declared the welfare state basic | False – welfare state: Kesavananda / Bhim Singhji (1981); Kumar Padma Prasad was about judicial independence in appointing judges

## SUB 2 | Evolution – Shankari Prasad to the NJAC Case
[21:2:0] TABLE head: Year | Case / event | Contribution
  [21:2:0.0] 1951 | Shankari Prasad | Art. 368 includes power to amend FRs; an amendment is not "law" under Art. 13
  [21:2:0.1] 1965 | Sajjan Singh | Upheld the 17th Amendment; reaffirmed Shankari Prasad; Mudholkar dissent
  [21:2:0.2] 1967 | Golaknath | Challenged the 17th (not the 24th); 11 judges, 6:5; FRs "transcendental and immutable"; amendment is "law"; Parliament cannot amend (abridge) FRs; first use of prospective overruling
  [21:2:0.3] 1971 | 24th Amendment | Parliament's reply – Art. 13(4), Art. 368: Parliament can abridge FRs

  [21:2:0.5] 1975 | Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain | First application; struck Art. 329A(4) (39th Amendment) putting the PM's and Speaker's election beyond courts; free and fair elections, rule of law, democracy basic
  [21:2:0.6] 1976 | 42nd Amendment | Art. 368(4), (5): no limit on the amending power, no court review
  [21:2:0.7] 1980 | Minerva Mills | Struck 368(4), (5) and the widened 31C; judicial review, limited amending power, FR–DPSP harmony basic
  [21:2:0.8] 1981 | Waman Rao | Doctrine applies to amendments after 24 April 1973
  [21:2:0.9] 1994 | S.R. Bommai | Nine judges; secularism, federalism basic
  [21:2:0.10] 2007 | I.R. Coelho | Nine judges; post-1973 Ninth Schedule laws reviewable
  [21:2:0.11] 2015 | NJAC case (SC Advocates-on-Record Assn.) | 99th Amendment (2014) and NJAC Act struck, 4:1 – judicial independence
[21:2:1] - **1967** key judgment on FRs = **Golak Nath v. State of Punjab** – the case that said **Fundamental Rights cannot be amended**.
[21:2:2] - Case by which Parliament got (back) the right to amend FRs = **Kesavananda Bharati** – it upheld the 24th Amendment after Golaknath had denied the power (Shankari Prasad and Sajjan Singh had also allowed it before 1967).
[21:2:3] - **Judicial review** was **explicitly held a basic feature in Minerva Mills (1980)** – the 42nd Amendment's bar on review was struck "as it excludes judicial review, which is a basic feature"; reaffirmed in S.P. Sampath Kumar (1987) and **L. Chandra Kumar (1997)**.
[21:2:4] - The term "**judicial review**" is **nowhere defined or used** in the Constitution; Arts. 13, 32, 226 confer the power.
[21:2:5] - **Election bar** for the PM was the **39th Amendment** (not the 42nd) – struck in Raj Narain; the **99th (NJAC)** was struck for violating **judicial independence** (UPSC 2019: only II correct).
[21:2:6] - Benches: Golaknath **11, 6:5** | Kesavananda **13, 7:6** | NJAC **4:1** | Bommai and Coelho **9**.

## SUB 4 | Ninth Schedule, Articles 31A–31C and the Coelho Case
[21:4:0] - Created by the **1st Amendment (1951)** with **Art. 31B** (and 31A); it also added **Art. 15(4)** and new **Art. 19(2)** grounds (public order, friendly relations with foreign States, incitement to an offence). Purpose: protect **land-reform** laws.
[21:4:1] - Covers **Central and State** laws; entries made **only by Parliament through a constitutional amendment** (UPSC 2018 statement I true) – not by ordinary law, a State Legislature or a Presidential order.
[21:4:2] - Example: **76th Amendment, 1994** placed **Tamil Nadu's 69% reservation** Act. Entries: **13** at the start → **282** now (more than 280).
[21:4:3] TABLE head: Case | Year | Rule
  [21:4:3.0] Kesavananda Bharati | 1973 | Amendments, including inclusions, cannot destroy the basic structure
  [21:4:3.1] Waman Rao | 1981 | Cut-off 24 April 1973; later entries open to challenge
  [21:4:3.2] I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu | 2007 | Nine judges; no blanket immunity; "rights test / direct impact test" on the essence of Arts. 14, 19, 21
[21:4:4] - So "the validity of a law placed in the Ninth Schedule cannot be examined by any court" is **false** – UPSC 2018 answer **"1 only"**.
[21:4:5] - **Coelho** = the "**Ninth Schedule case**" (not the Seventh, Eighth or Tenth).
[21:4:6] - Sequence: **1st Amendment (1951) → Kesavananda (1973) → Waman Rao (1981) → Coelho (2007)**.
[21:4:7] TABLE head: Article | Saves | Inserted by
  [21:4:7.0] 31A | Estate acquisition, agrarian reform laws from Arts. 14, 19 | 1st Amendment, 1951
  [21:4:7.1] 31B | Ninth Schedule laws | 1st Amendment, 1951
  [21:4:7.2] 31C | Laws implementing Art. 39(b), (c) from Arts. 14, 19 | 25th Amendment, 1971 (not the First); widened by the 42nd

## SUB 5 | Supreme Court vs Parliament – Doctrines, Supremacy, Criticism
[21:5:0] TABLE head: Doctrine / rule | Origin
  [21:5:0.0] Basic structure | Supreme Court – Kesavananda (1973)
  [21:5:0.1] Eclipse, severability, pith and substance, colourable legislation, harmonious construction, territorial nexus | Supreme Court (interpretive doctrines)
  [21:5:0.2] Prospective overruling | Supreme Court – Golaknath (1967)
  [21:5:0.3] DPSP primacy over Arts. 14, 19 (Art. 31C) | Parliament – 25th Amendment (1971)
  [21:5:0.4] Art. 13(4) | Parliament – 24th Amendment
  [21:5:0.5] Unlimited amending power (Art. 368(4), (5)) | Parliament – 42nd; struck in Minerva Mills
[21:5:1] - The doctrine rests on **supremacy of the Constitution and judicial review by the courts**.
[21:5:2] - Indian Parliament is **not sovereign in the British sense**: written supreme Constitution, FRs, judicial review, federal division, **limited** amending power. India = a **synthesis** of American judicial supremacy and British parliamentary supremacy.
[21:5:3] - The **43rd Amendment (1977)** removed the 42nd Amendment's bar on High Courts reviewing central laws.
[21:5:4] TABLE head: View | Argument
  [21:5:4.0] Criticism | No textual definition; "judicial rewriting" by unelected judges; open-ended list; "judicial supremacy"
  [21:5:4.1] Defence | Shield against majorities; checks misuse of Art. 368
[21:5:5] - **2026**: free and fair elections and democracy as basic features cited in the debate on making **voting** more than a statutory right.
[21:5:6] - **129th Amendment Bill, 2024** (simultaneous polls), JPC: ex-CJI **B.R. Gavai** – no violation of basic structure; ex-CJIs **U.U. Lalit** and **Sanjiv Khanna** raised doubts (as of 2026).
[21:5:7] - Cannot be done even by an Art. 368 amendment: **destroying the basic structure** (amending FRs, the Preamble or adding to the Ninth Schedule can be done).

=== NOTES 2:23 | Doctrines of Constitutional Interpretation
## SUB 0 | Federal Doctrines – Pith and Substance, Repugnancy and Others
[23:0:0] TABLE head: Doctrine | Meaning | Field
  [23:0:0.0] Pith and substance | Judge a law by its true nature; incidental trespass on the other List is fine – the doctrine used to decide encroachment of the Union List on the State List | Union–State Lists
  [23:0:0.1] Colourable legislation | What cannot be done directly cannot be done indirectly; "fraud on the Constitution" | Tests competence, not motive
  [23:0:0.2] Territorial nexus | State law may operate outside the State if there is a sufficient nexus | Extra-territorial operation
  [23:0:0.3] Harmonious construction | Read conflicting provisions so that both operate | Provision vs provision
  [23:0:0.4] Incidental and ancillary | An entry covers matters incidental to it; widest meaning | Entries
  [23:0:0.5] Repugnancy / federal paramountcy | Conflict on the same Concurrent subject – Union law prevails (Art. 254) | Concurrent List
  [23:0:0.6] Parens patriae | The State as legal protector of citizens unable to protect themselves (minors, persons of unsound mind, victims) | Welfare

## SUB 0 | Constitutional Government = Limited Government
[24:0:0] - **Constitutional government** by definition = **limited government** – a government **limited by the terms of the Constitution** (UPSC 2020, 2021 ×2) – not "government by the legislature", "popular", "multi-party", "federal structure" or a head with nominal/real powers.
[24:0:1] - "**Persons may change but rules should not change**" = principle of **constitutional government** (rule of law, not of persons).
[24:0:2] - In a democracy the powers of the government are limited by the **Constitution**.
[24:0:3] - **Constitutionalism**: limits + rule of law + checks and balances + judicial review + FRs; a **legislative majority cannot override constitutional limitations**.
[24:0:4] - Not an indication of true democracy: "**rule by majority only**" – free and fair elections, respect for the individual and equality before law are.
[24:0:5] - **PIL** is **not indispensable** for the rule of law, which existed before PIL through **Arts. 14, 32, 226**.

## SUB 2 | Separation of Powers – Montesquieu and the Indian Degree
[24:2:0] - **Montesquieu**: **division of powers** best protects liberty; "**The Spirit of Laws**" (**1748**); drew on the **British** constitution.
[24:2:1] - **Best safeguard of liberty in a liberal democracy = separation of powers** (UPSC 2021) – not a committed judiciary, centralisation or an elected government.
[24:2:2] - **Presidential** government operates on **separation of powers**; **parliamentary** = **fusion** (ministers drawn from and responsible to the legislature). Division of powers between Centre and States = federalism.
[24:2:3] - The organ made up of the **people's elected representatives** = the **legislature** (judges are appointed; the media is not an organ of government).
[24:2:4] - India: **Nominal head** President; **real executive** Council of Ministers. **Ambedkar** preferred **responsibility over stability**; reasons: familiarity (1919/1935 Acts), diversity, avoiding American-style deadlock.
[24:2:5] - India: **no rigid separation** – implicit, not absolute, **functional**; overlaps → checks and balances (**Ram Jawaya Kapur, 1955**). Separation of powers = basic feature (**Kesavananda, 1973**).
[24:2:6] - **Art. 50 (DPSP)**: separate the judiciary from the executive **in the public services of the State**. Judiciary insulated: **Arts. 50, 121, 211, 235**.
[24:2:7] - **Munro's Madras Regulations (1816)** reversed the Cornwallis separation – the **Collector** again got **magisterial and police powers**.

## SUB 1 | Election – Electoral College, Value of Votes, Disputes (Arts. 54, 55, 71)
[25:1:0] TABLE head: Votes in the Presidential election | Does NOT vote
  [25:1:0.0] Elected members of both Houses of Parliament (Lok Sabha + Rajya Sabha) | Nominated members of Parliament and of State Assemblies
  [25:1:0.1] Elected members of the State Legislative Assemblies | All members of State Legislative Councils (elected or nominated) – a CM who sits in the Council also cannot vote
  [25:1:0.2] Elected members of the Assemblies of NCT Delhi and Puducherry (added by the 70th Amendment, 1992) | Governors, Lt. Governors; UTs without Assemblies (e.g. Chandigarh, Daman and Diu)
[25:1:1] - **Art. 54** = the electoral college; **Art. 55** = manner of election; Arts. 54 and 55 together = **election of the President** (not Arts. 49, 56, 62, 74, 78, 103).
[25:1:2] - **Keyword – "elected" members**: "all the members of the two Houses" is **wrong** (nominated MPs are out); the correct code is **elected MPs + elected MLAs** (States, Delhi, Puducherry).
[25:1:3] - **Indirect election**: the President is chosen by the elected representatives, not directly by the people, and not by Parliament or the Lok Sabha/Rajya Sabha alone.
[25:1:4] - **Art. 55(3)**: **proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote**, by **secret ballot** – method borrowed from **Ireland**.
[25:1:5] - PR-STV is also used for the **Vice-President**, **Rajya Sabha** members and **Legislative Council** members; **Lok Sabha** and State Assembly members are elected directly (first-past-the-post).
[25:1:6] TABLE head: Elector | Value of vote | Effect
  [25:1:6.0] MLA | Population of the State ÷ elected MLAs × 1/1000 | Varies from State to State (uniformity among States, Art. 55(1))
  [25:1:6.1] MP | Total value of all MLAs' votes ÷ total elected MPs | Same for every MP, Lok Sabha or Rajya Sabha (parity between the Union and the States)
[25:1:7] - **Population base: 1971 Census** – frozen by the 42nd Amendment (till 2000) and then by the **84th Amendment (2001)** till the first Census after **2026**.
[25:1:8] - **Vote value depends on the population–seats ratio, not on the number of seats**: Puducherry MLA **16** > Arunachal Pradesh MLA **8**; Kerala MLA **152** > Madhya Pradesh MLA **131** (2022 values). So "more Assembly seats → higher MLA vote value" is **wrong**.
[25:1:9] - **Nomination**: at least **50 electors as proposers and 50 as seconders** (since 1997; **10 + 10** from 1974 to 1997); deposit ₹15,000. Vice-President: **20 + 20**.
[25:1:10] - **Quota**: more than half of valid vote value; lowest candidate eliminated and votes transferred by preference (V.V. Giri, 1969, won on **second-preference** votes).
[25:1:11] - **Art. 71**: all doubts and disputes about the election are decided **only by the Supreme Court**, whose decision is **final** (not the Election Commission or the RS Chairman).
[25:1:12] - **Acts done before the election is declared void remain valid** ("not invalidated").
[25:1:13] - **Art. 71(4)**: the election **cannot be challenged or postponed** because of **vacancies in the electoral college** – members of a **dissolved Assembly** simply do not vote.
[25:1:14] - **Election vs impeachment**: **elected MLAs vote in the election but take no part in impeachment** (Parliament alone impeaches); **nominated MPs** do the reverse. **Legislative Councils** do neither.

  [25:2:0.1] 57 | Eligibility for re-election – any number of times, no upper limit
  [25:2:0.2] 58 | Qualifications: citizen of India; 35 years completed; qualified for election to the Lok Sabha; no office of profit

## SUB 3 | Impeachment (Art. 61)
[25:3:0] - **Art. 61** = procedure for **impeachment** (removal) of the President (not Arts. 53, 60, 63, 71, 72, 74, 78); Art. 56(1)(b) refers to it.
[25:3:1] - **Only ground: "violation of the Constitution"** – not corruption, misbehaviour, criminal offence, moral degradation, unsoundness of mind or professional misconduct (the Constitution does not define the term).
[25:3:2] - **Who removes him**: **Parliament** (the two Houses) – not the PM, the CJI, the party in power, a court, or the Lok Sabha alone.
[25:3:3] - **Initiated in either House of Parliament** (Lok Sabha or Rajya Sabha) – not only the Lok Sabha and not a joint sitting.
[25:3:4] TABLE head: Step | Requirement
  [25:3:4.0] Notice | 14 days' written notice, resolution signed by one-fourth of the total members of the initiating House
  [25:3:4.1] Initiating House | Passes the charge by two-thirds of its total membership
  [25:3:4.2] Other House | Investigates the charge (President may appear or be represented) and sustains it by two-thirds of its total membership → removed from that date
[25:3:5] - **Quasi-judicial procedure**; borrowed from the **USA**. **No President has ever been impeached.**
[25:3:6] - **Nominated MPs take part**; **elected MLAs (States, Delhi, Puducherry) do not** – the President is removed by the national Parliament alone, keeping removal free from narrow regional considerations.
[25:3:7] - "Impeachment" is used **only for the President**; judges and the Vice-President are "removed". **VP removal**: Rajya Sabha resolution by a majority of all its then members, agreed to by the Lok Sabha; **no ground** is specified.

## SUB 4 | Powers of the President
[25:4:0] - **Art. 53**: the **executive power of the Union is vested in the President**, exercised directly or through subordinate officers **in accordance with the Constitution** – so his power is **not unlimited** (Art. 74: aid and advice).
[25:4:1] - **Art. 53(2)**: **Supreme Command of the Defence Forces** is vested in the **President** (regulated by law) – not the Defence Minister, Army Chief or a Field Marshal.
[25:4:2] - **Union executive**: **President, Vice-President, Prime Minister, Council of Ministers and Attorney General** (Part V, Chapter I) – not the Speaker or the RS Deputy Chairman.
[25:4:3] TABLE head: Power | Examples
  [25:4:3.0] Executive | Appoints the PM and on his advice other Ministers; Governors (Art. 155), CAG (Art. 148), AG (Art. 76), Election Commissioners (Art. 324), UPSC, Finance Commission; Ambassadors and High Commissioners; administers UTs; Art. 240 regulations for A&N, Lakshadweep, DNH and Daman & Diu, and Puducherry when its Assembly is suspended or dissolved
  [25:4:3.1] Legislative | Integral part of Parliament (Art. 79: President + Lok Sabha + Rajya Sabha); summons and prorogues the Houses, dissolves the Lok Sabha (Art. 85); summons a joint sitting (Art. 108 – presided over by the Speaker); addresses and sends messages (Art. 86); special address (Art. 87); nominates 12 to the Rajya Sabha; assent to Bills; ordinances
  [25:4:3.2] Financial | Budget laid in his name; Money Bills and demands for grants only on his recommendation; advances from the Contingency Fund; constitutes the Finance Commission

## SUB 5 | Vacancy and Acting President (Arts. 62, 65)
[25:5:0] - **Causes of vacancy**: expiry of term, **death**, **resignation**, **removal by impeachment**, disqualification, election declared void.
[25:5:1] - **Art. 62(1) – expiry**: the election must be **completed before the term expires**; if delayed, the **outgoing President continues** (no vacancy, so the VP does **not** act).
[25:5:2] - **Art. 62(2) – death, resignation, removal or otherwise**: election **within six months** of the vacancy (the office can stay vacant at most 6 months); the new President gets a **full five-year term**, not the remainder.
[25:5:3] - **Art. 65(1)**: in a vacancy by **death, resignation or removal**, the **Vice-President acts as President** until the new President enters office; **Art. 65(2)**: the VP also **discharges the functions** when the President is absent, ill or otherwise unable.
[25:5:4] - While acting, the VP has all the powers, immunities and emoluments of the President and does **not** act as RS Chairman – the **Deputy Chairman presides** over the Rajya Sabha.
[25:5:5] - **Both offices vacant**: the **Chief Justice of India**, and in his absence the **senior-most SC judge**, discharges the President's functions – **President (Discharge of Functions) Act, 1969** – not the PM, Speaker, AG or CEC.

## SUB 6 | Constitutional Position, Article 74 and Discretion
[25:6:0] - **Nominal / de jure** executive and **constitutional head of the State**; the **PM and Council of Ministers** are the **real / de facto** executive. A-R: "the Constitution vests executive power in the President" (A) because "he is the constitutional Head of the State" (R) – both true, R explains A.
[25:6:1] - **Art. 74(1)**: a Council of Ministers headed by the PM to aid and advise the President. **42nd Amendment (1976)**: advice made **binding** ("shall act"). **44th Amendment (1978)**: he may **return the advice once for reconsideration**, then must act on it.
[25:6:2] - So the President's influence on ministers = he **may seek reconsideration once** before putting his seal on a matter.
[25:6:3] - **Art. 74(2)**: whether and what advice was tendered **cannot be inquired into by any court**.
[25:6:4] - **Art. 361**: President/Governor not answerable to any court for official acts; no criminal proceedings during the term; **civil proceedings only after two months' written notice**; no court can compel a Governor to perform a duty.
[25:6:5] - **Situational discretion** (no constitutional discretion): appointing a PM when no party has a clear majority; dismissing a Ministry that has lost confidence; dissolving the Lok Sabha if the Ministry lost its majority; day-to-day decisions under a **caretaker government**; **suspensive veto** (returning a Bill), **pocket veto** (keeping a Bill pending); asking the Council to **reconsider** advice.
[25:6:6] - **Not discretionary**: **summoning and proroguing Parliament**, appointing Ministers, appointing the majority leader as PM (a convention), assent to Bills.
[25:6:7] - **PM** = "keystone of the Cabinet arch" – the President is only the formal head.

=== NOTES 2:26 | Veto Powers, Ordinance-making Power (art. 123) and Pardoning Power (art. 72)
## SUB 0 | Pardoning Power – Article 72: Forms and Nature
[26:0:0] - **Art. 72**: the President may "grant **pardons, reprieves, respites or remissions** of punishment or **suspend, remit or commute** the sentence of any person convicted of any offence" – not Arts. 62, 63, 70, 71, 73, 74 or 75.
[26:0:1] TABLE head: Form | Meaning
  [26:0:1.0] Pardon | Removes both sentence and conviction – as if the offence was never committed
  [26:0:1.1] Commutation | Substitutes a lighter form of punishment (death → rigorous life imprisonment)
  [26:0:1.2] Remission | Reduces the period without changing its character (2 years RI → 1 year RI)
  [26:0:1.3] Respite | A lesser sentence for special reasons – pregnancy, physical or mental disability
  [26:0:1.4] Reprieve | Temporary stay of execution (esp. death) to allow time to seek a pardon
[26:0:2] - Commutation changes the **form**; remission reduces the **duration**.
[26:0:3] - Covers: (a) sentence by a **court-martial**; (b) offence against a **Union law**; (c) every **death sentence**. Amnesty is outside Art. 72.
[26:0:4] - Nature: listed among the President's **judicial powers** (UPPCS key: judicial power); strictly it is an **executive power, independent of the judiciary** – he is not a court of appeal.
[26:0:5] - Exercised on the **aid and advice of the Council of Ministers** (**Maru Ram, 1980**) – he **cannot** act without the Central Government's advice; subject to **limited judicial review** (mala fide, arbitrary, irrelevant grounds). So UPSC 2025 → **I only**.

## SUB 1 | Vetoes Actually Used – Pocket Veto and the Indian Post Office Bill
[26:1:0] - **Pocket veto** = the President **keeps a Bill pending with him, without any time limit**, neither approving, rejecting nor returning it – not the Speaker blocking a Bill, the PM dropping ministers or the Rajya Sabha rejecting a Bill.
[26:1:1] TABLE head: Veto | President | Bill
  [26:1:1.0] Pocket | Giani Zail Singh | Indian Post Office (Amendment) Bill, 1986 (power to intercept mail)
  [26:1:1.1] Absolute | Dr. Rajendra Prasad (on the Council of Ministers' advice) | PEPSU Appropriation Bill, 1954
  [26:1:1.2] Absolute | R. Venkataraman | Salary, Allowances and Pension of MPs (Amendment) Bill, 1991
[26:1:2] - The Post Office Bill question is asked in every form – "refused to sign / did not assent / did not give assent / first to use the power on it" → **Zail Singh** (not Radhakrishnan, V.V. Giri, Zakir Hussain, R. Venkataraman, K.R. Narayanan, Rajendra Prasad or A.P.J. Abdul Kalam). He **did not return** it; he simply kept it pending, and it was never enacted (later withdrawn).
[26:1:3] - Pocket veto used **only once** at the Union level; "the only instance of the President's veto" in older keys means this Bill, though the **PEPSU (1954)** and **1991** Bills were absolute vetoes.
[26:1:4] - **2002**: the **Representation of the People (Amendment) Ordinance** on **electoral reforms** was returned by **President Kalam**; the Union Cabinet sent it back unchanged and he **assented under Art. 123**.

## SUB 2 | Ordinance-making Power – Article 123: Basis, Nature, Scope
[26:2:0] - **Art. 123** (Part V): the **President** promulgates ordinances during the **recess of Parliament** – not the Speaker, the RS Chairman or the Attorney-General. Governor: **Art. 213** (Part VI). Not Arts. 161, 200, 201, 356 or 360.
[26:2:1] - Nature: the **most important legislative power of the President** – a **legislative power of the Executive** (President is part of Parliament, Art. 79), not an executive order. Among his legislative powers (summoning, proroguing, assent), the ordinance is the one examiners key; pardon is a judicial power; **adjourning** a sitting is the presiding officer's job.
[26:2:2] - When: only when **both Houses, or either House, are not in session** and he is **satisfied** that circumstances need **immediate action**; none while both Houses sit. Exercised on the **Council of Ministers' advice**.
[26:2:3] - Force: the **same force and effect as an Act of Parliament** and the same limits; **co-extensive** with Parliament's legislative power but **not a parallel** power.
[26:2:4] TABLE head: Can | Cannot
  [26:2:4.0] Amend or repeal any Central Act (or an earlier ordinance) | Amend the Constitution (Art. 368)
  [26:2:4.1] Operate retrospectively (from a back date) | Abridge Fundamental Rights – it is "law" under Art. 13(2)
  [26:2:4.2] Introduce or change tax provisions | Go beyond Parliament's legislative competence
[26:2:5] - UPSC 2025: "amend any Central Act" and "come into effect from a back date" true; "abridge a FR" false → **I and III only**.

## SUB 3 | Ordinance – Life, Lapse, Withdrawal and Judicial Review
[26:3:0] - 1. ## SUB 5 | Veto Power of the President – Types and Article 111
[26:5:0] - **Article 111**: a Bill passed by both Houses is presented to the President, who **declares assent**, **withholds assent** or (for a **non-Money Bill**) **returns** it for reconsideration. So the power to withhold assent is in **Art. 111** (not Arts. 63, 108 or 109).
[26:5:1] - Does the Indian President have a veto? **Yes** – three of the four kinds: **absolute, suspensive and pocket**; **no qualified veto**.
[26:5:2] TABLE head: Veto | Meaning | Applies to
  [26:5:2.0] Absolute | Withholds assent; the Bill ends and never becomes an Act | Private members' Bills; Government Bills when the Cabinet resigns before assent and the new Cabinet advises against it – not "at all times"
  [26:5:2.1] Suspensive | Returns the Bill once; if re-passed (with or without amendment) he must assent | Ordinary Bills (and Financial Bills) – not Money Bills
  [26:5:2.2] Pocket | Neither assents, rejects nor returns – keeps the Bill pending indefinitely | Possible because Art. 111 prescribes no time limit
  [26:5:2.3] Qualified | Can be overridden only by a higher (two-thirds) majority | USA – not India
[26:5:3] - **Veto by type of Bill:**
[26:5:4] TABLE head: Bill | President (Union) | President (State Bill reserved, Art. 201)
  [26:5:4.0] Ordinary | Assent, withhold or return once – must assent if re-passed | Assent, withhold or direct return – not bound to assent even if re-passed
  [26:5:4.1] Money | Assent or withhold; cannot return (usually assents – introduced on his prior recommendation) | Assent or withhold – so he can veto a State Money Bill
  [26:5:4.2] Constitutional Amendment | Must assent (24th Amendment, 1971); cannot reject or return | Cannot be introduced in a State Legislature
[26:5:5] - The one-time return power can apply to **Financial Bills of Categories I and II**; a Money Bill and a Constitutional Amendment Bill cannot be returned.

  [26:5:7.2] Pocket veto | Yes – no time limit | Yes – President must act within 10 days (the 10-day rule is American)

## SUB 6 | State Bills Reserved for the President – Articles 200 and 201
[26:6:0] - **Art. 200**: the Governor may **assent, withhold, return** (non-Money Bill) or **reserve** a Bill for the President; the **Governor has no pocket veto**.
[26:6:1] - **Art. 201**: for a reserved Bill the President **declares or withholds assent**, or directs its return (non-Money Bill); no time limit is written in Arts. 200 or 201.
[26:6:2] - **8 April 2025, State of Tamil Nadu v Governor**: indefinite inaction under **Art. 200** held **justiciable**; **ten** TN Bills deemed assented.
[26:6:3] - **Nov 2025, Presidential reference (Art. 143, five judges)**: courts **cannot fix timelines** for the Governor or President; **no deemed assent**; the powers cannot be used arbitrarily.
