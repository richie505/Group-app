=== Functions of the Environment, Absorptive and Carrying Capacity
| Function | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Supplies resources** (first function) | **Both renewable** (trees, fish – replaced naturally or inexhaustible) and **non-renewable** (fossil fuels – exhausted with use) |
| Assimilates waste | Absorbs and neutralises wastes |
| Sustains life | Genetic and biodiversity |
| Aesthetic services | Scenery and amenity |
- **Renewable resources** = those that **can be replaced naturally over time or are inexhaustible** – used without the resource becoming depleted.
- **Absorptive capacity** = ability of the environment to absorb degradation = the **maximum amount of pollutants an ecosystem can process and neutralise without harmful effects**.
- **Carrying capacity** (NCERT): the environment works without interruption as long as **resource extraction is not above the rate of regeneration** and **wastes are within the assimilating capacity** of the environment.
- **Crisis**: when **extraction > regeneration** and waste > absorptive capacity, the environment **cannot sustain life** – "we are today at the threshold of environmental crisis".
- **Recycling** is NOT a function of the environment; "supplies renewable resources only" is wrong.
~~ Sources: NextGen Indian Economy Part 1 - APPSC 03 p.52 (functions, renewable resources, absorptive capacity); NCERT Indian Economic Development Class 11 ch.9; app notes 3:19 sub 0

=== Resource Depletion and Causes of Environmental Degradation
- **Resource depletion** = use faster than regeneration; **overuse of natural resources → resource depletion** (not conservation or sustainability).
- Best illustration of depletion with long-term impact: **overfishing in a coastal area** harming the whole marine ecosystem and livelihoods. A forest managed **sustainably for timber while keeping biodiversity** is NOT depletion.
| Resource | Cause of depletion |
|---|---|
| Groundwater | Deep bore tubewells for irrigation |
| Topsoil | Brick production |
| Biodiversity habitats | Expansion of agriculture |
| Human-driven causes | NOT causes (conservation) |
|---|---|
| **Deforestation** (primary cause) | Sustainable agriculture |
| Chemical-intensive farming | Renewable energy production |
| Groundwater over-extraction, dams | Wildlife conservation |
| Road and urban expansion, industrial emissions, overfishing | – |
- **Deforestation** is driven by urbanisation, agricultural expansion and timber extraction, and **reduces** biodiversity – "more biodiversity" is false.
- India's **two threats**: **poverty-induced degradation** + **pollution from affluence** and a rapidly growing industrial sector.
- Green Revolution: HYV monoculture and chemical fertilisers cut soil fertility; **HYV irrigation needs** depleted the water table. **Urea ≈ 56%** of fertiliser use; low-carbon alternative **green urea** (green hydrogen + captured CO2).
- India's low EPI score: **urban particulate pollution and coal-based power**.
~~ Sources: NextGen Indian Economy Part 1 - APPSC 03 p.53 (two threats); NCERT Indian Economic Development Class 11 ch.9; app notes 3:19 sub 1-2

=== Green Economy, Green GDP and Green Accounting
- **Green economy** (UNEP) = **low-carbon, resource-efficient and socially inclusive**; reduces environmental risks; focuses on **fostering green industries and creating green jobs**. **Green jobs** preserve or restore environmental quality.
- **Green GDP** = conventional GDP with the **environmental consequences** of growth factored in = GDP **net of natural-resource depletion and degradation**. The term **Green GNP** emphasises **sustainable development** (not mere growth of GNP or per capita income).
- **Uttarakhand** = first State to adopt green GDP, as **Gross Environment Product (GEP)**.
| Person / body | Contribution |
|---|---|
| Sir **Partha Dasgupta** | Chaired the Expert Group on Green National Accounts (set up **2011**, report **2013**) |
| **Paul Samuelson** | **NEW** (Net Economic Welfare) |
| **Nordhaus and Tobin** | **MEW** (Measure of Economic Welfare) |
| World Bank | Green Index; 2013 cost-of-degradation estimate |
| **MoSPI** | Environment accounts on UN **SEEA**; **EnviStats India** (annual) |
- **SEEA** Central Framework adopted by the UN Statistical Commission in **2012**; MoSPI ecosystem accounts since 2018.
- EnviStats India – six components: conditions and quality; resources and use; **residuals**; **extreme events**; **human settlements and health**; protection and management.

=== Environmental Kuznets Curve and the Growth vs Environment Debate
| Feature | Fact |
|---|---|
| X-axis | **Per capita income** |
| Y-axis | Environmental degradation |
| Shape | **Inverted U** – pollution rises at low income, falls after a turning point |
| Origin | Simon Kuznets' curve: income vs **income inequality**; for environment first shown by **Grossman and Krueger** (1991) |
| View | Argument |
|---|---|
| **Trade-off view** | Environmental rules slow growth; "**grow first, clean up later**" |
| Necessity view | Degradation **raises health costs and lowers labour efficiency** |
- Conventional GDP **ignores depletion** of natural resources, so growth can hide the loss of natural capital.
~~ Sources: Grossman and Krueger (1991, NBER); Ramesh Singh, Indian Economy ch.2; app notes 3:19 sub 3-4

=== Cost of Degradation and Land Degradation Data
- **World Bank (2013)**: India's annual cost of environmental degradation ≈ **5.7% of GDP** (**Rs 3.75 lakh crore**, 2009) – through higher **healthcare spending** and lower **labour productivity**.
- Global: about one-third of land transformed by humans; **60%+ of rivers** dammed or diverted; about **40%** of land degraded.
| ISRO-SAC Desertification and Land Degradation Atlas (2021) | Degraded area |
|---|---|
| 2003-05 | 94.53 Mha (28.76%) |
| 2011-13 | 96.40 Mha (29.32%) |
| **2018-19** | **97.85 Mha (29.77%)** |
- Leading process: **water erosion (10.98%)**, then vegetation degradation (8.91%); desertification rose in **28 of 31** States/UTs.
- **21.76 Mha** restored in 2011-2020; **Telangana first (4.18 Mha)**. Most desertification-vulnerable AP districts: **Anantapur, Kurnool, Kadapa**.
~~ Sources: World Bank, India: Diagnostic Assessment of Select Environmental Challenges (2013); ISRO-SAC Desertification and Land Degradation Atlas of India 2021 (sac.gov.in); app notes 3:19 sub 8
