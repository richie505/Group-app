=== NOTES 1:172 | Viceroys of India – Canning to Mountbatten: Key Measures, Acts and Events
## SUB 0 | Mayo, Lawrence and Dufferin – Assassination, Census, Shimla, Masterly Inactivity, INC
[172:0:0] # Lord Mayo (1869-72)
[172:0:1] - **Only Viceroy assassinated in office:** **Lord Mayo**, stabbed by the convict **Sher Ali Afridi** at Port Blair (**Andamans**) in **1872** while on tour – not Curzon, Minto, Ripon or Wellesley.
[172:0:2] - **First census:** **1872** under **Mayo** (non-synchronous, not all areas at once); from **1881 (Ripon)** the regular decennial, synchronous census.
[172:0:3]   - ⚠ NextGen Indian History p.3 prints "1871" – a slip; PMF and the Census give **1872**.
[172:0:4] - **Financial decentralisation:** started by **Mayo's Resolution of 1870** (not Ripon, Dalhousie or Metcalfe).
[172:0:5] - Also: **Rajkot College** (Kathiawar) and **Mayo College** (Ajmer) for princes; **Statistical Survey of India**; **Department of Agriculture and Commerce**; state railways; Indian Evidence Act 1872.
[172:0:6] # Sir John Lawrence (1864-69)
[172:0:7] - **Masterly Inactivity:** non-interference in Afghanistan – **John Lawrence** (not Bentinck, Canning or Mayo).
[172:0:8] - **Shimla** officially declared the **summer capital** of British India by **John Lawrence in 1864**.
[172:0:9] - Also: telegraph link with Europe (1865); High Courts at Calcutta, Bombay, Madras (1865); **Indian Forest Department**; Bhutan (Duar) War.
[172:0:10] # Lord Dufferin (1884-88)
[172:0:11] - **INC founded (Dec 1885)** under **Dufferin** – Ripon had left in 1884; Lytton, Lansdowne, Elgin II, Curzon are distractors.
[172:0:12] - In **1887** Dufferin mocked the Congress as representing only a **"microscopic minority"** of the people.
[172:0:13] - **Third Anglo-Burmese War (1885):** Upper Burma annexed (1 Jan 1886) = the **last major extension** of British Indian territory – Dufferin.
[172:0:14] # Canning, Elgin I, Northbrook
[172:0:15] - **Canning (1856-62):** last Company Governor-General and **first Viceroy** (1858); Revolt of 1857; withdrew the Doctrine of Lapse; universities at Calcutta, Bombay, Madras (1857); **Indian Councils Act 1861**, **portfolio system**; 3 Indians nominated (Raja of Benaras, Maharaja of Patiala, Sir Dinkar Rao).
[172:0:16] - **British PM during the Revolt of 1857:** **Palmerston** (1855-58).
[172:0:17] - **Elgin I (1862-63):** Wahabi movement. **Northbrook (1872-76):** abolished income tax; Kuka movement.
[172:0:18] - **Allahabad High Court** came in **1866** – after Canning's term.

## SUB 1 | Lytton and Ripon – VPA, Forward Policy, Ilbert Bill, Factory Act 1881
[172:1:0] # Lord Lytton (1876-80)
[172:1:1] TABLE head: Measure | Year
  [172:1:1.0] Royal Titles Act – Victoria "Kaiser-i-Hind" | 1876
  [172:1:1.1] Delhi Durbar in honour of Queen Victoria (held during famine) | 1 Jan 1877
  [172:1:1.2] Vernacular Press Act, Arms Act | 1878
  [172:1:1.3] Strachey Famine Commission | 1878
  [172:1:1.4] ICS maximum age cut 21 → 19; Statutory Civil Service | 1878-79
  [172:1:1.5] Second Afghan War; Treaty of Gandamak | 1878-80, 1879
[172:1:2] - **Forward / aggressive policy towards Afghanistan ("proud reserve", scientific frontier):** **Lytton** – a Disraeli nominee; not Minto, Dufferin, Elgin or Mayo.
[172:1:3] - **VPA (1878):** magistrates could demand a security bond from vernacular papers without prior government permission – passed by **Lytton**, **not Curzon**; **repealed by Ripon (1882)**.
[172:1:4] - Lytton is **not** linked with the **Ilbert Bill** (that is Ripon).
[172:1:5] # Lord Ripon (1880-84)
[172:1:6] - Sent by **Gladstone's** Liberal government; repealed the **VPA (1882)**; **Local Self-Government Resolution 1882** ("Father of Local Self-Government"); **Hunter Education Commission (1882)**; first synchronous census 1881; revenue heads split into imperial, provincial, divided (1882).
[172:1:7] - **Ilbert Bill (1883):** let Indian judges try Europeans → European agitation = **"White Mutiny"**; exposed racial discrimination in the courts.
[172:1:8] - **Malaviya (Congress 1909):** Ripon "the greatest and most beloved Viceroy whom India has known"; Ripon favoured Indians (not British interests) and undid Lytton's illiberal acts.
[172:1:9] # Factory Acts
[172:1:10] TABLE head: Act | Viceroy | Provisions
  [172:1:10.0] First Factory Act, 1881 | Ripon | Children below 7 barred; children 7-12 max 9 hours; four holidays a month; dangerous machinery fenced
  [172:1:10.1] Second Factory Act, 1891 | Lansdowne | Child age 9-14, 7 hours; women 11 hours with 1½-hour break; weekly holiday
[172:1:11] - The demand came from the **Lancashire** lobby; the **early nationalists (moderates) did NOT support** the Acts of 1881 and 1891 (feared loss of Indian industry's edge).
[172:1:12] # Chronology of the Acts
[172:1:13] - **Indian Civil Service Act 1861 → Royal Titles Act 1876 → VPA 1878 → First Factory Act 1881** → Ilbert Bill 1883 → Councils Act 1892.

## SUB 2 | Hardinge II and 1911 – Durbar, Capital to Delhi, Bengal Reunited
[172:2:0] # The 12 December 1911 Durbar
[172:2:1] - **Delhi Durbar 1911:** coronation of **George V** and Queen Mary – the **only Durbar attended by the monarch** (1877 = Victoria / Lytton; 1903 = Edward VII / Curzon).
[172:2:2] - Announced: **Partition of Bengal annulled** (Bengal reunited **1911**); **capital moved from Calcutta to Delhi** (formal shift 1912; **New Delhi** by Lutyens and Baker, inaugurated **1931**); **Bihar and Orissa** a separate province (**1912**; Bihar alone from 1936).
[172:2:3] - The Viceroy who **proposed / effected** the transfer of capital = **Lord Hardinge II** (not Minto, Chelmsford, Reading, Curzon, Canning or Mayo).
[172:2:4] - Capital before Delhi = **Calcutta**.
[172:2:5] - **Gateway of India** (Bombay): marks George V's 1911 landing; **foundation stone 31 March 1913**; completed 1924.
[172:2:6] # Hardinge II (1910-16) – other facts
[172:2:7] - **Delhi Conspiracy / Hardinge Bomb Case:** bomb thrown at **Hardinge** on **23 Dec 1912** during his **state entry into Delhi** (Rash Behari Bose, Sachin Sanyal); he was wounded but did not change his attitude towards Indians.
[172:2:8] - Sympathetic to Indians in **South Africa**; **Gokhale's 1912** South Africa visit is linked with him; memoir **"My Indian Years"**; Hindu Mahasabha (1915).
[172:2:9] # Colonial architecture
[172:2:10] - Early 20th century **Indo-Saracenic / Indo-European hybrid** style (New Delhi, public buildings): by integrating Indian and European styles the British sought to show they were **legitimate rulers** of India – both statements true, II explains I.
[172:2:11] ~~ Sources: Spectrum Modern India p.852; PMF Modern Indian History Part 5 p.187; PMF Modern Indian History Part 4 p.74; PMF Art & Culture Part 4 (Gateway of India); NextGen Indian History - APPSC Exams Part 6 p.5; app notes 1:172
## SUB 3 | Lord Curzon – Partition, Archaeology, Tibet, Hostility to Congress
[172:3:0] # Partition of Bengal and "divide and rule"
[172:3:1] - **Partition of Bengal:** decided by **Curzon**; announced 20 July 1905, in force **16 Oct 1905** – the classic example of **"Divide and Rule"** (Hindu–Muslim split).
[172:3:2]   - Minto II's separate electorates (1909) are the other "divide and rule" step – answer **Curzon** when the question is the strategy behind 1905.
[172:3:3] - **Annulled in 1911** (Hardinge II) after the Swadeshi / Vande Mataram agitation.
[172:3:4] # Archaeology
[172:3:5] TABLE head: Year | Event
  [172:3:5.0] 1784 | Asiatic Society (William Jones)
  [172:3:5.1] 1861 | Canning made Alexander Cunningham Archaeological Surveyor – ASI begun
  [172:3:5.2] 1871 | ASI a separate department
  [172:3:5.3] 1902 | Curzon revived it as a central Department of Archaeology; John Marshall Director-General
  [172:3:5.4] 1904 | Ancient Monuments Preservation Act – Curzon
[172:3:6] - **⚠ Disputed:** "Who set up the Department of Archaeology / ASI?" – exam keys (APPSC G1 2012, UPPSC) answer **Curzon**; strictly the ASI dates from **1861 under Canning**. Pick Curzon unless the question says 1861 / Cunningham.
[172:3:7] - **ASI today:** an **attached office of the Ministry of Culture**; motto "Pratnakirti Apavarini"; AMASR Act 1958 protects monuments over 100 years old.
[172:3:8] # Other Curzon measures
[172:3:9] - **Younghusband Mission to Tibet (1903-04)**; **Imperial Cadet Corps (1901)**; NWFP (1901); Police Commission (Frazer, 1902); Universities Commission (Raleigh, 1902) + **Indian Universities Act 1904**; Calcutta Corporation Act 1899; Dept of Commerce and Industry.
[172:3:10] - **On the Congress (1900):** "The Congress is **tottering to its fall**, and one of my great ambitions while in India is to assist it to a **peaceful demise**" – **Curzon** (not Dufferin, Minto or Hamilton).
[172:3:11] - Aloof, bureaucratic "efficiency" rule – credited in keys with the view that government should keep away from the people.
[172:3:12] - **Tenure 1899-1905:** the longest among Curzon, Dufferin, Hardinge, Mayo (overall longest Viceroy: **Linlithgow**, 1936-43).

=== NOTES 1:173 | Company's Constitutional Framework – Regulating Act 1773, Pitt's India Act 1784, Act of 1786
## SUB 0 | Regulating Act 1773 – Background, Provisions, Supreme Court
[173:0:0] # Before 1773
[173:0:1] - **1764** Buxar → **1765** Diwani of Bengal, Bihar, Orissa granted by **Shah Alam II**.
[173:0:2] - **1767:** first Parliamentary intervention – an Act obliged the Company to pay the British treasury **£400,000 a year**; dividend raised to/capped at **10%**.
[173:0:3] - **1772:** Select Committee (**Burgoyne**) and Secret Committee; the Company sought a £1 million loan → **Regulating Act, 1773**.
[173:0:4] # Provisions
[173:0:5] - **First step** by the British Parliament to **control and regulate** the Company; recognised its political and administrative functions; foundation of central administration.
[173:0:6] - **Governor of Bengal → Governor-General of Bengal** (**Warren Hastings**), with a **council of four** (Francis, Clavering, Monson, Barwell); majority decisions, GG only a **casting vote** (no veto) → Hastings outvoted.
[173:0:7] - **Bombay and Madras subordinated** to Bengal.
[173:0:8] - **Directors to submit** to the British Government all communications on the **civil and military affairs of Bengal** and the **revenues of India**.
[173:0:9] - **Supreme Court at Calcutta (Fort William), 1774:** **1 Chief Justice (Sir Elijah Impey) + 3 judges** – India's **first Supreme Court**, set up by the **Regulating Act** (not Pitt's India Act, the 1858, 1909 or 1919 Acts, nor the Charter Act 1853 or GoI Act 1935); its jurisdiction vis-à-vis the Council was **left vague**. Later Supreme Courts: **Madras (1801)** – serving the Madras Presidency incl. Andhra – and Bombay (1823).
[173:0:10] - Did **not** abolish the Court of Directors; did not create the Board of Control (1784).
[173:0:11] - **⚠ Trap statements:** "Board of Control of six commissioners" = Pitt's Act 1784; "officers to disclose property within two months of joining" = Pitt's Act 1784; "Supreme Court's powers clearly defined" = false.
[173:0:12] ~~ Sources: Laxmikanth Indian Polity 8th ed. Part 1 p.25; PMF Modern Indian History Part 2 p.101-104; Spectrum Modern India p.534-535; app notes 1:173
## SUB 1 | Act of Settlement 1781, Pitt's India Act 1784 and Act of 1786
[173:1:0] # Amending Act 1781 (Act of Settlement)
[173:1:1] - GG, Council and servants **exempt from the Supreme Court** for official acts; **revenue** matters excluded; Court to apply **personal laws of Hindus and Muslims**.
[173:1:2] # Pitt's India Act 1784
[173:1:3] - **Board of Control** created (**six commissioners**, incl. Chancellor of the Exchequer and a Secretary of State) for **political** affairs; **Court of Directors** kept **commercial** affairs → **double government** (ended by the GoI Act 1858).
[173:1:4] - Company territories first called **"British possessions in India"**; GG's council cut to **three**; Pitt's Act came under **Warren Hastings**.
[173:1:5] # Act of 1786
[173:1:6] - Passed to meet **Lord Cornwallis's** conditions: the GG could **override his Council** (reject its decision) in special cases and also be **Commander-in-Chief** – not the Regulating Act, Pitt's Act, the Charter Act 1793 or the Act of 1813.
[173:1:7] - **Charter Act 1793** extended the override to all future GGs and presidency Governors.
[173:1:8] - **Order:** 1773 → 1781 → 1784 → 1786 → 1793.
[173:1:9] # Landmarks vs non-landmarks
[173:1:10] - Landmarks of constitutional development: Regulating Act 1773, Charter Act 1833, GoI Act 1919 …; the **Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955** (renamed from the Untouchability (Offences) Act in 1976) is **post-independence**, not a British-era landmark.
[173:1:11] ~~ Sources: Laxmikanth Indian Polity 8th ed. Part 1 p.26-27; PMF Modern Indian History Part 2 p.104-105; Spectrum Modern India p.535-536; app notes 1:173

=== NOTES 1:174 | Charter Acts of 1793, 1813, 1833 and 1853 – Provisions and Significance
## SUB 0 | Charter Act 1813 – End of India Trade Monopoly, Education, Missionaries
[174:0:0] - **Trade:** ended the Company's **monopoly of Indian trade** (thrown open to all British merchants) **except tea, opium and the China trade** – a statement "abolished the monopoly **including its tea trade**" is false.
[174:0:1] - **Sovereignty:** asserted the **sovereignty of the British Crown** over the Company's Indian territories; charter renewed for **20 years**.
[174:0:2] - **Education:** at least **Rs 1 lakh a year** for the revival of literature and promotion of science among Indians – first State responsibility for education (the chief reason the Act is called important).
[174:0:3] - **Missionaries** allowed to come to India and preach (Wilberforce, Charles Grant).
[174:0:4] - Courts in India got more power over European British subjects; local governments could tax persons; separate commercial and territorial accounts.
[174:0:5] - **UPSC 2019:** (1) monopoly ended except tea and China ✓ (2) Crown sovereignty ✓ (3) "revenues now controlled by Parliament" ✗ → **1 and 2 only**.
[174:0:6] > Which Act ended the Company's India trade monopoly? **1813** (1833 ended the tea/China monopoly).

## SUB 1 | Charter Act 1833 – Governor-General of India and Centralisation
[174:1:0] - **GG of Bengal → Governor-General of India** (first: **Lord William Bentinck**) – the **final step towards centralisation**; GG-in-Council got exclusive law-making for all British India; laws called **Acts** (earlier Regulations).
[174:1:1] - **Trade:** ended the remaining monopoly in **tea and the China trade**; Company became a purely **administrative** body; Europeans free to settle.
[174:1:2] - **Law Member** added as **4th member**: **T.B. Macaulay** (a Briton) – **no Indian Law Member** was provided; **First Law Commission (1834)** under **Macaulay** to codify Indian laws (IPC). The Law Member post is the origin of the **Ministry of Law and Justice**.
[174:1:3] - **Section 87:** no Indian to be barred from office by religion, birth, descent or colour; slavery to be abolished (done 1843).
[174:1:4] - Territories held **"in trust for His Majesty"** (Laxmikanth); the 1853 Act repeated it without a term.
[174:1:5] - **Agra Presidency:** almost all of present UP separated from Bengal Presidency in **1834** → North-Western Provinces (1836).
[174:1:6] - **Macaulay** (Commons, 1833 Bill): "The Company is an **anomaly** but it is part of a system where everything is an anomaly".

## SUB 2 | Charter Act 1853 – Legislative Council and Open Competition
[174:2:0] - **Last** Charter Act; Company's rule renewed **with no fixed term** ("in trust" for the Crown till Parliament provides otherwise).
[174:2:1] - **First functioning Legislative Council:** legislative and executive functions separated; **6 new legislative members** (4 from **Madras, Bombay, Bengal, Agra** + **Chief Justice** + one Calcutta judge) → Central Legislative Council of 12, a **"mini-Parliament"**.
[174:2:2] - **Civil services:** Directors' patronage ended → **open competitive examination** (the Act of **1853**; not 1833, 1858 or 1882); **Macaulay Committee 1854**.
[174:2:3] - **Imperial Legislative Council** (under the Indian Councils Act) was British India's legislature from **1861** to 1947.

=== NOTES 1:175 | Land Revenue Systems – Permanent Settlement, Ryotwari and Mahalwari; Areas and Effects
## SUB 0 | Ryotwari System – Read, Munro, Baramahal; Features and Areas
[175:0:0] # Origin and First Areas
[175:0:1] - **Devised by:** **Captain Alexander Read** (first experiment) and **Thomas Munro** – not Cornwallis, Elphinstone or Holt Mackenzie; also called the **"Munro system"**.
[175:0:2] - **First experiment:** **Baramahal (1792)** – territory ceded by Tipu after the Third Mysore War (Treaty of Srirangapatnam, 1792); Read was Superintendent of Revenue, Munro his assistant (1792-99).
[175:0:3]   - Baramahal = present **Salem** and environs (Krishnagiri, Dharmapuri, Tiruvannamalai) – not Anantapur.
[175:0:4] - **First in Andhra:** the **Ceded Districts (Rayalaseema)** by Munro, after his survey (1802-07); old notes date it **1802** (one APPSC stem says 1808 – still Munro).
[175:0:5] - **Whole Madras Presidency:** officially enforced by Munro as **Governor (1820)**; approved by Lord Hastings.
[175:0:6] - **Bombay:** extended by Munro's disciple **Mountstuart Elphinstone** after 1818 (Pringle's Ricardo-based survey; later Wingate and Goldsmid).
[175:0:7] - **Areas:** **Madras**, **Bombay**, **Assam**, Coorg, Burma – about **51%** of British India; so Ryotwari = **South India** first, West India (Bombay) second.
[175:0:8] > "First in Madras Presidency" = Baramahal; "first in Andhra" = Rayalaseema; "prevalent in" = South India (Madras).
[175:0:9] # Features
[175:0:10] - **Settlement directly with the ryot**, who paid revenue **directly to the Government** – no intermediary; peasant recognised as **owner (proprietor)**.
[175:0:11] - Ryots **registered and given pattas**; land **surveyed and assessed before being taxed** (UPSC 2012: all three statements correct).
[175:0:12] - Ryot could **sell, mortgage, gift, lease or sublet**; not evicted while the assessment was paid.
[175:0:13] - Assessment fixed on the **land, not the crop**; **revised periodically – about every 30 years** (20-30).
[175:0:14] - First demand **50%** of estimated produce (not 30%); **no automatic remission** after a bad harvest – only occasional reduction.
[175:0:15] - Munro: **"The Ryotwari is a system which has always prevailed in India."**
[175:0:16] # In Practice – Criticism
[175:0:17] - "Many zamindars replaced by **one giant zamindar**" – the State as **ultimate landlord**; revenue very high, enhanced at will, payable even in drought.
[175:0:18] - Arbitrary **'putcut'** assessment where no survey; over-assessment, poor records, unclear boundaries.

## SUB 1 | Permanent Settlement (Zamindari) – Cornwallis 1793
[175:1:0] # Origin
[175:1:1] - Warren Hastings' five-year farming (1772) → annual leases → Cornwallis' **decennial (10-year) settlement, 1789** → declared **permanent in 1793**.
[175:1:2] - **Introduced by Lord Cornwallis, 1793**, in **Bengal and Bihar**, then Orissa, the northern districts of Madras (Circars, 1802) and Varanasi – about **19%** of British India; **planned by John Shore** (with James Grant).
[175:1:3] - "Introduced in **1795**" is wrong; Madras and Coorg were **not** under it.
[175:1:4] # Features
[175:1:5] - Made with **zamindars**, recognised as **owners (proprietors)**; rights **hereditary, transferable, saleable** (alienable, rentable, heritable).
[175:1:6] - Revenue **fixed for ever**; zamindar paid **10/11** of the rental, kept **1/11**.
[175:1:7] - **Sunset Law:** revenue due by sunset of the date, else the estate was sold.
[175:1:8] - Cultivators reduced to **tenants-at-will**; many zamindars **did not issue pattas** because there was **no official check** on them.
[175:1:9] - **Regulations of 1799 (Haftam) and 1812 (Panjam)** let zamindars seize tenants' property without a court.
[175:1:10] # Why a fixed, high demand
[175:1:11] - Stable income for the Company, cheap collection through few zamindars, a **loyal landlord class (political allies)**; officials hoped that with ownership and a fixed demand **zamindars would invest in land** so that area and yield rose.
[175:1:12] - Drawback for the Company: after **1810 prices rose**, the zamindars gained, the Company could not raise its share → it was **rarely extended beyond Bengal** (UPSC-type: Statement I true, II false).
[175:1:13] # Results
[175:1:14] - **Ryots at the mercy of zamindars**; stable income for the Company; zamindars **allies of the Company** (not deprived of ownership).
[175:1:15] - By 1815 about half of Bengal's estates sold for arrears → new landlords: **urban merchants and moneylenders**; **absentee landlordism, sub-infeudation, rack-renting**.
[175:1:16] - **Litigation rose after 1793** because of Cornwallis' **judicial reforms (abolition of court fees, separate civil courts)** – not the land provisions (UPSC 2011 key: none of the three).
[175:1:17] - Profits of agriculture went to the **zamindars**, not the cultivators or the Company.
[175:1:18] - **Bengal Tenancy Act, 1885:** tenants' (occupancy) rights in **Bengal and Bihar**.
[175:1:19] ~~ Sources: PMF Modern History Part 3 (src/indian-history/pmf-modern-history-03.txt) p.55-60, p.83-84; NextGen Indian History - APPSC Exams Part 6 (src/indian-history/nextgen-indian-history-appsc-06.txt) p.24; Spectrum Modern India (src/indian-history/spectrum-modern-india.txt) p.576; https://www.pmfias.com/cornwallis-permanent-settlement-litigation-pyq-2011/; app notes 1:175
[175:1:20] - **Chronology:** Permanent Settlement 1793 → Subsidiary Alliance 1798 → Doctrine of Lapse 1848 → Partition of Bengal 1905.
## SUB 2 | Mahalwari and Taluqdari; the Three Systems Compared
[175:2:0] # Mahalwari
[175:2:1] - **Devised by Holt Mackenzie, 1822** (Regulation VII of 1822) in the **North-Western Provinces of the Bengal Presidency** – the "father" of Mahalwari and the first officer to try it.
[175:2:2] - **1833:** revised under **William Bentinck** by **R.M. Bird** (Regulation IX of 1833, "father of land settlement in north India"), carried on by **James Thomason**.
[175:2:3] - **Areas:** **North-Western Provinces (Agra-Oudh), Punjab**, Gangetic valley, parts of Central India – about **30%**.
[175:2:4] - **Unit:** the **mahal** (village or group of villages); land owned **jointly by the village community** (not the government); common ownership = **"Bhai Chara" / "Mahali"**.
[175:2:5] - Revenue fixed per village, collected by the **village headman (lambardar)**; each cultivator paid his share, **village collectively liable**; **periodically revised** (not fixed).
[175:2:6] # Taluqdari
[175:2:7] - **Taluqdari = Awadh (Oudh)** – "Taluqdari – Bombay" is the wrong pair; taluqdar = landlord-middleman.
[175:2:8] # Comparison
[175:2:9] TABLE head: Basis | Permanent | Ryotwari | Mahalwari

## SUB 4 | Effects of the Land Systems; Revenue in Andhra
[175:4:0] # Effects
[175:4:1] - Rigid **cash revenue**, payable even when crops failed → loans from **moneylenders** → mortgage and **loss of land**; the moneylender became the **greatest evil** of the village economy.
[175:4:2] - Cash revenue pushed **commercial crops** (indigo, cotton, opium, jute); the **American Civil War** cotton slump (1866) → defaults → land to moneylenders.
[175:4:3] - New classes: **merchant-landlords** in Zamindari areas; **moneylender-landlords** in Ryotwari and Mahalwari areas.
[175:4:4] - **Alice Thorner:** Ryotwari destroyed the village system and isolated farmers from the government.
[175:4:5] # Andhra
[175:4:6] TABLE head: Year | Event
  [175:4:6.0] 1765-66 | Northern Circars (Sarkars) obtained – Mughal farman and treaty with the Nizam

  [175:4:6.5] 1846 | Uyyalawada Narasimha Reddy's revolt – attacked the Koilkuntla (Kovela Kuntla) treasury
  [175:4:6.6] 1858 | Madras Inam Commission – checked rent-free inam titles
  [175:4:6.7] 1948 | Madras Estates (Abolition and Conversion into Ryotwari) Act – zamindari abolished
[175:4:7] - **Pattern:** **Zamindari** in the coastal Circars (zamindars paid **peshcush**); **Ryotwari** in **Rayalaseema** (Ceded Districts); Mahalwari-type (headmen) in **Nellore**.
[175:4:8] - **Haveli** = land under direct Company control; grouped into **mootahs** and auctioned (1802) – held as proprietary estates, many fell into arrears; **Moturpha** = tax on professions, trades and looms; **Inam** = rent-free title.
[175:4:9] - Permanent Settlement in Andhra (1802): **auction system abolished, tax fixed**.
[175:4:10] - **Mirasidar** (Madras) = holder of mirasi rights, the designated revenue payer; **Banian** = Indian agent of the Company; **Aurang** = warehouse/workshop (not the treasury) – UPSC 2020.

=== NOTES 1:176 | Economic Impact of Colonial Rule – Drain of Wealth, Deindustrialisation, Commercialisation of Agriculture
## SUB 0 | Drain of Wealth and the Economic Nationalists
[176:0:0] # Meaning
[176:0:1] - **Drain of wealth:** the part of India's wealth carried to **Britain** without **adequate economic or material return** – peculiar to **British** rule (not Portuguese, Dutch or French).
[176:0:2] - **Began** with the **Diwani of Bengal (1765)** – Bengal's revenue bought the Company's export "investments".
[176:0:3] - India usually had a **favourable balance of trade**; the drain took the form of **unrequited exports** (export surplus paid for Home Charges, British office and war expenses) – NOT an excess of imports.
[176:0:4] - In the 18th century the drain was about **2-3% of Britain's national income** (Spectrum) and nearly 9% of India's income (PMF) – it helped finance Britain's industrial revolution.
[176:0:5] # Components (Home Charges and more)
[176:0:6] TABLE head: Component | What it covered
  [176:0:6.0] Salaries, pensions | Of British civil and military officials, remitted home
  [176:0:6.1] Interest | On the public debt raised in Britain
  [176:0:6.2] Railway guarantee | 5% assured return to British investors, paid from Indian revenue
  [176:0:6.3] Military | British Indian Army and wars abroad – the largest budget head
  [176:0:6.4] Profits | Of British capitalists, merchants, banks, shipping and insurance
[176:0:7] - **Reason per Naoroji's theory (APPSC FRO):** profits taken by British capitalists from India.
[176:0:8] - Naoroji: what was drained was a **"potential surplus"** that could have developed India; imperialists claimed linking India to the world capitalist market was itself modernisation (both true, II does not explain I).
[176:0:9] # Dadabhai Naoroji
[176:0:10] - **"Grand Old Man of India"**; propounded and popularised the **drain theory**; first stated in **"England's Debt to India" / "England's Duties to India" (1867)**; coined **"moral drain"**.
[176:0:11] - Book: ***Poverty and Un-British Rule in India* (1901)**; also ***Poverty of India: Papers and Statistics*** (from his 1876 paper "The Poverty of India"); he used the phrase **"Un-British"**.
[176:0:12] - **First estimate of per capita income: Rs 20 (1867-68)** – not Ranade, Hunter or R.C. Dutt.
[176:0:13] - Showed (1880) that a **jail convict's food and clothing cost about 50% more** than the average Indian's income (old app key; not checked).
[176:0:14] - First Indian elected to the **House of Commons (1892, Finsbury Central)**; first to make **Swaraj** the national demand (Calcutta Congress, 1906).
[176:0:15] # Other Economic Critics
[176:0:16] TABLE head: Writer | Work
  [176:0:16.0] R.C. Dutt | *Economic History of India* (2 vols, 1901-03) – economic record of colonial rule; *Open Letters to Lord Curzon* (1900)

  [176:0:16.2] William Digby | *'Prosperous' British India* (1901)

## SUB 1 | Stages of Colonialism; One-way Free Trade and Deindustrialisation
[176:1:0] # Stages (R.P. Dutt, *India Today*)
[176:1:1] TABLE head: Stage | Period | Feature
  [176:1:1.0] Mercantilist (merchant capital) | 1757-1813 | Company monopoly; mostly purchase of Indian goods for export, paid from Bengal revenue
  [176:1:1.1] Free trade (industrial capital) | 1813-1858 | India a market for British goods and supplier of raw materials
  [176:1:1.2] Finance capital | 1858 onwards | British capital in railways, plantations, banks
[176:1:2] - India changed from a **leading exporter of manufactures** to a **supplier of raw materials** – an **agricultural colony of manufacturing Britain**.
[176:1:3] # One-way Free Trade
[176:1:4] - **Charter Act of 1813** ended the Company's trade monopoly → **one-way free trade**: British goods entered India **duty-free / nominal duty**; Indian textiles paid **heavy duties in Britain** (already from the 1720 laws) – so "tax-free import of Indian textiles into the UK" is false.
[176:1:5] - **Imperial preference:** special privileges (lower duties) for **British imports in India**.
[176:1:6] - **Cotton excise (1894-96)** on Indian mill cloth to offset the import duty on British goods; **Suez Canal (1869)**.
[176:1:7] - **Railways:** built by private British companies with a **5% guarantee from Indian revenue** (not financed by the British government); they carried British goods inland and raw materials out – **accelerated the decay of handicrafts**.
[176:1:8] - Karl Marx: British economic policy in India "disgusting"; the British **"inundated the very mother country of cotton with cottons"**.
[176:1:9] # Deindustrialisation
[176:1:10] - Weavers coerced (1769 directive on silk; Bolt 1772); Lancashire cloth in Bengal **1786**; by **1850** India took **one-fourth** of British cotton exports.
[176:1:11] - **Industrial Revolution (first half of the 19th century):** **Indian handicrafts were ruined**; **raw cotton** exports rose (not textile exports).
[176:1:12] - Process intensified **after 1813**, aggravated by the end of the Company's monopoly (UPPSC key: both statements).
[176:1:13] - Other causes: loss of princely patrons, export of raw materials, transit duties, British privileges (**B.D. Basu**); shipbuilding crushed by the 1813-14 laws.
[176:1:14] - **Share of world manufactures:** about **24.5% (1750)** → under **2% (1900)** (Paul Bairoch).
[176:1:15] - **Results:** urban handicrafts' sudden collapse; **artisans crowded into agriculture**; **Dhaka** (muslin) declined while Calcutta, Madras, Bombay grew.
[176:1:16] - **No independent industrial development:** **absence of heavy (capital-goods) industries**; laissez-faire did **not** promote indigenous capitalism.
[176:1:17] ~~ Sources: PMF Modern History Part 3 (src/indian-history/pmf-modern-history-03.txt) p.69-72, p.78, p.80-81; Spectrum Modern India (src/indian-history/spectrum-modern-india.txt) p.577-579, p.583; NextGen Indian History - APPSC Exams Part 6 p.25; app notes 1:176
[176:1:18] - **Foreign trade** of British India grew **artificially (fostered)** – raw materials out, British manufactures in – not by the natural growth of the economy.
[176:1:19] - **Calico Acts:** 1700 – ban on imports of printed calicoes; **1721 – ban on (imports and use of) printed cotton textiles**, to protect British wool and silk makers.
