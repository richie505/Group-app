=== NOTES 4:3 | Concept of Time – Local and Standard Time, Gmt/utc, Indian Standard Time, Time Zones, International Date Line
## SUB 0 | Longitude and Local Time
[3:0:0] - Earth turns **360° in 24 h**, **west to east**, so places to the **east are ahead** (add) and places to the **west behind** (subtract). Moving west, local time falls behind.
[3:0:1] TABLE head: Longitude gap | Time gap
  [3:0:1.0] 1° | 4 minutes
  [3:0:1.1] 15° | 1 hour
  [3:0:1.2] 180° | 12 hours
  [3:0:1.3] 1' of arc | 4 seconds
[3:0:2] # Method
[3:0:3] - 1. Longitude gap: **same side subtract**, **opposite sides add** (25°E and 35°W = 60° = 4 h).
[3:0:4] - 2. Gap × 4 min = time gap.
[3:0:5] - 3. **Add if east**, subtract if west.
[3:0:6] - Flight/message: arrival local time = departure time + journey time ± longitude time (add when going east). Depart 10:00 a.m., 3-hour flight, 15° east → **2:00 p.m.**

## SUB 1 | Time Terms – Gmt, Utc, Dst
[3:1:0] TABLE head: Term | Meaning
  [3:1:0.0] GMT | Mean solar time at Royal Observatory, Greenwich (0°); Prime Meridian fixed at International Meridian Conference, 1884
  [3:1:0.1] UTC | World standard kept by caesium atomic clocks; corrected with leap seconds
  [3:1:0.2] Daylight Saving Time | Clocks +1 h in summer (mid/high latitudes) to use evening light; India does not use it
  [3:1:0.3] Natural units | Day (rotation), month (Moon's revolution), year (Earth's revolution)
[3:1:1] - Standard time is set from the Prime Meridian; **180°** = Date Line, **12 h** (720 min) from Greenwich.
[3:1:2] - **NavIC** (7 satellites: 3 GEO + 4 GSO) uses **rubidium** clocks; IRNSS-1F clock failed.
[3:1:3] UTC = caesium; NavIC = rubidium.

## SUB 5 | International Date Line
[3:5:0] - Imaginary pole-to-pole line roughly along **180°**, in the **middle of the Pacific Ocean** (**APPSC 2021**); passes the **Bering Strait** (Chukchi Peninsula, Russia / Seward Peninsula, Alaska).
[3:5:1] - **Zigzags** where land is in the way: Chukotka, **Aleutians**, Kiribati, Fiji, Tonga, Samoa.
[3:5:2] TABLE head: Crossing | Date change
  [3:5:2.0] Eastward (Asia → America) | Subtract a day (repeat)
  [3:5:2.1] Westward (America → Asia) | Add a day (skip)
  [3:5:2.2] Place | Fact
  [3:5:2.3] Big Diomede (Russia) / Little Diomede (USA) | Neighbours, different dates
  [3:5:2.4] Samoa | Moved to the Asian side in 2011

[3:5:3] - Old key: crossing 180° east to west a day is lost, west to east gained; shortest ship path is along the meridian.

[3:6:0] - Tropic of Cancer at **23°26'N** (written 23½°N); divides India into almost two halves (not the boundary).
[3:6:1] - Crosses **8 states** (**APPSC 2025**), W→E: **Gujarat** (1st), **Rajasthan**, **MP** (3rd), **Chhattisgarh**, **Jharkhand** (5th), **West Bengal**, **Tripura** (7th), **Mizoram**. Not Odisha, UP, Bihar (older keys wrote Bihar for Jharkhand).
[3:6:2] - Wholly north: UP, Bihar, Punjab, Haryana, Himachal, Uttarakhand, Sikkim, Assam, Meghalaya, Arunachal, Nagaland, **Manipur**. **AP wholly south**.

## SUB 7 | Rotation, Axial Tilt and Seasons
[3:7:0] - Rotation (west to east) gives day and night and time differences; the **circle of illumination** divides day from night; Coriolis deflects winds right in NH.
[3:7:1] - Axis **23½°** from the perpendicular = **66½°** to the orbital plane. **Parallelism** (angular momentum) → **opposite seasons** in two hemispheres.
[3:7:2] - Tilt gives seasons, unequal day length, tropics and polar circles – **not** day and night.
[3:7:3] - **Precession**: **Vega** was pole star ~14,000 years ago, again in ~12,000 years. **Milankovitch cycles** drive long-term climate change.
[3:7:4] - Melting ice shifts mass away from the axis → slows rotation.
[3:7:5] - To walk east, keep the **pole star on your left**. Stars rise perpendicular to the horizon at the Equator.

=== NOTES 4:4 | Seasons – Inclination of the Axis, Solstices and Equinoxes, Distribution of Insolation
## SUB 0 | Why Seasons Occur
[4:0:0] - Earth **revolves** round the Sun in **365.25 days** with its axis tilted **23½° from the perpendicular** (**66½° to the orbital plane**) – the key reason (**APPSC 2018**).
[4:0:1] - **Parallelism**: the axis keeps pointing the same way (towards the Pole Star) during revolution.
[4:0:2] - Seasons come from **axial inclination + parallelism + revolution + spherical shape** → changing **angle of insolation** and **day length**.
[4:0:3] - Hemispheres tilt alternately towards (summer) and away (winter) from the Sun; so seasons in the two hemispheres are **opposite**.
[4:0:4] - Tilt + revolution also give changing day length, solstices and equinoxes, six-month polar day and night.
[4:0:5] Rotation causes day and night, not seasons. Revolution alone is not enough – it needs axial tilt.
## SUB 1 | Distance Is Not the Cause
[4:1:0] TABLE head: Point | Date | NH season
  [4:1:0.0] Perihelion (nearest) | ~3 January | Winter
  [4:1:0.1] Aphelion (farthest) | early July | Summer
[4:1:1] - Earth is nearest the Sun during **northern winter**, so distance cannot cause seasons; **tilt** does (solar angle + day length).
[4:1:2] Seasons = axial tilt, not Earth–Sun distance.
## SUB 2 | Solstices and Equinoxes
[4:2:0] TABLE head: Date | Event | Sun overhead at
  [4:2:0.0] 21 March | Vernal (spring) equinox | Equator
  [4:2:0.1] 21 June | Summer solstice (NH) | Tropic of Cancer
  [4:2:0.2] 23 September | Autumnal equinox | Equator
  [4:2:0.3] 22 December | Winter solstice (NH) | Tropic of Capricorn
[4:2:1] - Sun overhead at the Equator **twice a year**: 21 March and 23 September; day and night equal everywhere.
[4:2:2] - **21 March – 23 September**: Sun between Equator and Tropic of Cancer → **NH summer**.
[4:2:3] - **21 June** = **International Day of Yoga** (2026 theme "Yoga for Healthy Ageing") – marks the summer solstice.
## SUB 3 | Day Length by Latitude
[4:3:0] TABLE head: Latitude | 21 June | Equinoxes | 22 Dec
  [4:3:0.0] Equator | 12 h | 12 h | 12 h
  [4:3:0.1] Tropic of Cancer | ~13½ h | 12 h | ~10½ h
  [4:3:0.2] Arctic Circle | 24 h day | 12 h | 24 h night
  [4:3:0.3] North Pole | Continuous day | Sunrise/sunset | Continuous night
[4:3:1] - Place with **equal day and night all year**: the **Equator**.
[4:3:2] - On **21 June** the Sun **does not set at the Arctic Circle** (makes a full circle above the horizon).
[4:3:3] - **Midnight sun** = Sun visible above the horizon at midnight.
## SUB 4 | Summer vs Winter
[4:4:0] TABLE head: Basis | Summer | Winter
  [4:4:0.0] Hemisphere | Tilted towards Sun | Tilted away
  [4:4:0.1] Rays | More direct | More oblique (slanting)
  [4:4:0.2] Energy | Concentrated, more heating | Spread over larger area, less heating
  [4:4:0.3] Days | Longer | Shorter
  [4:4:0.4] Sun altitude / shadow | Higher / shorter | Lower / longer
[4:4:1] - Noon is hotter in summer at a place like Delhi because in winter the rays fall **more slanting**.
[4:4:2] - Example: NH tilted towards the Sun → NH summer.
## SUB 5 | Insolation – Factors and Distribution
[4:5:0] - Factors: **angle of incidence** (vertical rays heat a smaller area), **duration of day**, **transparency of atmosphere**, **land configuration**.
[4:5:1] Direction of ocean tides is NOT a factor of insolation.
[4:5:2] - Insolation **falls from the tropics to the poles**.
[4:5:3] - Maximum over **cloudless subtropical deserts**, not the Equator.
## SUB 6 | Seasons by Hemisphere and Latitude Zone
[4:6:0] TABLE head: Zone | Seasonal feature
  [4:6:0.0] Low latitudes | Small temperature contrast; wet and dry seasons
  [4:6:0.1] Subtropics | Hot summers, mild winters
  [4:6:0.2] Mid-latitudes | Four distinct seasons; large summer–winter temperature gaps
  [4:6:0.3] High latitudes | Largest swings in day length and insolation
[4:6:1] - Greatest seasonal contrast: **high latitudes** (**APPSC-G1 2023**); another key gave **mid-latitudes** for the biggest summer–winter temperature gap.
[4:6:2] - **Southern Hemisphere**: longest day ~**22 December**, shortest ~21 June; summer in **January** (its hottest month).
[4:6:3] - **Christmas in Australia falls in summer** – Australia is in the Southern Hemisphere.
## SUB 7 | Shadows and Places in India
[4:7:0] - Vertically **overhead Sun → no noon shadow**. Higher Sun → shorter shadow.
[4:7:1] - **21 June**: no noon shadow on the **Tropic of Cancer**; north India's noon shadow is **smallest on 21 June** and longest in December.
[4:7:2] - Equator: no noon shadow in March and September.
[4:7:3] - Overhead Sun only **between the Tropics**, twice a year (**zero-shadow days**). **Delhi, Srinagar** (north of 23½°N) **never** get it (⚠ APPSC key (2002) gave Mumbai, which lies south of the Tropic).
[4:7:4] - **AP** (12°41'N–19°07'N) lies between Equator and Tropic of Cancer → overhead noon Sun **twice a year**, high temperatures all year.
[4:7:5] - **21 June day length grows northward**: **Srinagar > Delhi > Hyderabad > Chennai > Kanyakumari**. Longest summer sunlight: **Srinagar**.
[4:7:6] # Indian and AP Seasons
[4:7:7] - Hot weather season **March–May** (Sun's northward march): ~**50°C at Ganganagar (1935)**; ~10% rain pre-monsoon.
[4:7:8] - **IMD's four seasons**: winter (Jan–Feb) → pre-monsoon (Mar–May) → South-West monsoon → post-monsoon. Traditional: six **ritus** (Vasanta to Shishira).
