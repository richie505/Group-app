=== NOTES 5:83 | International Treaties on Chemicals and Waste – Basel, Rotterdam, Stockholm POPs, Minamata; Montreal Protocol and Kigali Amendment
## SUB 0 | Ozone and the Ozone Layer
[83:0:0] # Ozone
[83:0:1] - Formula **O₃** – three oxygen atoms; formed by **UV radiation + O₂ → O₃** (ozone–oxygen cycle).
[83:0:2] - Concentrated in the **stratosphere** (about **10–50 km**), with most ozone near **20–25 km**; **absorbs harmful UV-B**.
[83:0:3] - Stratospheric ozone is naturally controlled by **nitrogen dioxide**.
[83:0:4] # Measurement – Dobson Unit
[83:0:5] - **Dobson Unit (DU)** measures the thickness/concentration of ozone in a **column of air from ground to top of atmosphere**.
[83:0:6] - **1 DU = 0.01 mm** of pure ozone at **0°C and 1 atm (STP)**.
[83:0:7] - **"Ozone hole"** = total column ozone **below 220 DU**.
## SUB 1 | Ozone Hole
[83:1:0] - First detected over **Antarctica**; first reported **1985** by **British Antarctic Survey** scientists.
[83:1:1] - Season: **Southern Hemisphere spring, August–November**.
[83:1:2] - Causes of the Antarctic hole: a prominent **polar front (vortex)**, **polar stratospheric clouds** and inflow of **CFCs**.
[83:1:3] - **Polar stratospheric clouds** form in extreme cold; **nitric acid** on their surfaces reacts with CFCs → stable chlorine becomes **active chlorine** → destroys ozone **when sunlight returns**.
[83:1:4] - **Tibetan ozone low ("halo")** over the Tibetan Plateau (2005) – **G. W. Kent Moore**, University of Toronto.
[83:1:5] - Dense stratospheric ozone pocket at **21–23 km** over the **North Bay of Bengal** (16 Aug 2026).
[83:1:6] # Recovery Projection
[83:1:7] TABLE head: Region | Back to 1980 levels
  [83:1:7.0] Most of the world | 2040
  [83:1:7.1] Arctic | 2045
  [83:1:7.2] Antarctic | 2066
[83:1:8] 2040 – 2045 – 2066 = world – Arctic – Antarctic
## SUB 2 | CFCs – Main Cause of Depletion
[83:2:0] - **CFCs (chlorofluorocarbons)** are the **main cause** (APPSC 2025, twice): under UV they release **chlorine** in the stratosphere, which breaks ozone.
[83:2:1] - **Chlorine and bromine** destroy ozone **catalytically** – not consumed in the first reaction.
[83:2:2] - An increase in **halocarbons** depletes the ozone layer.
[83:2:3] - CFCs are also **greenhouse gases** (~15% of the greenhouse effect) – they cause both warming and ozone loss.
[83:2:4] - CFC uses: refrigerators, ACs, **aerosol propellants (pressurising agents)**, **plastic foams**, **cleaning electronic components**, solvents – **not tubeless tyres**.
[83:2:5] CO₂, methane and N₂ are the usual wrong options – they are not the main ozone depleters.
[83:2:6] - **APPSC-G2 2025 assertion-reason**: A = "CFCs are GHGs and increase UV"; R = "CFCs are man-made (fridges, ACs, aerosols)" → **both true, R is NOT the explanation** (R only says where CFCs are used).
## SUB 3 | Ods vs Non-ods – Uses
[83:3:0] TABLE head: Substance | ODS? | Main uses / sources
  [83:3:0.0] CFCs | Yes – main culprit | Refrigerators, ACs, aerosols, foams, solvents
  [83:3:0.1] Halons (bromine; 1211, 1301) | Yes – ~100 times more potent than CFCs | Portable fire extinguishers
  [83:3:0.2] HCFCs | Yes, weaker | Interim/transitional CFC substitute
  [83:3:0.3] Carbon tetrachloride, methyl chloroform | Yes | Solvents, chemical feedstock
  [83:3:0.4] Methyl bromide | Yes | Agricultural fumigant, quarantine
  [83:3:0.5] Nitrous oxide | Ozone depleter | Laughing gas (N₂O)
  [83:3:0.6] HFCs | No (non-ODS) – but high GWP | ODS substitutes in ACs, refrigeration → Kigali
[83:3:1] - **HFCs**: entirely **man-made**; used in refrigeration, ACs, insulating foams, aerosols, foam-blowing, **fire suppression (clean agents)**; minor use as solvents; **not lubricants**.

## SUB 4 | Uv Radiation Effects

  [83:4:0.1] Crops | Reduced growth/photosynthesis in rice, wheat, corn, soybean; 1% UV-B rise may cut food output ~1%
  [83:4:0.2] Phytoplankton / marine | Damage to plankton and larvae of fish, shrimp, crab; food-chain disruption
  [83:4:0.3] Materials | Degrades wood, plastic, rubber, fabrics
## SUB 5 | Vienna Convention and Montreal Protocol
[83:5:0] # Vienna Convention (1985)
[83:5:1] - **Framework treaty** for the **protection of the ozone layer** – cooperation on research, monitoring, protective measures.
[83:5:2] # Montreal Protocol (1987)
[83:5:3] TABLE head: Particular | Fact
  [83:5:3.0] Signed | 16 September 1987, Montreal, Canada (not Helsinki)
  [83:5:3.1] In force | 1 January 1989
  [83:5:3.2] Nature | Action treaty under Vienna; legally binding
  [83:5:3.3] Aim | Phase out production and consumption of ODS
  [83:5:3.4] Coverage | ~100 chemicals: CFCs, halons, CTC, methyl chloroform, HCFCs, methyl bromide
  [83:5:3.5] Parties | 198 – first and only UN treaty universally ratified
  [83:5:3.6] Secretariat | Ozone Secretariat, Nairobi
[83:5:4] - Results: **~99% of ODS phased out** (2024); avoids **0.5–1°C** warming by 2050.
[83:5:5] - **Multilateral Fund (1991)**: technical and financial aid to developing countries; India argued for it from 1989, secured it in 1990.
[83:5:6] - **MOP37** focused on implementing **Kigali**.
[83:5:7] FIRST: Vienna Convention + Montreal Protocol = first treaties with **universal ratification**.
## SUB 6 | World Ozone Day
[83:6:0] - **16 September** = **International Day for the Preservation of the Ozone Layer**; declared by **UNGA on 19 December 1994**; marks the **signing of the Montreal Protocol (1987)**.
[83:6:1] - **2026 theme: "Global Action for a Cooler Planet"** – marks **10 years of the Kigali Amendment**.
## SUB 7 | Kigali Amendment, 2016
[83:7:0] - Adopted **2016, Kigali, Rwanda**; **amends the Montreal Protocol**; in force **1 January 2019**.
[83:7:1] - Targets **HFCs** – not ODS but **very high GWP**; gradual reduction of their production and consumption.
[83:7:2] - Global target: cut HFC use **80–85% by late 2040s**; avoids up to **0.5°C** warming by 2100.
[83:7:3] TABLE head: Group | Baseline | Freeze | Final cut
  [83:7:3.0] Developed | 2011–13 | Reductions from 2019 | 85% by 2036
  [83:7:3.1] Group 1 (incl. China) | 2020–22 | 2024 | 80% by 2045
  [83:7:3.2] India (Group 2, with Gulf states, Iran, Iraq, Pakistan) | 2024–26 | 2028 | 85% by 2047
[83:7:4] - India **ratified Kigali in 2021**; steps **10% by 2032 → 20% by 2037 → 30% by 2042**.
[83:7:5] - MoEFCC: **no environmental clearance** for new/additional HFC production **after 31 December 2027**.
[83:7:6] Kigali amends **Montreal**, not Kyoto (Kyoto belongs to UNFCCC).
## SUB 8 | India's Ozone and Cooling Action
[83:8:0] # Ozone Cell and ODS Rules
[83:8:1] - **Ozone Cell (MoEFCC)** = India's **National Ozone Unit**.
[83:8:2] - **ODS (Regulation and Control) Rules, 2000** – under the **Environment (Protection) Act**.
[83:8:3] TABLE head: Substance | Phased out by
  [83:8:3.0] Halons (except essential use) | 1 Jan 2001
  [83:8:3.1] CFCs in manufacturing (except MDIs) | 1 Jan 2003
  [83:8:3.2] CTC, methyl chloroform, CFCs in MDIs | 1 Jan 2010
  [83:8:3.3] Methyl bromide | 1 Jan 2015
  [83:8:3.4] HCFCs | Allowed up to 1 Jan 2040
[83:8:4] # HCFC Phase-out Management Plan (HPMP)
[83:8:5] - **Stage-I**: met 2013 freeze + 10% cut by 2015; phased out 341.77 ODP tonnes.
[83:8:6] - **Stage-II**: India **first developing country** to fully phase out **HCFC-141b** in rigid foam; 44% cut vs 35% target by Jan 2020.
[83:8:7] - Over **20,000** RAC technicians trained.
[83:8:8] # India Cooling Action Plan (ICAP)
[83:8:9] - Launched by **MoEFCC, March 2019**; among the world's **first** national cooling plans; **20-year** horizon to **2037–38**.
[83:8:10] - Targets: cut **cooling demand 20–25%**, **refrigerant demand 25–30%**; train **1 lakh** servicing technicians.
[83:8:11] - Thematic areas: **space cooling in buildings, cold chain, servicing sector, domestic production, R&D, transport** – **not coal-based power**.
## SUB 9 | Basel Convention, 1989
[83:9:0] - Adopted **Basel, Switzerland, 1989**; in force 1992; India ratified **June 1992**.
[83:9:1] - Controls **transboundary movement of hazardous wastes and their disposal**; reduces generation; **environmentally sound management**.
[83:9:2] - Problem addressed (APPSC-G2 2025): **physical transfer of hazardous waste across borders** for reprocessing/storage (dumping from rich to poorer countries).
[83:9:3] - India implements Basel's **PIC mechanism** under the **Hazardous and Other Wastes (Management and Transboundary Movement) Rules, 2016**.
[83:9:4] - **Ban Amendment**: bans hazardous-waste exports from **OECD/EU + Liechtenstein** to other countries; in force **5 December 2019**.
[83:9:5] - Recent: plastic waste amendment operative; e-waste provisions strengthened.
[83:9:6] "Biodiversity" is the usual wrong option for Basel.
## SUB 10 | Rotterdam, Stockholm and Minamata
[83:10:0] # Rotterdam Convention
[83:10:1] - **Prior Informed Consent (PIC)** for hazardous **chemicals and pesticides** in international trade – **importing country must agree** before a listed chemical is exported.
[83:10:2] - In force **2004**; India **acceded 2005**; Designated National Authorities: **Ministry of Chemicals & Fertilisers + Ministry of Agriculture**.
[83:10:3] - Annex III: pesticides and industrial chemicals banned or severely restricted by Parties.
[83:10:4] - **BRS**: Basel, Rotterdam, Stockholm share **one joint secretariat** under UNEP; India – MoEFCC's **Hazardous Substances Management Division**.
[83:10:5] # Stockholm Convention (POPs)
[83:10:6] - Adopted **2001**, in force **2004**; India ratified **2006**; **7 more POPs ratified 2020** (chlordecone, hexabromobiphenyl, pentachlorobenzene, HBCD, hexachlorobutadiene etc.).
[83:10:7] - **POPs**: toxic, **long-lasting, bioaccumulate, travel long distances**; **POPRC** assesses candidates.
[83:10:8] - Initial list: **12 POPs, "dirty dozen"** – DDT, PCBs, dioxins, furans, aldrin; now **37** chemicals listed (**34** by 2023 + **3** added in 2025).
[83:10:9] TABLE head: Annex | Meaning | Example
  [83:10:9.0] A | Elimination | Aldrin
  [83:10:9.1] B | Restriction | DDT (malaria control)
  [83:10:9.2] C | Unintentional by-products | Dioxins, furans
[83:10:10] - **2025 Annex A additions**: **chlorpyrifos** (neurotoxic insecticide – not in the dirty dozen), **long-chain PFCAs** (PFAS **"forever chemicals"** in cookware), **medium-chain chlorinated paraffins** (plastics).
[83:10:11] # Minamata Convention (Mercury)
[83:10:12] - Adopted **2013**, in force **2017**; protects from **man-made emissions of mercury** across its lifecycle.
[83:10:13] - Major sources: **coal-fired power plants**, **artisanal and small-scale gold mining** (one of the largest), industrial processes, waste – **not solar PV panels** in normal operation.
[83:10:14] - Phase out **dental amalgam by 2034**.
[83:10:15] - Name: **Minamata, Japan** – factory wastewater mercury poisoned fish-eaters (**Minamata disease**, 1950s; 1953).

=== NOTES 5:84 | Biodiversity – Levels, Values, Measurement; India As A Megadiverse Country
## SUB 0 | Meaning, Definition and Levels
[84:0:0] - Term **"biodiversity"** coined by **Walter G. Rosen (1985)**; **popularised by E.O. Wilson**.
[84:0:1] - **CBD (1992) definition**: variability among living organisms from **all sources** (terrestrial, marine, other aquatic) and the **ecological complexes** they belong to – **within species, between species and of ecosystems**.
[84:0:2] TABLE head: Level | Meaning / example
  [84:0:2.0] Genetic | Variation within a species – basic source; all rice varieties are Oryza sativa but differ in size, aroma, nutrients
  [84:0:2.1] Species | Variety of species in an area (richness + abundance)
  [84:0:2.2] Ecosystem | Variety of ecosystems; one ecosystem cannot replace another (boreal conifers ≠ tropical deciduous)
[84:0:3] - Chinese, Americans, Indians and Black Africans = the **same species, Homo sapiens**.
[84:0:4] - Butterflies, fish and frogs all have **poisonous species**; **Monarch butterfly** is toxic from **cardenolides** of **milkweed** eaten as caterpillar.
## SUB 1 | Measuring Biodiversity
[84:1:0] TABLE head: Measure | Scale
  [84:1:0.0] Alpha (α) | Within one habitat – local; mean species diversity there
  [84:1:0.1] Beta (β) | Between ecosystems – species turnover along a gradient
  [84:1:0.2] Gamma (γ) | Regional total across a landscape
  [84:1:0.3] Species richness | Number of species
  [84:1:0.4] Evenness | Relative abundance; low = few species dominate
[84:1:1] - Terms alpha, beta, gamma by **R.H. Whittaker**.
[84:1:2] - Example: Forest A (2 tigers, 6 deer, 7 rabbits) vs B (1, 6, 8) – same richness; **A more even**.
[84:1:3] - Indices: **Shannon-Wiener** and **Simpson**.
[84:1:4] - **Functional diversity**: range of ecological roles – functional richness, evenness, divergence.
[84:1:5] Alpha = within; Beta = between; Gamma = whole region.
## SUB 2 | Species Estimates and Richness Patterns
[84:2:0] - Described: about **1.5 million** living species (+300,000 fossil). Totals: **Wilson (1992) 10–50 million**; **Robert May ~7 million**.
[84:2:1] - **Over 70%** of recorded species are **animals** (insects richest); **plants ~22%**.
[84:2:2] - **Latitudinal gradient**: richest in **tropics**, falls towards poles; **ants ~10 species near North Pole vs ~2,000 at equator**.
[84:2:3] - Richest: **tropical rainforests** (land), **coral reefs** (sea) – not taiga, desert or temperate forest.
[84:2:4] - Tropics richest: **long undisturbed evolutionary time (no glaciation)**, **stable climate**, **more solar energy → productivity**; moderate disturbance and habitat variety also help.
[84:2:5] - Most butterflies in tropics – **they cannot tolerate low temperature** (A/R: R explains A).
[84:2:6] - **Himalayas** rich: **meeting point of biogeographical zones** (Palearctic + Indo-Malayan); ~25% flora, 30% fauna.
[84:2:7] - **Species-area relationship** (**Humboldt**, log S = log C + Z log A): richness rises with area, up to a limit.
## SUB 3 | Values of Biodiversity
[84:3:0] TABLE head: Value | Meaning
  [84:3:0.0] Consumptive | Used locally, not sold: fuelwood, fodder, wild food
  [84:3:0.1] Productive | Sold commercially: timber, fibre, medicines, genetic material
  [84:3:0.2] Ecosystem services | Clean air, water, pollination, climate
  [84:3:0.3] Social / cultural | Sacred groves, indigenous knowledge
  [84:3:0.4] Aesthetic | Beauty, ecotourism
  [84:3:0.5] Option | Unknown future uses – new drugs, crop genes
[84:3:1] - Most significant aspect: **maintenance of the ecosystem**. Role in SD (APPSC 2025): **supports ecosystem services**.
[84:3:2] - **Rivet popper hypothesis** (**Paul Ehrlich**): ecosystem = aeroplane, species = rivets; losing a key rivet (**keystone species**) may collapse it.
[84:3:3] - **TEEB**: G8+5 initiative, **2007**; "make nature's values visible"; hosted by **UNEP** (not IMF/WEF); led by **Pavan Sukhdev**.
[84:3:4] - **Dasgupta Review (2021)**: **Partha Dasgupta** for **UK Treasury**; nature as an asset; GDP ignores nature's depreciation.
