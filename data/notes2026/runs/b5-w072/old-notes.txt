=== NOTES 5:73 | Environment, Ecology and Ecosystem – Components, Food Chains, Energy Flow and Ecological Pyramids
## SUB 0 | Ecosystem – Concept, Biotic and Abiotic Components, Producers and Decomposers
[73:0:0] # Concept
[73:0:1] - **Ecosystem:** a community of organisms **together with the non-living environment** in which they live, interacting as one functional unit (pond, ocean, forest, even an **aquarium**). Term coined by the British botanist **Sir Arthur G. Tansley in 1935**.
[73:0:2] - **Two components:** **biotic** (producers, consumers, decomposers) and **abiotic** (energy, water, air, soil, nutrients, climate). "Evolution" is a process, not a component.
[73:0:3] - An ecosystem is a **natural, open system**: energy and matter enter and leave; it has its own **productivity**; it is **not a closed system with fixed boundaries** (UPSC/APPSC "NOT correct" item).
[73:0:4] - The **biosphere** (narrow zone where **lithosphere, hydrosphere and atmosphere** meet) is treated in most books as an **open system** – energy enters as sunlight and leaves as heat (UPPSC RO/ARO 2017 key: A true, R false). Strictly, the Earth is open for energy but nearly **closed for matter**.
[73:0:5] - All components are **interdependent**: a change in one population affects the rest – so "components are not interdependent" is false, while "human activities affect the environment" is true.
[73:0:6] - **Functions:** energy flow (one-way), nutrient cycling, production, **self-regulation (homeostasis)**; the **food chain** is the **dynamic part** of an ecosystem (APPSC G1 2008), while producers, consumers and decomposers form its structure.
[73:0:7] - **Biogeochemical (nutrient) cycle:** movement of **chemical elements** (C, N, P) through **biotic and abiotic** components – energy does not cycle; **photosynthesis removes CO₂**; the **phosphorus cycle begins with rock weathering**.
[73:0:8] # Abiotic components
[73:0:9] TABLE head: Type | Factors
  [73:0:9.0] Energy | Sunlight – primary source and driving force
  [73:0:9.1] Climatic | Light, temperature, rainfall, humidity, wind
  [73:0:9.2] Edaphic | Soil – texture, pH, minerals, drainage
  [73:0:9.3] Topographic | Altitude, slope, aspect
  [73:0:9.4] Materials | CO₂, water, nitrates, phosphates; humus and other organic compounds
[73:0:10] - **Abiotic factor:** any **non-living** component that affects organisms or their environment – air, water, light are abiotic; plants, bacteria and animals are biotic.
[73:0:11] - **Liebig's Law of the Minimum:** growth limited by the nutrient in shortest supply. **Shelford's Law of Tolerance:** both too little and too much limit.
[73:0:12] - **Stenothermal** organisms tolerate only a **narrow** temperature range; **eurythermal** a wide range (likewise stenohaline / euryhaline for salinity).
[73:0:13] - **Cold-blooded (poikilothermic)** animals – **frogs**, snakes, lizards, fish, crocodiles – take the temperature of their surroundings.
[73:0:14] # Producers (autotrophs) – trophic level I
[73:0:15] - **Autotrophs** make their own food; their **carbon and energy needs are met by photosynthesis** (NCERT X): they take in **CO₂ and water** (not oxygen) and, with **sunlight and chlorophyll**, convert them into **carbohydrates**, which give the plant energy.
[73:0:16] - **Photosynthesis** turns **light energy into chemical energy**; it needs light – normally daytime sunlight, but it also runs under moonlight or artificial light, so it is not tied to clock time (State PSC key "day or night").
[73:0:17] - On land the major producers are **herbaceous and woody plants**; in water, **phytoplankton** / microscopic **algae** and seaweeds.
[73:0:18] - **Chemoautotrophs:** bacteria of deep-sea vents / active volcanoes oxidise **hydrogen sulphide**; many **archaea** (methanogens) use energy from chemical compounds – they too are producers.
[73:0:19] - Some **bacteria** (green sulphur bacteria) photosynthesise with **invisible infrared** light.
[73:0:20] - **Producers act as intermediaries between the biotic and abiotic components** (official UPPSC key; many books also call **decomposers** the link, because they return nutrients to the abiotic pool).
[73:0:21] - **Chlorella** (a green alga) has the highest efficiency of converting solar energy into chemical energy among the usual options.
[73:0:22] - **Oxygen:** most of Earth's oxygen comes from **algae** / marine phytoplankton – ocean phytoplankton and photosynthetic bacteria give **about 50%** of the world's oxygen, more than rainforests; oxygen-rich surface water holds far **less** oxygen than air (UPSC 2025: only statement II correct).
[73:0:23] - **Cactus** photosynthesises in its green **stem** (leaves reduced to spines).
[73:0:24] # Consumers and decomposers
[73:0:25] TABLE head: Component | Examples
  [73:0:25.0] Primary consumers (herbivores) | Cow, deer, goat, rabbit, caterpillar, zooplankton
  [73:0:25.1] Secondary consumers | Wolves, frog, fox

  [73:0:25.5] Detritivores | Earthworms, millipedes, woodlice, nematodes – not jellyfish or seahorses
[73:0:26] - **Fungi are heterotrophs** (mostly saprotrophs that absorb food from dead matter; some parasitic or symbiotic).
[73:0:27] - **Parasitic nutrition:** obtaining food from another living organism **without killing it**.
[73:0:28] - **Decomposition:** breakdown of dead organic matter by **microorganisms (bacteria, fungi)** into simple inorganic nutrients; biodegradable waste is converted into useful compost by **bacteria**.
[73:0:29] - Steps: **fragmentation → leaching → catabolism → humification → mineralisation**; fully decomposed litter = **humus**. Fast when detritus is rich in nitrogen and sugars and climate warm and moist; slow with lignin, chitin, cold or anaerobic conditions.
[73:0:30] - If **decomposers are removed**, **mineral (nutrient) movement is blocked** – energy flow would continue for a while, but nutrients are not recycled.
[73:0:31] - Macroconsumers = phagotrophs; microconsumers = saprotrophs (osmotrophs). Free-living **Azotobacter** fixes nitrogen.
[73:0:32] - **Protists** are unicellular **eukaryotes**, not prokaryotes; cyanobacteria are photosynthetic prokaryotes.

## SUB 1 | Food Chains, Trophic Levels, Food Webs and Biomagnification
[73:1:0] # Food chain
[73:1:1] - **Food chain:** the order in which organisms **feed upon each other** – producer → herbivore → carnivore; energy passes along it. It runs **across species**, not within one species' population, and it does **not** show the **numbers** eaten (that is a pyramid / food web) – UPSC 2013: only statement 1 correct.
[73:1:2] TABLE head: Chain | Starts with | Example
  [73:1:2.0] Grazing | Living green plants (autotrophs pass energy to herbivores) | Grass → grasshopper → frog → snake (→ eagle); grass → goat → human

  [73:1:2.2] Pond / aquatic | Phytoplankton | → zooplankton → small fish → large fish
  [73:1:2.3] Marine | Diatoms | → crustaceans → herrings (UPSC 2014)

[73:1:4] - In water the grazing chain is the main energy conduit; on land more energy flows through the detritus chain.
[73:1:5] # Trophic levels
[73:1:6] TABLE head: Level | Organisms
  [73:1:6.0] I | Producers – green plants, phytoplankton, seaweeds, chemosynthetic bacteria (vents, active volcanoes), algae
  [73:1:6.1] II | Primary consumers / herbivores – deer, ants, caterpillar, goat, zooplankton

[73:1:7] - The **highest trophic level** is held by **carnivores** (top carnivores), not herbivores or decomposers.
[73:1:8] - **Man** is an omnivore – **primary as well as secondary consumer**; eating goat flesh makes him a **secondary consumer**.
[73:1:9] - **Producers outnumber primary consumers** (energy and numbers fall at each level).
[73:1:10] - A species can occupy more than one level; chains rarely exceed **4–5 levels**.

[73:1:12] # Food web and stability
[73:1:13] - **Food web:** network of interlinked food chains; **more complex → more stable** (alternative feeding paths).
[73:1:14] TABLE head: Term | Meaning
  [73:1:14.0] Resistance | Withstand a disturbance
  [73:1:14.1] Resilience | Recover after a disturbance
  [73:1:14.2] Homeostasis | Self-regulation up to a limit through feedback
[73:1:15] - Diverse systems (rainforest, coral reef) are more stable than tundra, desert or monoculture; overall the **ocean (marine ecosystem) is the most stable ecosystem** – it stays unchanged over long periods.
[73:1:16] - Warming and ocean acidification weaken calcifying plankton and corals, disrupting marine food webs.
[73:1:17] # Bioaccumulation vs biomagnification
[73:1:18] TABLE head: Bioaccumulation | Biomagnification (biological magnification)
  [73:1:18.0] Build-up of a pollutant inside one organism over its life | Rising concentration of toxicants at each higher trophic level – maximum in the topmost level
[73:1:19] - Only **persistent, fat-soluble, non-biodegradable** pollutants (DDT, mercury, PCBs) biomagnify – an increase in **toxicants**, not in algae, insects or weeds.
[73:1:20] - DDT (a **chlorinated hydrocarbon**): food crop → rat → snake → **hawk** – highest in the hawk; grasshopper → toad → **snake** – highest in the snake; causes **eggshell thinning** and seabird crashes.
[73:1:21] - **Methylmercury** – Minamata disease (Japan).
[73:1:22] - Pollutant types: biodegradable = sewage; non-biodegradable = DDT, plastic, asbestos; primary = CO, SO₂, NOx; secondary = O₃, **PAN**, smog.
[73:1:23] - **Trophic level:** the functional position (feeding step) an organism occupies in a food chain.

## SUB 3 | Environment, Ecology and Levels of Organisation
[73:3:0] # Environment
[73:3:1] - **Environment:** the sum total of living and non-living components, influences and events surrounding an organism; **biotic and abiotic factors interact**; it is never static. Composite: **physical** (land, water, air, nutrients), **biological** (organisms) and **cultural** elements – "all of the above" definitions hold.
[73:3:2] - Term "environment" first used in English by **Thomas Carlyle (1828)**.
[73:3:3] TABLE head: Category | Includes
  [73:3:3.0] Physical | Lithosphere, hydrosphere, atmosphere; nutrients, water, air
  [73:3:3.1] Biotic | Plants, animals, microbes
  [73:3:3.2] Cultural | Man-made – social, economic, political institutions
[73:3:4] - Renewable resources come from **both** biotic and abiotic parts (sunlight, wind, water) – "only biotic elements give renewable resources" is false.
[73:3:5] - **Primary functions:** provide resources, **assimilate wastes**, habitat for biodiversity – "generating synthetic chemicals" is NOT a function.
[73:3:6] - Every **habitat is an environment**, but an environment may exist without life.
[73:3:7] # Ecology
[73:3:8] - **Ecology** (Greek *oikos* = home + *logos* = study): study of the **relationship between organisms (plants, animals) and their environment**; term coined by **Ernst Haeckel in 1866** ("Ökologie"); also called **bionomics** – synonymous with ecology, stressing natural systems that influence human systems.
[73:3:9] TABLE head: Branch / term | Meaning
  [73:3:9.0] Autecology | Ecology of a single species in relation to its environment
  [73:3:9.1] Synecology | Ecology of communities
  [73:3:9.2] Deep ecology | Term of the Norwegian philosopher Arne Naess (1973)
  [73:3:9.3] Ecological transition | Concept of John W. Bennett (*The Ecological Transition*, 1976)
  [73:3:9.4] Ecad | An organism (plant) modified by its environment – a habitat form without genetic change
  [73:3:9.5] Variation | Differences among individuals of the same species
  [73:3:9.6] Bionomics | = ecology ("management / laws of life")
[73:3:10] # Levels of organisation
[73:3:11] - Order from smallest to largest: **organism → population → community → ecosystem → (landscape) → biome → biosphere** (UPPSC 2017: population – community – ecosystem – landscape).
[73:3:12] TABLE head: Level | Meaning
  [73:3:12.0] Population | Individuals of one species in an area
  [73:3:12.1] Community | All populations of different species in an area
  [73:3:12.2] Ecosystem | Community + its non-living environment; basic functional unit of ecology
  [73:3:12.3] Biome | Large region defined by climate + dominant vegetation (rainforest, tundra, desert)
  [73:3:12.4] Biosphere | Narrow zone of contact of lithosphere, hydrosphere and atmosphere where life exists; all ecosystems together
[73:3:13] - **Biodiversity** measured as **alpha, beta, gamma**; "biological diversity" coined by **Raymond F. Dasmann**; hotspot concept – **Norman Myers** (≥1,500 endemic vascular plants, ≥70% habitat lost).
[73:3:14] - **Sere** = whole sequence of communities in succession; **habitat fragmentation** = continuous habitat broken into isolated patches; **anthrome** = human-altered terrestrial biome.

## SUB 5 | Energy Flow, Productivity and the Ten Percent Law
[73:5:0] # Energy flow
[73:5:1] - **Unidirectional**: **sunlight → producers → herbivores → carnivores**; energy is not cycled. **Solar energy** is the primary source and **driving force** of an ecosystem.
[73:5:2] - **Law of conservation of energy** (first law of thermodynamics): energy can be neither created nor destroyed but **can be transformed** from one form to another – light → chemical energy in photosynthesis. Second law: each transfer loses energy as **heat**.
[73:5:3] - Under 50% of sunlight is **PAR**; plants fix about **2–10% of PAR**; green plants capture only about **1% of the sunlight** falling on their leaves (UPPSC 2025, SSC 2011).
[73:5:4] - **Total primary production efficiency** = share of incoming **solar energy captured and used in photosynthesis** – about **1–2%**.
[73:5:5] # Ten percent law
[73:5:6] - **Raymond Lindeman (1942)**: only about **10%** of the energy (as food) passes to the next trophic level; about **90%** is lost (respiration, excretion, locomotion, heat) – energy **decreases** at each transfer, hence 4–5 levels.
[73:5:7] - 1% = solar energy captured by plants; 10% = energy passed between trophic levels.
[73:5:8] # Productivity
[73:5:9] TABLE head: Term | Meaning
  [73:5:9.0] GPP (Gross Primary Production) | Total rate of organic matter (solar energy) fixed by producers in photosynthesis
  [73:5:9.1] NPP | GPP − producer respiration; available to consumers
  [73:5:9.2] Primary productivity | Rate at which solar energy is stored in green plants as food (autotroph level)
  [73:5:9.3] Secondary productivity | Rate of formation of new organic matter by consumers (heterotroph level)
[73:5:10] - Primary production in an **aquatic ecosystem** depends on **light penetration, nutrients and temperature** (all of these), with phytoplankton as producers.
[73:5:11] - Highest NPP per unit area: **tropical rainforest**; estuaries, coral reefs, swamps, mangroves also high. Lowest: **deserts and open ocean** (ocean total still large by area).
[73:5:12] - Decreasing productivity: **mangroves > grasslands > lakes > oceans** (UPSC 2013); ecotones (mangroves, estuaries) beat their surroundings.
[73:5:13] > Exam angle: about 17 PYQs – energy-flow sequence (APPSC 2019 ×2), 10% law (×2), 1% capture (×2), GPP / secondary productivity / primary productivity definitions (APPSC 2023, FRO, FSO), UPSC 2013 productivity order.

[73:5:15] - **Upwelling** brings **nutrient-rich** deep water to the surface – high phytoplankton productivity (UPSC 2011).

## SUB 7 | Habitat, Niche and Population
[73:7:0] # Habitat vs niche
[73:7:1] TABLE head: Habitat | Niche
  [73:7:1.0] Physical place where an organism lives – its "address" | Functional role of a species in its habitat – its "profession"

[73:7:2] - **Ecological niche** describes not only the physical space occupied but also the **functional role** in the community (UPSC 2013); it covers the range of conditions tolerated, resources used and role played. Concept enunciated by **Grinnell**.

=== NOTES 5:74 | Biogeochemical Cycles, Ecological Succession and Ecosystem Services
## SUB 0 | Nitrogen Cycle
[74:0:0] # Reservoir and Processes
[74:0:1] - Main reservoir = **atmosphere (N₂ about 78% of air)**, NOT soil (APPSC G2 2025).
[74:0:2] - Plants take nitrogen as **nitrate**; atmospheric N₂ is not directly usable.
[74:0:3] TABLE head: Process | What happens | Agents
  [74:0:3.0] Fixation | N₂ → ammonia/nitrate (enzyme nitrogenase) | Rhizobium, Azotobacter, cyanobacteria (Anabaena, Nostoc), lightning, industrial Haber process
  [74:0:3.1] Ammonification | Organic N in dead matter → ammonia | Decomposer bacteria and fungi
  [74:0:3.2] Nitrification | Ammonia → nitrite → nitrate | Nitrosomonas (to nitrite), Nitrobacter (to nitrate)
  [74:0:3.3] Denitrification | Nitrate → N₂ gas, returned to air | Pseudomonas, Thiobacillus
[74:0:4] - Fixation, ammonification and nitrification **add** usable N to soil; denitrification **removes** it.
[74:0:5] # Legumes and Root Nodules
[74:0:6] - **Rhizobium** – symbiotic, in **legume root nodules** (pulses, pea); **Rhizobium japonicum** – soybean.
[74:0:7] - **Leghaemoglobin**: **pink/red** pigment in nodules (alfalfa, soybean); binds oxygen to keep it low, since **nitrogenase is destroyed by oxygen**.
[74:0:8] - **Boron** is necessary for nodule formation.
[74:0:9] - Pea family (Fabaceae/Leguminosae): groundnut, horse-gram, soybean; N-fixing plants: alfalfa, chickpea, clover.
[74:0:10] # Biofertilisers
[74:0:11] TABLE head: Organism | Nature | Crop / role
  [74:0:11.0] Rhizobium | Symbiotic, legume nodules | Pulses, pea
  [74:0:11.1] Azotobacter, Azospirillum | Free-living N fixers | Cereals, millets, non-leguminous upland crops
  [74:0:11.2] Blue-green algae (cyanobacteria) | Photosynthetic N fixers; heterocysts | Rice / paddy
  [74:0:11.3] Azolla–Anabaena | Water fern Azolla + Anabaena in its leaves | Water-logged rice fields (Azolla pinnata)
  [74:0:11.4] Frankia | Actinomycete, non-legumes | Alnus, Casuarina
  [74:0:11.5] VAM (mycorrhiza) | Fungus–root association | Phosphorus and water uptake
  [74:0:11.6] PSB | Bacillus megaterium, Pseudomonas striata | Solubilise phosphate
[74:0:12] Azotobacter is NOT partnered with Anabaena; symbiotic fixation in legume nodules is by Rhizobium, not cyanobacteria; VAM does not denitrify.
[74:0:13] - **Insectivorous plants** grow in **nitrogen-deficient** (often waterlogged) soils and trap insects for nitrogen.
[74:0:14] - **Uptake forms:** plants take nitrogen as **inorganic nitrates/nitrites** (also ammonium) and some **organic** compounds – **never directly from the air**.
[74:0:15] - **Rain** raises soil **nitrogen**: lightning fixes N₂ into nitrogen oxides that rain carries down as nitrates.
[74:0:16] ~~ Sources: PMF Environment Part 2 p.18-21; NextGen Rocket Biology p.98-104, 129; app notes 5:74 sub 4

## SUB 2 | Carbon Cycle
[74:2:0] TABLE head: Adds CO₂ | Removes CO₂
  [74:2:0.0] Respiration, decomposition, fossil-fuel combustion, volcanic activity, deforestation | Photosynthesis (plants, algae, some bacteria), dissolving in oceans, carbonate sedimentation, silicate weathering
[74:2:1] - Reservoirs and sinks: atmosphere, oceans, forests, soils; long-term – carbonate rocks and fossil fuels. Forests = vital carbon sinks.

## SUB 3 | Biogeochemical Cycles – Basics and Types
[74:3:0] - Nutrients are recycled indefinitely between organisms, air and the Earth's crust; driven by energy flow; **all cycles are linked to the water cycle**.
[74:3:1] - Organisms need at least **27 elements** (15 metals). Macronutrients: potassium, sodium, magnesium, calcium, sulphur, phosphorus, chlorine.
[74:3:2] TABLE head: Type | Cycles | Main reservoir
  [74:3:2.0] Gaseous | Carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, water | Atmosphere / ocean
  [74:3:2.1] Sedimentary | Phosphorus, sulphur, calcium, potassium, iron | Earth's crust – soil and rocks
[74:3:3] Hydrogen (H₂) is NOT a gaseous cycle in exam lists.
[74:3:4] APPSC 2025: Removing decomposers blocks mineral movement – nutrients locked in dead matter never return to the soil.
[74:3:5] # Phosphorus Cycle
[74:3:6] - **No atmospheric phase**; natural reservoir = **rock (phosphates)**; weathering of rocks is the main source; much ends in **ocean sediments**.
[74:3:7] - P is used in membranes, nucleic acids, energy transfer, shells, bones, teeth.
[74:3:8] - **Definition:** movement of nutrient elements through the biotic and abiotic components = **biogeochemical (nutritional) cycle**.
[74:3:9] - **Soil's major nutrients** for cultivation: **nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium (NPK)**.

## SUB 4 | Ecological Succession – Terms and Types
[74:4:0] # Terms
[74:4:1] TABLE head: Term | Meaning
  [74:4:1.0] Succession | Gradual, orderly replacement of one community by another in an area over time
  [74:4:1.1] Pioneer species | First hardy colonisers – lichens, mosses
  [74:4:1.2] Sere / seral stage | Whole sequence = sere; each step = seral stage (seral communities in between)
  [74:4:1.3] Climax community | Final stable community in balance with climate (forest, grassland, coral reef)
  [74:4:1.4] Seasonal succession | Cyclic, driven by environmental rhythms; NOT caused by disturbance
[74:4:2] # Primary vs Secondary
[74:4:3] TABLE head: Primary | Secondary
  [74:4:3.0] Area that never had life – lava, bare rock, volcanic island; no soil | After fire, flood, logging; soil, seeds, roots remain
  [74:4:3.1] Pioneers: lichens and mosses | Faster than primary

  [74:4:7.3] Psammosere | Sand (sub-type of xerosere)
  [74:4:7.4] Halosere | Saline soil or water

## SUB 5 | Succession – Phases, Sequences and Theories
[74:5:0] # Clements' Phases (1916)
[74:5:1] TABLE head: Phase | What happens
  [74:5:1.0] 1. Nudation | Bare area forms (erosion, landslide, volcano, fire)
  [74:5:1.1] 2. Invasion | Seeds/spores arrive; ecesis (successful establishment), aggregation
  [74:5:1.2] 3. Competition and co-action | Unfit species eliminated
  [74:5:1.3] 4. Reaction | Organisms modify soil, water, light, temperature – most important phase
  [74:5:1.4] 5. Stabilisation | Climax community
[74:5:2] Invasion comes right after nudation; ecesis belongs to the invasion phase.
[74:5:3] # Sequences
[74:5:4] - **Xerosere**: crustose lichens → foliose lichens → mosses → herbs and grasses → shrubs → forest. **Lichens** (algae + fungi) start succession on bare rock.
[74:5:5] - Pioneer trees (birch, oak) need high light; shade-tolerant species form the climax.
[74:5:6] - **Hydrosere**: **small phytoplankton** (pioneers in water) → submerged rooted (Chara) → floating (Lemna, Pistia, water lilies) → reed-swamp → sedge-meadow → woodland → forest; ends in wet woodland (**fen carr**).
[74:5:7] - Hydroseres are better studied – **lake sediments keep a permanent record**.
[74:5:8] # Ecologists and Climax Theories
[74:5:9] TABLE head: Person | Contribution
  [74:5:9.0] F.E. Clements | Phases of succession (1916); monoclimax – one climax per region, set by climate
  [74:5:9.1] Tansley | Polyclimax; coined "ecosystem"
  [74:5:9.2] Whittaker | Climax-pattern
  [74:5:9.3] E.P. Odum (1971) | Succession = orderly community change within a unit area
  [74:5:9.4] H.C. Cowles | Succession on sand dunes
  [74:5:9.5] Elton / Lindeman | Food chains, pyramid of numbers / 10% law

## SUB 8 | Water Cycle Regulation – Wetlands and Forests
[74:8:0] - Water carries elements through the ecosystem; cycles would stop if water did not cycle.
