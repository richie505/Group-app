## SUB 0 | Devaluation, Depreciation, Appreciation and Revaluation
[0:0] - **Devaluation:** an **official reduction in the value of a currency vis-a-vis major internationally traded currencies**, made by the government / monetary authority under a **fixed** exchange-rate system (more rupees per dollar) – not a general "decline in values" or a rise in inflation.
[0:1] TABLE head: Term | System | Meaning | Done by
  [0:1.0] Devaluation | Fixed | Official reduction of the currency's value | Government / monetary authority
  [0:1.1] Revaluation | Fixed | Official rise in the currency's value | Government / monetary authority
  [0:1.2] Appreciation | Flexible | Market-driven rise in value – fewer rupees per $ | Demand and supply
  [0:1.3] Depreciation | Flexible | Market-driven fall in value – more rupees per $ | Demand and supply
[0:2] # Reading a rate change
[0:3] - **$1 = ₹60 → ₹58:** the **rupee appreciated** and the **dollar depreciated**.
[0:4] - **$1 = ₹60 → ₹65 (or ₹70) by market forces:** the **rupee depreciated** (not "devalued" – that word is for an official change). ₹96 → ₹94 per $ = rupee appreciated.
[0:5] - **"Rate of exchange falls"** (foreign currency per rupee falls = rupee cheaper), ceteris paribus: **exports rise and imports fall**.
[0:6] > Exam angle: official change → devaluation / revaluation; market change → depreciation / appreciation. Wrong-pair MCQs swap these.
[0:7] ~~ Sources: NextGen Indian Economy Part 1 - APPSC 04 p.19-20; NextGen Rocket Sheets Indian Economy p.69-70
## SUB 1 | Effects of Devaluation
[1:0] - Devaluation makes **exports cheaper abroad** (their foreign-currency price falls) and **imports dearer at home** → it **promotes exports**; it is an **expenditure-switching** policy (spending switches to home-produced goods) used to **correct the balance of trade / BoP**.
[1:1] - **A-R pattern:** "Devaluation may promote exports" (A) is explained by "the price of the country's products in the international market falls" (R) – **A true, R true, R explains A**. A reason saying devaluation **raises** export prices is **false**.
[1:2] - Devaluation does **not necessarily** improve the trade balance or raise the currency's foreign value: it **necessarily** only improves export competitiveness (UPSC 2021: "1 only"). Limits: **imported inflation** (it **raises domestic prices**), inelastic oil imports, costlier ECB repayment.
[1:3] - It **raises the cost of imports** and does **not curb inflation** – so "to fix the balance of trade" is the purpose, not "reduce import costs" or "curb inflation".
[1:4] - **When does it work?** When exports actually **become cheaper to importers** abroad, i.e. export prices fall in proportion to the devaluation and domestic prices do not rise to cancel it (CDS 2017; APPSC 2011 key: "exports change proportionately").
[1:5] - **1991:** the rupee was devalued to **make exports cheaper**; export growth rose after 1991 and the devaluation is credited for it (UPSC 2000 key: A and R true, R explains A).
[1:6] - **Trade imbalance and controls:** import duties, exchange controls and devaluation are used to **wipe out** the trade imbalance and keep the rate stable (APPSC 2011 key).
[1:7] - BoP correction methods (NextGen): monetary policy, **exchange depreciation, devaluation, exchange control**, import duties and quotas, export promotion, foreign loans and investment.
[1:8] ~~ Sources: NextGen Indian Economy Part 1 - APPSC 02 p.15-16; NextGen Rocket Sheets Indian Economy p.69-70; testbook.com CDS 2017 question page
## SUB 2 | Rupee Devaluation Episodes – 1949, 1966, 1991
[2:0] TABLE head: Year | Extent / steps
  [2:0.0] Sept 1949 | First devaluation, with sterling; about 30.5% (₹3.30 → ₹4.76 per US$)
  [2:0.1] June 1966 | ₹4.76 → ₹7.50 per US$ = rupee down 36.5% (dollar's rupee price up 57.5%); wars, drought, 36% inflation; "short-lived liberalisation"
  [2:0.2] July 1991 | Two steps, 1 and 3 July; about 18% (17.38% against sterling, 18.7% against the US$; Ramesh Singh says 22%); BoP crisis; last formal devaluation
[2:1] - **Count:** 1949 (1st), 1966 (2nd), July 1991 two steps (3rd and **4th**) – so the fourth devaluation fell in **1991**.
[2:2] > Trap: the 1991 steps were on **1 and 3 July**, not 1 and 2 July. 1966's "57.5%" is the rise in the dollar's price; the rupee itself lost 36.5%.

## SUB 3 | Exchange Rate and Forex Market Terms
[3:0] - **Exchange rate:** the price / value of one currency in terms of another (₹50 for $1 = ₹50 per dollar). **Forex market** participants: commercial banks, brokers, authorised dealers, monetary authorities.
[3:1] TABLE head: Term | Meaning
  [3:1.0] Direct quote | Units of domestic currency per one unit of foreign currency (₹95 = $1)
  [3:1.1] Indirect quote | Units of foreign currency per one unit of domestic currency
  [3:1.2] Spot rate | Immediate delivery – settled within two days
  [3:1.3] Forward rate | Agreed now for future delivery; an importer's forward contract covers exchange-rate risk
  [3:1.4] Hedging / speculation | Covering exchange risk (forwards) / taking an open position on expected moves
  [3:1.5] Arbitrage | Buying a currency where it is cheaper and simultaneously selling it where it is dearer
  [3:1.6] Hot currency (money) | Foreign currency that migrates quickly between markets for the highest short-term return
  [3:1.7] Tobin tax | Tax on foreign exchange transactions (James Tobin)
  [3:1.8] Currency war | Countries competitively devaluing their currencies to gain export advantage
  [3:1.9] Hawala | Illegal transactions of foreign exchange (informal transfer outside banking channels)
[3:2] - **Money laundering** turns the **proceeds of crime** (drugs, smuggling, corruption) into apparently legal money – hence "drugs and crime"; hawala is a common channel.
[3:3] - **Import cover:** the **number of months of imports that could be paid for by the country's reserves** – about **11 months** with $701.4 bn (16 Jan 2026, Economic Survey 2025-26).
[3:4] - **Most valuable currency (in ₹): Kuwaiti dinar**; of pound, euro and US dollar the costliest is the **pound sterling** (₹125.18 vs euro ₹109.05, $ ₹93.00 on 17 Apr 2026).
[3:5] - **International liquidity problem** = shortage of **dollars and other hard currencies** (internationally accepted reserve assets).
[3:6] - **RBI** (through authorised dealers, under FEMA) sanctions foreign exchange for imports.
[3:7] ~~ Sources: NextGen Indian Economy Part 1 - APPSC 04 p.19; app notes 3:76 sub 0 (Carpediem CA, 17 Apr 2026 rates; ES 2025-26 import cover); general knowledge for hawala / money laundering
## SUB 4 | Exchange Rate Regimes and Determinants
[4:0] TABLE head: Regime | Meaning
  [4:0.0] Fixed / pegged | Government fixes the rate (to gold, a currency or a basket); the central bank buys / sells forex to hold it, so private demand and supply need not come into balance
  [4:0.1] Exchange control | Rate fixed and held by rationing out the available foreign exchange
  [4:0.2] Flexible / floating | Set by demand and supply of foreign currency in the market – not by the central bank
  [4:0.3] Managed float (dirty float) | Market-set, with central-bank intervention to curb volatility – India's market-determined system
[4:1] - **Post-Bretton Woods classes:** **fixed** – currency unions, dollarised regimes, **currency boards (Argentina 1991 – Jan 2002)**, pegs; **intermediate** – bands, crawling pegs; **flexible** – managed and independent floats.
[4:2] - **Floating rates:** traders face the risk of an **exchange-rate loss**; price differences between markets give scope for **arbitrage** (APPSC 2019 key).
[4:3] - **Determinants:** demand for the country's **goods and services**, **stability of its government**, economic potential – the **World Bank does not decide a currency's price** (UPSC 2012 key: II and III).
[4:4] - **Raghuram Rajan:** **high inflation, high fiscal deficit and high crude oil prices** all weaken the rupee's exchange value.
[4:5] - **PPP theory:** equality of purchasing power (the **inflation differential**) decides the long-run **rate of exchange**. Long-run anchors (ES 2025-26): PPP, savings-investment balance, current account.
[4:6] - **Capital flows:** large inflows → **appreciation** (Dutch disease); higher imports can coexist with a stable rupee if backed by **net investment inflows**; a repo hike attracts **carry-trade** capital; deliberate undervaluation (alleged of China) = **currency manipulation**.
[4:7] - **Impossible trinity (Mundell-Fleming trilemma):** no country can have a **fixed exchange rate, free capital flows and an independent monetary policy** at once.
[4:8] ~~ Sources: NextGen Indian Economy Part 1 - APPSC 02 p.16; NextGen Indian Economy Part 1 - APPSC 04 p.20-21; NextGen Rocket Sheets Indian Economy p.70; app notes 3:76 sub 8
## SUB 5 | Bretton Woods, IMF and India's Exchange-Rate History
[5:0] - **Gold standard (1870-1914):** the epitome of the fixed-rate system.
[5:1] - **Bretton Woods Conference** = **United Nations Monetary and Financial Conference**, **1-22 July 1944**, **New Hampshire (USA)**, **44 nations**: set up the **IMF and IBRD** (not the UN or WTO) and fixed rates centred on the **dollar** (two-tier convertibility – the origin of **currency convertibility**).
[5:2] - **IMF:** formally created **27 December 1945**; **India a founding member (27 Dec 1945)**; **191 members** (Liechtenstein 191st, Oct 2024); India's quota **2.75%** of SDRs, votes 2.63% (March 2026). Its purposes are monetary cooperation, trade growth, exchange **stability** and multilateral payments – **not** promoting flexible exchange rates.
[5:3] TABLE head: Year | India's exchange-rate step
  [5:3.0] 1947-1971 | Par value system under the IMF (rupee linked to sterling)
  [5:3.1] 1971 | Smithsonian Agreement – ±2.25% band (NextGen: 2.5%), lasted 14 months
  [5:3.2] Sept 1975 | Rupee delinked from pound sterling, pegged to a basket of currencies – the end of the strict fixed system (Ramesh Singh: "between fixed and floating")
  [5:3.3] 1992-93 | LERMS (Budget 1992-93): dual rate – 40% official, 60% market; partial convertibility
  [5:3.4] 1 March 1993 | Rates unified; market-determined
  [5:3.5] August 1994 | IMF Article VIII – current account convertibility
[5:4] - **Official exchange rate of the rupee:** associated with a **basket of selected currencies** (NextGen; the 1975 basket peg); since 1993 it is market-determined, with the US dollar as the RBI's reference and intervention currency.
[5:5] - **India's current policy:** **market-determined exchange rate** (managed float).
[5:6] ~~ Sources: NextGen Indian Economy Part 1 - APPSC 04 p.20-22, p.32-33; Ramesh Singh Indian Economy 10th ed. (05) p.168-169; imf.org India country page and IMF at a Glance
## SUB 6 | FERA to FEMA and Rupee Convertibility
[6:0] - **FERA 1973** (restrictive, to prevent outflow of currency) → replaced by **FEMA 1999**: proposed in the **1997-98 Budget**; **in force 1 June 2000**, with a **two-year sunset clause** for FERA cases (ended 31 May 2002 – "finally implemented 2002" in the UPPSC key). **FEMA regulates the flow of foreign exchange**; no "Foreign Exchange Control Act" ever replaced FERA.
[6:1] - **Convertibility:** **freely permitting the conversion of the rupee to other currencies and vice versa** – not conversion into gold, nor fixing by market forces.
[6:2] - **Full convertibility** may mean the rupee's **free float**, its **direct exchange anywhere** inside and outside India, and its acting **like any other international currency** (UPSC 2002: 1, 2 and 3).
[6:3] - India: **full current account** convertibility (Aug 1994), only **partial capital account** convertibility.
[6:4] TABLE head: Committee | Facts
  [6:4.0] Tarapore I | 1997 – roadmap to capital account convertibility
  [6:4.1] Tarapore II | Appointed 2006 (report 31 July 2006) – fuller capital account convertibility
[6:5] ~~ Sources: NextGen Indian Economy Part 1 - APPSC 04 p.33; NextGen Rocket Sheets Indian Economy p.37, p.95; app notes 3:76 sub 7
## SUB 7 | SDR, Reserve Tranche and Paper Gold
[7:0] - **SDR (Special Drawing Rights)** – relates to the **IMF**; an **artificial currency / international reserve asset** created in **1969** (agreed 1967) to **supplement members' official reserves**; called **"paper gold"**; exists only as **book-keeping entries**. ADR / GDR are depository receipts, not currency.
[7:1] - **Basket (since 1 Oct 2016):** US dollar, euro, **renminbi** (the currency proposed / added in 2015-16), yen, pound sterling – **no rupee**. Largest allocation **about $650 bn, August 2021**.
[7:2] - **Reserve (gold) tranche:** the part of a member's quota paid in reserve assets (historically gold); a **credit facility of the IMF to its members** drawable **without conditions**; the **smallest** component of India's reserves (FCA > gold > SDR > RTP).
[7:3] - **Reserves:** **RBI is the custodian**; reserves rose to **$785.71 bn on 15 Sep 2026 – India 4th**, overtaking Russia; rising **gold share** = diversification; **India-Japan bilateral swap** renewed from 28 Feb 2026 (two-way, local currencies against US dollars).
[7:4] ~~ Sources: NextGen Indian Economy Part 1 - APPSC 04 p.21; app notes 3:76 sub 10 (Carpediem CA, Sep 2026)
## SUB 8 | NEER and REER
[8:0] - **NEER:** weighted average of the rupee's bilateral **nominal** rates against a **trade-weighted basket**; RBI indices of **40 and 6 currencies**, **base 2015-16 = 100**. **A rise in NEER = rupee appreciation.**
[8:1] - **REER** = NEER **adjusted for relative inflation** differentials – the better competitiveness measure. A **rise in REER = loss of competitiveness** (overvalued rupee), not an improvement.
[8:2] - Higher domestic inflation than abroad **widens the divergence between NEER and REER** (UPSC 2022: statements A and C correct).
[8:3] ~~ Sources: NextGen Rocket Sheets Indian Economy p.69; vajiramandravi.com UPSC 2022 solution page
## SUB 9 | Rupee Movement and RBI Tools
[9:0] - **RBI aim:** curb **excessive volatility**, not defend a fixed level. If the rupee falls fast, the RBI **sells dollars**; if US / EU rates fall and inflows surge, it **buys dollars**; to fight high inflation it **sells** (not buys) government securities (UPSC 2022: B and C).
[9:1] TABLE head: Tool | Example
  [9:1.0] Spot intervention | Selling dollars from reserves
  [9:1.1] Forex swap | Buy/sell swap: banks sell dollars to the RBI and buy them back later at a premium
  [9:1.2] NDF curbs | Restricting domestic banks in offshore Non-Deliverable Forward markets; Net Open Position limits
  [9:1.3] FCNR(B) support | RBI bore the full hedging cost on fresh FCNR(B) deposits till 30 Sep 2026
[9:2] - **NOT RBI tools:** **fiscal policy adjustments** (APPSC 2025); **import curbs and export promotion** are Government structural measures.
[9:3] - **To stop a slide:** curb non-essential imports, promote exports, **masala bonds**, ease **ECB** norms – but **not an expansionary monetary policy** (UPSC 2019).
[9:4] - **Recent:** FY 2025-26 (to Jan 2026) rupee **depreciated about 5.4%**; crossed **₹96.2/$ on 19 May 2026** (Brent near $110, FPI outflows over ₹2 lakh crore). In 2002-07 the rupee **gained against the US dollar** (₹48.4 → ₹44.3).
[9:5] ~~ Sources: superkalam.com UPSC 2022 solution page; app notes 3:76 sub 9 (Carpediem CA); NextGen Indian Economy - APPSC 06 p.2
## SUB 10 | Rupee Internationalisation, Bonds and World Currencies
[10:0] - **INR trade settlement:** RBI framework of **July 2022**, built into the **Foreign Trade Policy 2023** – it **encourages the use of the rupee in cross-border trade**. **Special Rupee Vostro Accounts** = foreign banks' rupee accounts with Indian banks; surplus may go into **corporate bonds and CPs** (2025). **Vostro** = foreign bank's account with a domestic bank; **Nostro** = ours abroad.
[10:1] - **SAARC Currency Swap Framework:** RBI, **2012**; 3-month tenor, rollable twice; 2024-27 **₹250 bn INR window**; first INR drawdown **Maldives, April 2026**.
[10:2] - **SWIFT:** member-owned **cooperative** founded **1973**, headquartered in **Belgium**; a **messaging** network – not a payment network and no power to impose sanctions (APPSC G1 2023: none correct).
[10:3] TABLE head: Bond | Facts

  [10:3.2] Resurgent India Bonds | SBI, 5 Aug 1998, in US dollar, pound sterling and Deutsche Mark
