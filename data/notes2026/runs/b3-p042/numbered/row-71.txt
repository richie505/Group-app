## SUB 1 | Globalisation – Meaning and Impact
[1:0] # Meaning
[1:1] - **Dismantling barriers** in international trade; interdependence through trade, technology, investment, people and information; **minimum possible restrictions** across borders.
[1:2] - **Includes**: lower import duties (incl. on capital goods), abolition of import licensing, free FDI flow, MNC entry.
[1:3] - **Excludes**: **disinvestment of public-sector equity** (privatisation), greater state control, reduced foreign trade.
[1:4] - **Free trade** = reduction or removal of trade barriers (tariffs, quotas).
[1:5] - Key element of India's market strategy since 1991 = **integration with the global economy**. A stable macroeconomy helps by withstanding shocks.
[1:6] TABLE head: Liberalisation | Globalisation
  [1:6.0] Removes domestic controls | Integrates with the world economy
  [1:6.1] De-licensing, deregulation, import liberalisation | Trade, foreign investment, MNC participation
  [1:6.2] Greater market orientation | Greater global integration; technology flows
[1:7] # Impact
[1:8] - Exports and imports both grew fast - but **imports grew faster than exports** (persistent trade deficit) (APPSC 2025).
[1:9] - India's world trade share 1.78% (1950) → 0.6% (1995); later rose.
[1:10] - **Largest FDI sources 1991-95: USA and Japan**. Export growth 1994-95: about **18%**.
[1:11] - **Services** (IT, outsourcing) benefited most; IT exports cushioned the CAD; 1,800+ GCCs earned $38.25 bn of services exports in July 2026.
[1:12] - Indian MNCs: Tata, Reliance, Infosys, Sun Pharma.
[1:13] - Net FDI: $44.0 bn (2020-21) → under $1 bn (2024-25) → $7.6 bn (2025-26); gross $94.6 bn.
[1:14] - **ISO 9001/9002** for many industries (BIS) and licensing liberalised on 24 July 1991 - both true, but R does not explain A.
[1:15] - **Major impact (APPSC 2010):** poverty fell but **economic disparities rose**.
[1:16] - **Trade/GDP:** merchandise trade 14.2% of GDP (1990-91) → ~43% (2011-12) – about **three times**.
[1:17] - **FII inflows began only after 1992** (FIIs allowed Sept 1992) – not in the 1980s.
[1:18] > A-R (UPSC 1996): lower import duty on capital goods is a liberalisation tool and helps firms upgrade technology – R explains A.
[1:19] ~~ Sources: NextGen Indian Economy APPSC 14 p.3-7; app notes 3:71 sub 9; general knowledge (trade/GDP), not checked

## SUB 5 | Causes and Indicators of the 1991 Crisis
[5:0] # 1980s Groundwork (Indira and Rajiv Gandhi, 1984-89)
[5:1] - **Broad-banding of licences**; easier **capital-goods imports**; higher MRTP limit; **FERA relaxed**.
[5:2] - Growth broke the **Hindu rate**, touched **5.5%** - but it was **debt-fuelled**.
[5:3] - Some liberalisation began under **Rajiv Gandhi**, but the 1991 reforms were **far more comprehensive**.
[5:4] # Long-term Causes
[5:5] TABLE head: Cause | Effect / data
  [5:5.0] Fiscal profligacy | Gross fiscal deficit 5.4% (1981-82) → 8.0% (1989-90); average 6.3% (Sixth Plan) → 8.2% (Seventh)
  [5:5.1] Licence-Permit-Quota Raj | Heavy controls; MRTP Act 1970
  [5:5.2] PSU losses | Low returns on public investment
  [5:5.3] Weak exports | World export share 2.2% (1948) → 0.5% (1983)
  [5:5.4] Structural | CADs financed by foreign borrowing since the 1980s, incl. short-term ECBs
[5:6] - The **long-term** cause = constant **fiscal profligacy** and rising fiscal deficit; the others below were **immediate triggers**.
[5:7] # Immediate Triggers
[5:8] TABLE head: Trigger | Effect
  [5:8.0] Gulf War (Aug 1990) | Oil prices doubled; Gulf remittances dried up (did NOT rise)
  [5:8.1] Political instability | Two changes of government Dec 1989 - June 1991; fall of Chandra Shekhar govt
  [5:8.2] Capital flight | NRIs withdrew deposits; foreign banks stopped lending
[5:9] - Road to crisis: 1980s fiscal profligacy → CAD on foreign borrowing → Gulf War shock → BoP crisis 1991.
[5:10] TABLE head: Indicator (1990-91 / 1991) | Value
  [5:10.0] Forex reserves (June 1991) | ~$975 million - about two weeks of imports
  [5:10.1] Fiscal deficit | 9.4% of GDP (Centre + States); Centre's GFD 8.4% (6.1% in 1980-81)
  [5:10.2] Current account deficit | 3.1-3.2% of GDP
  [5:10.3] Trade deficit | ₹10,644 crore (3.2% of GDP)
  [5:10.4] Internal debt of govt | 35% (1980-81) → 53% of GDP (1990-91)
  [5:10.5] GDP growth | 1.1% (1990-91); below 1% in 1991-92
[5:11] - Crisis = **high inflation, very low forex reserves, large fiscal deficit** (APPSC 2025).
[5:12] - NEP 1991 was a **response to a severe BoP crisis**.
[5:13] - **Immediate cause of forex loss:** **sharp rise in the oil and petroleum import bill** after the Gulf War (APPSC G1).
[5:14] - The crisis = a **Balance of Payments crisis**; response = **Structural Adjustment Programme** with IMF-World Bank loans (~$7 bn).

## SUB 7 | Stabilisation vs Structural Adjustment
[7:0] TABLE head: Basis | Stabilisation | Structural adjustment
  [7:0.0] Term | Short-term | Long-term (medium/long-term efficiency and growth)
  [7:0.1] Nature | Demand-side (demand management) | Supply-side
  [7:0.2] Targets | Inflation, fiscal deficit, BoP | Industrial, trade, financial, investment structures
  [7:0.3] Fiscal tools | Cut fiscal deficit: less wasteful spending, subsidy cuts, better tax collection | Tax-sector reform
  [7:0.4] Monetary tools | Tight monetary policy by RBI to control inflation | Financial-sector reform
  [7:0.5] Trade / exchange | Rupee devaluation to correct BoP | Tariff cuts, QR removal, export promotion
  [7:0.6] Industrial | - | Delicensing, MRTP relaxation, FDI opening, disinvestment
  [7:0.7] Lead institution | IMF | World Bank (IBRD)
[7:1] - Sequencing: stabilisation first, structural adjustment alongside and after.
[7:2] - **Structural measures**: delicensing, tariff reduction, public-sector disinvestment.
[7:3] # IMF-World Bank SAPs
[7:4] - **Structural Adjustment Programmes** = IMF and World Bank loan packages to **reduce fiscal imbalances and adjust the economy for long-term growth** (APPSC 2018).
[7:5] - Conditions: austerity, privatisation, deregulation, **currency devaluation**, better tax collection.
[7:6] - **Eighth Plan (1992-97)** began just after stabilisation and structural adjustment.
[7:7] - **Washington Consensus** (ten rules): fiscal discipline, tax reform, trade and FDI liberalisation, privatisation, deregulation, property rights - NOT expansion of import quotas. Now seen as obsolete; blamed for the 1997 and 2008 crises (14 Mar 2026).

## SUB 8 | New Industrial Policy 1991, PSU Reform and National Renewal Fund
[8:0] # Industrial Policy (24 July 1991)
[8:1] - **Industrial licensing abolished** for all but a few industries (hazardous and strategic, e.g. hazardous chemicals, aerospace); now only **5-6** industries need a licence; reserved for public sector now only **atomic energy and railways** (+ specified minerals).
[8:2] - Delicensed firms: **pricing freedom**, no expansion curbs.
[8:3] - **Public-sector reserved industries cut from 17 (1956 policy) to 8**; later pruned further.
[8:4] - **MRTP**: **asset limits, pre-entry restrictions and merger/expansion controls abolished**. (SEBI's status was a separate reform.)
[8:5] - **FDI**: automatic approval up to **51%** in high-priority industries (now 100% in most sectors); NRIs/OCBs up to 100%; FIIs allowed into stock markets.
[8:6] - **BIFR**: set up to revive **sick industrial units**.
[8:7] # National Renewal Fund (February 1992)
[8:8] - A **safety net** for workers hit by industrial restructuring under the new policy.
[8:9] - Objectives: **retraining and redeployment** of employees affected by modernisation and restructuring; **compensation** of affected employees; counselling.
[8:10] - In practice mostly paid for **Voluntary Retirement Schemes (VRS)** in central PSUs; later limited to public sector units only.
[8:11] ~~ Sources: NextGen Indian Economy APPSC 13 p.1-3, p.6-11; NextGen Indian Economy APPSC 01 p.23 (safety net); app notes 3:71 sub 6
