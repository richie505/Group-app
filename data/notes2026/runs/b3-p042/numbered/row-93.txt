## SUB 0 | Measuring Inflation – CPI, WPI and the Headline Index
[0:0] # Who compiles what
[0:1] TABLE head: Index | Compiled by | Base year (as of 2026) | Main use
  [0:1.0] CPI (Rural / Urban / Combined) | NSO, MoSPI (earlier CSO) | 2024 (replaced 2012; first release 12 Feb 2026 for Jan 2026) | Headline inflation; RBI's inflation target
  [0:1.1] CPI-IW (Industrial Workers) | Labour Bureau, Ministry of Labour and Employment | 2016 (from Sep 2020) | Dearness allowance of industrial workers and Central Government employees
  [0:1.2] CPI-AL / CPI-RL (Agricultural / Rural Labourers) | Labour Bureau | 2019 (from June 2025; earlier 1986-87) | Farm and rural labourers' wages
  [0:1.3] CPI-UNME (Urban Non-Manual Employees) | CSO (not the Labour Bureau) | 1984-85 | Discontinued from January 2011 after CPI-Urban began
  [0:1.4] WPI | Office of the Economic Adviser, DPIIT, Ministry of Commerce and Industry | 2022-23 (revised 2026; earlier 2011-12, 2004-05) | Wholesale prices; contract escalation
  [0:1.5] CFPI (Consumer Food Price Index) | NSO | Same as CPI | Food inflation
  [0:1.6] GDP deflator | NSO | National-accounts base | Widest measure – all goods and services produced
[0:2] - **CPI-IW base**: **1982 → 2001 → 2016** (w.e.f. September 2020).
[0:3] - **Labour Bureau**: set up **1920**, at **Chandigarh**, under the **Ministry of Labour and Employment**.
[0:4] - **Odd one out** among CPI-IW, CPI-AL, CPI-RL, CPI-UNME: **CPI-UNME** – the only one compiled by the **CSO** (UPSC 2010).
[0:5] - **Dearness allowance (DA)** is fixed on the **Consumer Price Index – CPI-IW** (not national income or per capita income); linking pay to a price index is **indexation** ("fixing of wage compensation").
[0:6] - **IIP** measures **industrial output, not prices** (NSO).
[0:7] # Headline measure – from WPI to CPI
[0:8] - WPI was India's **headline / "primary" / most common** inflation measure **till April 2014**.
[0:9] - **April 2014**: RBI adopted **CPI (Combined)** as its key measure (Urjit Patel Committee); **headline inflation is now based on CPI-C**.
[0:10] - India still publishes inflation on CPI, WPI and the labour CPIs, but the **policy (headline) measure is CPI-Combined**.
[0:11] - Inflation rate = **year-on-year % change in CPI** (current month's CPI over the CPI of the same month last year).
[0:12] # WPI
[0:13] - Prices of goods traded in bulk (first point); **no services** – CPI covers goods **and services**.
[0:14] - **2011-12 series**: **697 items**; **manufactured products = largest weight, 64.23%** (primary articles 22.62%, fuel and power 13.15%; 2004-05 series: manufactured 64.97%).
[0:15] - **New WPI (base 2022-23, 2026)**: **957 items**; weights on **Gross Value of Output**; runs with a new **Producer Price Index (PPI)** for **5 years**, then WPI is phased out.
[0:16] - **PPI** measures the **average change in prices received by producers** (output PPI: goods leaving production; input PPI: inputs entering it).
[0:17] - WPI was released **weekly** (primary articles, fuel) until 2012, so "WPI only monthly" was wrong in 2010; WPI gives **less weight to food** than CPI-IW.
[0:18] - WPI series on base **2004-05** was introduced on **14 September 2010**.
[0:19] # CPI structure
[0:20] - New CPI (base 2024): **12 divisions** (COICOP 2018), **358 items**; **food and beverages 36.75%** (about 46% in the 2012 series); 12 online platforms and rural house rent added.
[0:21] - **Food has a bigger weight in CPI than in WPI** (UPSC 2020).
[0:22] # Committees on price indices
[0:23] TABLE head: Committee | Subject
  [0:23.0] Abhijit Sen (2007; report May 2008) | WPI revision – base 2004-05
  [0:23.1] Saumitra Chaudhuri (2012) | WPI revision – base 2011-12
  [0:23.2] Ramesh Chand (NITI Aayog, 2025) | WPI base 2022-23
  [0:23.3] Urjit Patel (2014) | CPI as nominal anchor; flexible inflation targeting
[0:24] # GDP deflator
[0:25] - **GDP deflator = (Nominal GDP / Real GDP) × 100**; the **broadest** measure (all goods and services produced).
[0:26] - A **rise in the GDP/GNP deflator** = rising prices → **fall in the purchasing power of money**.
[0:27] > Exam angle: "headline / most common measure" now = **CPI-Combined**; older keys (pre-2014) = WPI. DA = CPI-IW (Labour Bureau). WPI = Commerce Ministry, manufactured goods ~64%.

## SUB 1 | Causes and Types of Inflation by Cause
[1:0] # By cause
[1:1] TABLE head: Type | Cause
  [1:1.0] Demand-pull | Aggregate demand > aggregate supply: expansionary fiscal and monetary policy, fiscal stimulus, higher purchasing power, rising money supply – "too much money chasing too few goods"
  [1:1.1] Cost-push | Rise in prices of factors of production (wages, raw materials, fuel) → supply falls while demand is constant
  [1:1.2] Structural / bottleneck | Deficiencies in the economy – poor farm productivity, supply bottlenecks, inefficient distribution and storage; persists long and is hard for government to tackle
  [1:1.3] Export-led | Strong export demand drains domestic supply
[1:2] - **Inflationary gap**: excess of aggregate demand (spending) over aggregate supply at **full-employment** output.
[1:3] - Demand-pull is **not** caused by **inflation-indexing of wages** (cost side) or **rising interest rates** (they cut demand) – UPSC 2021: 1, 2 and 4 only.
[1:4] - A **permanent fall in input costs** reduces cost-push pressure.
[1:5] # Causes of price rise in India
[1:6] TABLE head: Side | Factors
  [1:6.0] Demand | Increase in money supply, rise in non-developmental public expenditure, deficit financing / deficit budget, loose monetary policy, rapid GDP growth, population growth
  [1:6.1] Supply | Erratic / less agricultural production, hoarding, higher MSPs, rise in crude oil and fertiliser prices (oil shocks), inefficient storage and marketing, rupee depreciation, cartelisation
[1:7] - General price rise may come from **more money supply**, **lower aggregate output** and **higher effective demand** (UPSC 2013: all three).
[1:8] - **Food inflation** (UPSC 2011): changing **consumption pattern with rising incomes** and **structural constraints in the food supply chain** – not a 30% fall in foodgrain area.
[1:9] - **Unstable currency** works **against** inflation management; stability, sound monetary policy and balanced supply-demand help.
[1:10] # Crude oil benchmarks
[1:11] - **Brent** (North Sea), **West Texas Intermediate (WTI)** (US) and **Dubai** – grades of **crude oil** used as price benchmarks; oil-price shocks feed cost-push inflation.
[1:12] ~~ Sources: NextGen Indian Economy - APPSC 10 p.15, p.17-18; NextGen Indian Economy Part 1 - APPSC 01 p.6; Ramesh Singh Indian Economy 10th ed. Part 4 p.117 (bottleneck, inflationary gap); Rocket Indian Economy p.76; old app notes 3:93 sub 2-3
## SUB 2 | Meaning of Inflation and Its Kinds by Speed
[2:0] # Definition
[2:1] - **Inflation**: a sustained **rise in the general price level** and a **fall in the purchasing power (value) of money**; the essential attribute is **increase in prices**.
[2:2] - During inflation prices **increase**; when all prices rise, the purchasing power of money **decreases**.
[2:3] - A **rise in a commodity's price** = **fall in the value of currency and rise in the value of the commodity** (in money terms).
[2:4] - Effects together: prices rise **and** the value of money falls (not "exchange rate improves").
[2:5] - **Economic growth** is usually coupled with **(moderate) inflation** – rising income and demand push prices up.
[2:6] # Related terms
[2:7] TABLE head: Term | Meaning
  [2:7.0] Deflation | Persistent fall in the general price level (negative inflation) – not a currency fall or a fall in the inflation rate
  [2:7.1] Disinflation | Fall in the rate of inflation, still positive (e.g. 3% → 2%)
  [2:7.2] Reflation | Inflation returning after deflation – sign of recovery
  [2:7.3] Stagflation | High inflation + slow growth / stagnant output + high unemployment
  [2:7.4] Skewflation | Inflation in some items (e.g. food) while others are stable or falling
[2:8] - A **fall in the inflation rate** means only that **prices are rising more slowly** – not that prices fell, food supply rose or industry stagnated.
[2:9] # Stagflation factors
[2:10] - Inflationary pressure (e.g. from **higher government spending**) **plus** **declining industrial production and sluggish growth** → stagflation.
[2:11] - A **fall in aggregate demand** and **currency appreciation** pull prices **down** – they slow growth but do not create the inflation half of stagflation.
[2:12] # By speed
[2:13] TABLE head: Type | Rate
  [2:13.0] Creeping | Gradual, below about 3%
  [2:13.1] Walking / trotting | 3-10%
  [2:13.2] Galloping | Double or triple digit (20%, 100%, 200% a year)
  [2:13.3] Hyperinflation | Large and accelerating; prices shoot up overnight

## SUB 3 | Controlling Inflation
[3:0] # Tools
[3:1] TABLE head: Type | Measures
  [3:1.0] Monetary (RBI) | Decrease money circulation: raise repo, reverse repo, Bank Rate, CRR, SLR; sell government securities (OMO) – reducing interest rates is not an anti-inflation step
  [3:1.1] Fiscal (Government) | Cut non-developmental spending, avoid deficit financing, raise direct taxes, cut indirect taxes on essentials
  [3:1.2] Supply / administrative | Control demand, rationing, prevent hoarding and black marketing, ban exports of scarce goods, suspend futures trading, buffer stocks
[3:2] - Control needs a **combination of monetary, fiscal and exchange-rate policies** – monetary policy alone cannot fix supply-side inflation; controlling inflation is **not** the Government's job only – the **RBI** has the key role.
[3:3] - **Dual price policy** (to control prices): **sugar** – a share of output (**levy sugar**) bought at a controlled price for the PDS, the rest sold in the open market (levy abolished 2013).
[3:4] - **Price Stabilisation Fund (PSF)**: Central Sector Scheme, **2014-15 (launched March 2015)**; began with **onion and potato**; pulses added later.
[3:5] - **Bharat Atta** (subsidised wheat flour) helped contain **wheat inflation**; **Bharat Dal** (chana dal, July 2023) was extended to **moong dal**; sold via NAFED, NCCF, Kendriya Bhandar (APPSC G1 2024: II and IV).
[3:6] - OMSS (Domestic): FCI sells **wheat and rice**.
[3:7] - **RBI's biggest concern** in its policy statements: **inflation**.
[3:8] - Repo rate (as of 7 Oct 2026): **5.50%** (was 5.25% in Aug 2026); repo acts on demand, **cannot directly fix supply shocks** (onion, crude oil).
[3:9] # Inflation-Indexed Bonds (IIBs)
[3:10] - **Principal and/or interest linked to an inflation index** → protect investors from **uncertainty about inflation** (real return).
[3:11] - Government can **reduce coupon rates** on its borrowing (no inflation-risk premium); interest and capital gains are **taxable** (UPSC 2022: A and B only).
[3:12] - RBI issued IIBs in **2013**.
[3:13] # AP measures
[3:14] - **State-level Price Monitoring Committee** headed by the **Chief Secretary**; **DES monitors 8 essential commodities daily** (groundnut and palm oil added Feb 2025).

## SUB 4 | Effects of Inflation and Deflation
[4:0] # Gainers and losers of inflation
[4:1] TABLE head: Gain | Lose
  [4:1.0] Debtors (borrowers) – repay money of lower real value | Creditors (lenders) – hurt most
  [4:1.1] Businessmen, producers, big farmers (profits rise faster than costs) | Fixed-income groups – pensioners, salary and wage earners
  [4:1.2] Holders of real assets, equity investors | Bond / debenture holders, savings-account holders
  [4:1.3] Exporters (if the rupee depreciates) | Consumers, the poor
[4:2] - Rule: **inflation benefits debtors**; it does **not** benefit bond-holders, consumers or fixed-income groups.
[4:3] - Price-hike: businessmen gain and consumers must curtail needs (most keys: both true, R not the explanation; strictly not "all" businessmen gain).
[4:4] - Other effects: falling saving (MPS falls), shift to gold, rising inequality, wage-price spiral, discourages exports; **food inflation is regressive** (the poor spend more of income on food); **menu costs** of re-pricing.
[4:5] # Deflation
[4:6] - Purchasing power of money **rises** → **fixed-income groups (pensioners, salary earners)** and **creditors gain**; **debtors lose** (heavier real debt).
[4:7] - **Deflationary spiral**: postponed purchases → lower profits → higher unemployment → heavier real debt burden.
[4:8] ~~ Sources: NextGen Indian Economy - APPSC 10 p.12, p.18-20; Ramesh Singh Indian Economy 10th ed. Part 4 p.122; old app notes 3:93 sub 4
## SUB 5 | Headline, Core and Food Inflation
[5:0] TABLE head: Basis | Headline inflation | Core inflation
  [5:0.0] Covers | Total inflation, including food and energy (oil, gas) | Excludes volatile food and fuel / energy
  [5:0.1] Shows | Raw figure – what consumers feel | Underlying, long-run trend
  [5:0.2] Volatility | More volatile | More stable
  [5:0.3] Index | Change in CPI-Combined | CPI excluding food and fuel
[5:1] - **Headline** = rate of change of the CPI (basket of a typical family); **core** = CPI change after excluding volatile items such as food and fuel – both statements true (RAS 2023).
[5:2] - Core does **not** include food and energy; headline does **not** exclude food (RAS 2024: only "headline is more volatile" is correct).
[5:3] - Inflation that leaves out commodities with volatile prices = **core inflation**.
[5:4] - **Food inflation**: measured by the **CFPI** (10 sub-groups; cereals carry the highest weight).
[5:5] ~~ Sources: NextGen Indian Economy - APPSC 10 p.16, p.22; NextGen Indian Economy Part 1 - APPSC 01 p.7-8; Rocket Indian Economy p.75; old app notes 3:93 sub 1
## SUB 6 | Index Numbers and the Business Cycle
[6:0] - A **price index** is a **statistical device** to measure changes in the general price level.
[6:1] - **Laspeyres** index: **base-year (initial-year) quantities** as weights; **Paasche**: current-year weights; **Fisher's ideal index** = geometric mean of the two.
[6:2] - **Irving Fisher** is the economist most associated with **index numbers** (ideal index, tests of index numbers).
[6:3] - **Business cycle** phases: **expansion / recovery** (real GDP rising) → **peak / boom** → **recession** (falling) → **trough / depression** (bottom).
[6:4] ~~ Sources: Ramesh Singh Indian Economy 10th ed. Part 4 p.136-137 (boom, recession, recovery); index-number facts general knowledge, not checked

## SUB 8 | Inflation Targeting and the Phillips Curve
[8:0] TABLE head: Step | Year | Provision
  [8:0.0] Urjit Patel Committee | 2014 | CPI as nominal anchor
  [8:0.1] Monetary Policy Framework Agreement | Feb 2015 | Led to RBI Act amendment
  [8:0.2] RBI Act 1934 amended | 2016 | Statutory flexible inflation targeting; Sec 45ZA target set by Centre with RBI every 5 years; Sec 45ZB six-member MPC; Sec 45ZN report after 3 consecutive quarters of breach (first invoked 2022)
[8:1] - **Target (1 Apr 2026 – 31 Mar 2031)**: **4% CPI-Combined ± 2% (2-6%)**; 6% is only the upper limit.
[8:2] - Average CPI fell from 6.8% (2012-16) to about 4.9% under the framework.
[8:3] - **Phillips curve** (A.W. Phillips, 1958): **inverse relation between unemployment and (wage) inflation**; Friedman: long-run curve **vertical**; India's curve is flat (about 92% informal workers).
[8:4] ~~ Sources: Ramesh Singh Indian Economy 10th ed. Part 4 p.120; old app notes 3:93 sub 7-8
