=== Globalisation – Meaning and Impact
# Meaning
- **Dismantling barriers** in international trade; interdependence through trade, technology, investment, people and information; **minimum possible restrictions** across borders.
- **Includes**: lower import duties (incl. on capital goods), abolition of import licensing, free FDI flow, MNC entry.
- **Excludes**: **disinvestment of public-sector equity** (privatisation), greater state control, reduced foreign trade.
- **Free trade** = reduction or removal of trade barriers (tariffs, quotas).
- Key element of India's market strategy since 1991 = **integration with the global economy**. A stable macroeconomy helps by withstanding shocks.
| Liberalisation | Globalisation |
|---|---|
| Removes domestic controls | Integrates with the world economy |
| De-licensing, deregulation, import liberalisation | Trade, foreign investment, MNC participation |
| Greater market orientation | Greater global integration; technology flows |
# Impact
- Exports and imports both grew fast - but **imports grew faster than exports** (persistent trade deficit) (APPSC 2025).
- India's world trade share 1.78% (1950) → 0.6% (1995); later rose.
- **Largest FDI sources 1991-95: USA and Japan**. Export growth 1994-95: about **18%**.
- **Services** (IT, outsourcing) benefited most; IT exports cushioned the CAD; 1,800+ GCCs earned $38.25 bn of services exports in July 2026.
- Indian MNCs: Tata, Reliance, Infosys, Sun Pharma.
- Net FDI: $44.0 bn (2020-21) → under $1 bn (2024-25) → $7.6 bn (2025-26); gross $94.6 bn.
- **ISO 9001/9002** for many industries (BIS) and licensing liberalised on 24 July 1991 - both true, but R does not explain A.
- **Major impact (APPSC 2010):** poverty fell but **economic disparities rose**.
- **Trade/GDP:** merchandise trade 14.2% of GDP (1990-91) → ~43% (2011-12) – about **three times**.
- **FII inflows began only after 1992** (FIIs allowed Sept 1992) – not in the 1980s.
> A-R (UPSC 1996): lower import duty on capital goods is a liberalisation tool and helps firms upgrade technology – R explains A.

=== LPG – Meaning, Measures and What Was Not Part
| Pillar | Meaning - action it promotes | Key measures |
|---|---|---|
| Liberalisation | Removes domestic controls - de-licensing, deregulation, import liberalisation, private-sector expansion | Licensing abolished; MRTP limits scrapped; interest rates deregulated, private banks allowed; tax rates cut, base widened; SEBI statutory (1992); RBI from regulator to facilitator |
| Privatisation | Bigger private role - disinvestment, sale of PSUs, opening sectors | Dereservation; PSU equity sale; private buyers above 51% take ownership and management; MoUs; Navratna/Maharatna autonomy |
| Globalisation | Integration with the world economy - MNC entry, import duty cuts, free flow of capital/technology | Import substitution → export promotion; QRs and EXIM scrips abolished; market rate 1993; FERA → FEMA 1999 |
- **Import liberalisation** = cutting tariffs and QRs.
- Liberalisation + privatisation = strategies; **globalisation = their outcome**; **outsourcing** is its major feature.
- LPG reforms opened the door to **multinational corporations**.
- **Navratnas (1997): 9 PSUs** vs **Miniratnas: 97**. **New PSE Policy (2021)**: strategic disinvestment for Atmanirbhar Bharat.
# Part of NEP 1991 vs NOT Part (full list)
| Part of 1991 NEP | NOT part of 1991 NEP |
|---|---|
| Delicensing | Nationalisation (APPSC 2018 - odd one out) |
| Disinvestment of PSU equity | Full capital account convertibility |
| FDI liberalisation - automatic route | Demonetisation |
| Devaluation, tariff cuts, removal of import quotas | FDI in retail and defence - opened much later |
| Opening to foreign and private investment (APPSC 2018) | Strengthening Licence Raj or MRTP; import substitution; reserving more items for SSI |
- 100% FDI in insurance now in place (May 2026).
- **First step of liberalisation:** substantial changes in **industrial licensing** (UPSC 2000).
- **Private sector = engine of growth** under LPG (key role); **disinvestment** = reducing Government's share in PSUs.
- **Primary goals:** boost growth, improve governance, widen people's participation; **not** employment for the poorest, more tariffs or nationalisation.
- **Golden Handshake (VRS)** belongs to the first reform phase (1991-2001); Antyodaya, Food for Work, IRDP are 1970s schemes.

=== Crisis Response 1991 – Devaluation, Gold and the IMF
| Measure | Status |
|---|---|
| Devaluation - two steps, ~20%, on 1 and 3 July 1991 | Taken - to make exports cheaper and correct BoP (APPSC 2024) |
| Gold pledge - about 67 tonnes in all | Taken - **46.91 t** pledged by RBI with the **Bank of England and Bank of Japan** (July 1991, ~$405 mn, airlifted); **20 t** sold with repurchase option via **SBI to UBS** (May 1991) |
| IMF loan with IMF/World Bank conditionalities | Taken; earlier IMF borrowings July 1990 and Jan 1991 |
| Market-determined exchange rate | Later - 1993 |
| Current account convertibility | Later - August 1994 (IMF Article VIII) |
| Demonetisation | NOT part of 1991 (India's demonetisations: 1978 and 2016) |
| Full capital account convertibility | NOT adopted - capital account still only partly convertible |
# Reform Sequence
- 1. Devaluation (July 1991)
- 2. Industrial delicensing
- 3. MRTP asset limits abolished
- 4. Removal of EXIM scrips and import quotas
- 5. Market-determined exchange rate (1993)
- **IMF Stand-By Arrangement:** **SDR 1,656 million** for **20 months**, approved by the IMF Board on **31 October 1991**; it supported the 1991-92 and 1992-93 programme.
> 'Emergency IMF loan by pledging gold' is the usual key for how the crisis was tided over; strictly the gold went to the BoE/BoJ, not the IMF.

=== Causes and Indicators of the 1991 Crisis
# 1980s Groundwork (Indira and Rajiv Gandhi, 1984-89)
- **Broad-banding of licences**; easier **capital-goods imports**; higher MRTP limit; **FERA relaxed**.
- Growth broke the **Hindu rate**, touched **5.5%** - but it was **debt-fuelled**.
- Some liberalisation began under **Rajiv Gandhi**, but the 1991 reforms were **far more comprehensive**.
# Long-term Causes
| Cause | Effect / data |
|---|---|
| Fiscal profligacy | Gross fiscal deficit 5.4% (1981-82) → 8.0% (1989-90); average 6.3% (Sixth Plan) → 8.2% (Seventh) |
| Licence-Permit-Quota Raj | Heavy controls; MRTP Act 1970 |
| PSU losses | Low returns on public investment |
| Weak exports | World export share 2.2% (1948) → 0.5% (1983) |
| Structural | CADs financed by foreign borrowing since the 1980s, incl. short-term ECBs |
- The **long-term** cause = constant **fiscal profligacy** and rising fiscal deficit; the others below were **immediate triggers**.
# Immediate Triggers
| Trigger | Effect |
|---|---|
| Gulf War (Aug 1990) | Oil prices doubled; Gulf remittances dried up (did NOT rise) |
| Political instability | Two changes of government Dec 1989 - June 1991; fall of Chandra Shekhar govt |
| Capital flight | NRIs withdrew deposits; foreign banks stopped lending |
- Road to crisis: 1980s fiscal profligacy → CAD on foreign borrowing → Gulf War shock → BoP crisis 1991.
| Indicator (1990-91 / 1991) | Value |
|---|---|
| Forex reserves (June 1991) | ~$975 million - about two weeks of imports |
| Fiscal deficit | 9.4% of GDP (Centre + States); Centre's GFD 8.4% (6.1% in 1980-81) |
| Current account deficit | 3.1-3.2% of GDP |
| Trade deficit | ₹10,644 crore (3.2% of GDP) |
| Internal debt of govt | 35% (1980-81) → 53% of GDP (1990-91) |
| GDP growth | 1.1% (1990-91); below 1% in 1991-92 |
- Crisis = **high inflation, very low forex reserves, large fiscal deficit** (APPSC 2025).
- NEP 1991 was a **response to a severe BoP crisis**.
- **Immediate cause of forex loss:** **sharp rise in the oil and petroleum import bill** after the Gulf War (APPSC G1).
- The crisis = a **Balance of Payments crisis**; response = **Structural Adjustment Programme** with IMF-World Bank loans (~$7 bn).

| Year | Reform |
|---|---|

=== Stabilisation vs Structural Adjustment
| Basis | Stabilisation | Structural adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| Term | Short-term | Long-term (medium/long-term efficiency and growth) |
| Nature | Demand-side (demand management) | Supply-side |
| Targets | Inflation, fiscal deficit, BoP | Industrial, trade, financial, investment structures |
| Fiscal tools | Cut fiscal deficit: less wasteful spending, subsidy cuts, better tax collection | Tax-sector reform |
| Monetary tools | Tight monetary policy by RBI to control inflation | Financial-sector reform |
| Trade / exchange | Rupee devaluation to correct BoP | Tariff cuts, QR removal, export promotion |
| Industrial | - | Delicensing, MRTP relaxation, FDI opening, disinvestment |
| Lead institution | IMF | World Bank (IBRD) |
- Sequencing: stabilisation first, structural adjustment alongside and after.
- **Structural measures**: delicensing, tariff reduction, public-sector disinvestment.
# IMF-World Bank SAPs
- **Structural Adjustment Programmes** = IMF and World Bank loan packages to **reduce fiscal imbalances and adjust the economy for long-term growth** (APPSC 2018).
- Conditions: austerity, privatisation, deregulation, **currency devaluation**, better tax collection.
- **Eighth Plan (1992-97)** began just after stabilisation and structural adjustment.
- **Washington Consensus** (ten rules): fiscal discipline, tax reform, trade and FDI liberalisation, privatisation, deregulation, property rights - NOT expansion of import quotas. Now seen as obsolete; blamed for the 1997 and 2008 crises (14 Mar 2026).

=== New Industrial Policy 1991, PSU Reform and National Renewal Fund
# Industrial Policy (24 July 1991)
- **Industrial licensing abolished** for all but a few industries (hazardous and strategic, e.g. hazardous chemicals, aerospace); now only **5-6** industries need a licence; reserved for public sector now only **atomic energy and railways** (+ specified minerals).
- Delicensed firms: **pricing freedom**, no expansion curbs.
- **Public-sector reserved industries cut from 17 (1956 policy) to 8**; later pruned further.
- **MRTP**: **asset limits, pre-entry restrictions and merger/expansion controls abolished**. (SEBI's status was a separate reform.)
- **FDI**: automatic approval up to **51%** in high-priority industries (now 100% in most sectors); NRIs/OCBs up to 100%; FIIs allowed into stock markets.
- **BIFR**: set up to revive **sick industrial units**.
# National Renewal Fund (February 1992)
- A **safety net** for workers hit by industrial restructuring under the new policy.
- Objectives: **retraining and redeployment** of employees affected by modernisation and restructuring; **compensation** of affected employees; counselling.
- In practice mostly paid for **Voluntary Retirement Schemes (VRS)** in central PSUs; later limited to public sector units only.

=== Criticism and Current Relevance
| Criticism | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Jobless growth | Output rose faster than employment; capital-intensive growth |
| Agrarian neglect | Public investment in agriculture lagged |
| Crony capitalism | Gains held by large, politically connected firms |
- SAP outcomes: raised poverty and inequality in the Global South (May 2026).
- Reforms 1.0 = LPG (1991); Reforms 2.0 = DPI, UPI, Jan Dhan, DBT (2014 on); "Reforms 3.0" debate (Jul 2026).
- CAD 0.8% of GDP (H1 FY26), peak 4.8% (FY13); ES 2025-26: persistent CAD = structural cause of **high cost of capital**.
- May 2026: PM urged citizens to cut fuel, gold and foreign-travel spending to save forex; ₹57,381 crore **Economic Stabilisation Fund** after the $100/barrel oil shock (14 Mar 2026).
- **Poverty vs inequality:** post-1991 growth **cut poverty** ratios but **widened** income and regional **inequality** (rising consumption Gini).
