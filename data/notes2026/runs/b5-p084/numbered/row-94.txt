## SUB 0 | Observed Impacts – Ice Sheets, Sea Level, Tipping Points and Species
[0:0] # Already observed (cryosphere)
[0:1] - **Observed consequence (APPSC Degree Lecturers 2023):** a **significant reduction in ice-sheet mass** – Greenland and Antarctica have lost mass for about two decades (Greenland ~278 bn t/yr and Antarctica ~155 bn t/yr in 2006-15; loss has **quadrupled since the 1990s**). Falling global temperature, more forest cover and fewer heatwaves are the opposite of what is observed.
[0:2] - **Effects of climate change (NextGen):** rising sea levels, **shrinking mountain glaciers**, accelerating loss of the cryosphere, ocean acidification.
[0:3] - **Most fragile, first affected (UKPSC 2010):** the **Arctic and Greenland ice sheet** – the Arctic warms **about four times** faster than the global average (polar amplification).
[0:4] - **WMO (2025 report):** oceans hold **over 90% of excess heat**; Arctic sea ice near record low; glacier loss threatens water for **about 2 billion people** (not 200 million).
[0:5] - **Ice core = cryogenic indicator** of past climate (UPPCS 2015): drilled from Antarctic/Greenland ice sheets or mountain glaciers; the **lower layers are older**; trapped air bubbles record past CO₂. Pollen, tree rings and evaporites are not cryogenic.
[0:6] TABLE head: Place | Feature
  [0:6.0] Okjökull, Iceland | First glacier declared "dead" (2014; memorial plaque 2019)
  [0:6.1] Quelccaya, Peru | Largest tropical ice cap; retreating ~200 ft/yr
  [0:6.2] Tebua Tarawa and Abanuea (Kiribati) | Uninhabited islets submerged in 1999
  [0:6.3] Kiribati, Tuvalu | Inhabited islands at risk of submergence
  [0:6.4] Maldives | World's first underwater Cabinet meeting (2009) to highlight sea-level rise
  [0:6.5] IndARC | India's first underwater observatory in the Arctic (Kongsfjorden, Svalbard, 2014; UPSC 2015) – not a radar, satellite or Antarctic base
[0:7] # Sea-level rise
[0:8] - **Long-term (eustatic) change:** caused by **melting of ice sheets** and thermal expansion of seawater (CDS 2016). Atmospheric pressure, water density and floating **icebergs** cause only short-term or no change.
[0:9] - **Principal causes:** **thermal expansion + melting glaciers and ice sheets**; global sea level up **~20 cm (1901-2018)**, ~11 cm since 1993; the rate has doubled to ~4.5 mm/yr. **Melting polar ice and sea-level rise** = most important effect of warming.
[0:10] - "Sea-level rise of one metre by 2070" is **not** a correct statement (IPCC: ~0.43-0.84 m by 2100).
[0:11] # Tipping points
[0:12] - **Tipping point:** a threshold where **small changes trigger large, self-perpetuating, potentially irreversible** shifts.
[0:13] - **Loss of Arctic (sea) ice = potential tipping point (APPSC G2 2025):** bright ice (high albedo) melts → darker ocean absorbs more heat → more melting. Cutting CO₂ or less solar radiation are not tipping points.
[0:14] TABLE head: Tipping elements (listed) | NOT tipping elements
  [0:14.0] Greenland and West Antarctic ice sheets, AMOC, permafrost, warm-water coral reefs, Amazon, Arctic sea ice (near record lows) | Sahara dunes, Deccan basalts, European temperate deciduous forests
[0:15] - **UNSG Brief #12 (Jun 2026):** warming ~1.3°C; **coral 1.2°C threshold already crossed** (most fragile; recovery from mass bleaching takes **more than 15 years**); the **Amazon** beyond ~2°C may flip to savanna and turn from a **carbon store into a carbon source**; beyond 3°C the land biosphere may become a source.
[0:16] - **Amazon:** ~17-18% already lost; makes ~50% of its own rain.
[0:17] TABLE head: Impact (IPCC SR1.5, 2018) | 1.5°C | 2°C
  [0:17.0] Coral reefs lost | 70-90% | >99%
  [0:17.1] Ice-free Arctic summer | Once a century | Once a decade
[0:18] # Global effects by region and species
[0:19] - **TSPSC 2023 match:** **bush fires – Australia/USA**; **locust swarming – Africa/Middle East**; **heat wave – Antarctica** (record 2022 heat); **permafrost melt – Arctic** → A-IV, B-III, C-I, D-II.
[0:20] - **Rutgers University study (2019):** warming wipes out about **twice as many ocean species** as land species (APPSC G1).
[0:21] - **IPBES 2019:** ~8 million species; **up to 1 million threatened**; **~40% of amphibians**.
[0:22] - Species shift **poleward and upslope** (cooler toward higher latitudes and elevations).

## SUB 2 | Heat Waves, IMD Alerts and Wet-bulb Temperature
[2:0] - **Heat wave (IMD):** max at least **40°C in plains** and at least **30°C in hilly regions** (37°C at coastal stations), with a departure of 4.5-6.4°C from normal; or actual max **≥45°C regardless of normal** (severe ≥47°C). "30°C plains / 40°C hills" → **neither** statement correct.
[2:1] - **Causes:** meandering **Rossby waves → heat domes**; **loo** over the Gangetic plains; global warming – **not** cold Arabian Sea currents.
[2:2] TABLE head: Colour | Meaning | Action
  [2:2.0] Green | Normal | No action
  [2:2.1] Yellow | Watch / alert | Be updated
  [2:2.2] Orange | Severe | Be prepared
  [2:2.3] Red | Extreme | Take action
[2:3] # Wet-bulb temperature
[2:4] - Lowest temperature air reaches by evaporative cooling; combines **heat + humidity**; equals **dry-bulb at 100% humidity**.
[2:5] - **~35°C wet-bulb = survivability limit**: **sweat can no longer cool the body**. **World Bank:** India among the first places to cross it routinely → implication = survival of humans and animals hit because shedding body heat by perspiration fails (**II only**, UPSC 2025); floods, cyclones and droughts in peninsular India are not what the wet-bulb warning implies.
[2:6] - Indians faced **19.8 heatwave days** in 2024 (Lancet Countdown).
[2:7] # Heat action and health
[2:8] - **Ahmedabad (2013): South Asia's first Heat Action Plan**.
[2:9] - **AP:** heat rising in **Palnadu, Kurnool, Anantapur**; hottest place **Rentachintala (Palnadu)**; **APSDMA Heatwave Action Plan 2026** – heatwaves kill more in AP than any other natural disaster; the **AP Heat Wave Action Plan** is cited as an **early-warning and preparedness** measure; **Rayalaseema – heat stress and chronic drought**.
[2:10] - **Health:** warmer and wetter → **dengue and malaria** spread; floods → cholera; waterlogging → leptospirosis.

## SUB 4 | Agriculture, Monsoon and Adaptation
[4:0] # Agriculture
[4:1] - **Climate-Smart Village** approach = part of **CCAFS** (Climate Change, Agriculture and Food Security) research programme of **CGIAR** (headquartered in **France**, Montpellier); **ICRISAT (Patancheru, Hyderabad)** is a CGIAR centre → all three statements correct (UPSC 2021).
[4:2] - Climate change is already **reducing wheat** output; **wheat/maize lose 4-10% yield per 1°C**; extreme heat cuts **milk yield 15-25%** (FAO-WMO, Apr 2026).
[4:3] TABLE head: Programme | Detail
  [4:3.0] NICRA | ICAR project, 2011, led by CRIDA, Hyderabad
  [4:3.1] ACASA-India | GIS atlas of crop vulnerability to climate change
  [4:3.2] Varieties | 2,996 climate-resilient varieties released 2014-2025; heat-tolerant wheat HD 3385
[4:4] # Monsoon, water and drought
[4:5] - **Over 80% of rain in under 100 days**; SW monsoon ≈ **75%** of annual rain; **NE monsoon (Oct-Dec) ≈ 11%**.
[4:6] - **Each 1°C warming → ~7% more moisture** → heavier rain; extreme **pre-monsoon** rain events **doubled** (1981-2020). **El Niño weakens** the SW monsoon.
[4:7] - **68%** of India drought-prone; droughts = **meteorological, agricultural, hydrological**. **National Water Mission:** **20%** better water-use efficiency.
[4:8] - **Urban floods:** floodplain encroachment, wetland loss, poor drainage and more intense rain.
[4:9] # Social impact and adaptation
[4:10] - **UPPSC 2017 A-R:** climate change is increasing **social tension** (A); more frequent and intense extreme water events threaten **food security** (R) → both true, **R explains A**.
[4:11] - **AP and PMFBY (APPSC G2 A-R):** extreme weather (floods, droughts, cyclones) caused big crop losses in AP (**R true**), but AP's PMFBY coverage did **not** rise steadily – AP left PMFBY for its own free crop-insurance scheme from 2019-20 (**A false**).

## SUB 5 | Coasts and Cyclones – India and Andhra Pradesh
[5:0] - **Cyclone-prone area:** about **8% of India's land area** (NDMA); ~5,700 km of coastline cyclone-prone.
[5:1] - Cyclones are **more frequent in the Bay of Bengal**, but Arabian Sea cyclones rose **52%** (2001-2019) and very severe ones 150%.
[5:2] - **Marine heatwaves** doubled since 1982; raise cyclone **winds up to 20%** and **rain up to 12%** → rapid intensification; in 2025 **more than 90%** of oceans had marine heatwaves.
[5:3] - **INCOIS (Hyderabad):** Coastal Vulnerability Index from **7 parameters**. **NCCR, Chennai:** **33.6%** of India's coastline eroding (1990-2018).

[5:6] - AP cyclones (**Hudhud, Titli, Michaung**) peak **October-December**.
