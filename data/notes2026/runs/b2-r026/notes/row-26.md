=== Veto Power of the President – Types and Article 111
- **Article 111**: a Bill passed by both Houses is presented to the President, who **declares assent**, **withholds assent** or (for a **non-Money Bill**) **returns** it for reconsideration. So the power to withhold assent is in **Art. 111** (not Arts. 63, 108 or 109).
- Does the Indian President have a veto? **Yes** – three of the four kinds: **absolute, suspensive and pocket**; **no qualified veto**.
| Veto | Meaning | Applies to |
|---|---|---|
| **Absolute** | Withholds assent; the Bill ends and never becomes an Act | **Private members' Bills**; **Government Bills when the Cabinet resigns** before assent and the new Cabinet advises against it – **not "at all times"** |
| **Suspensive** | Returns the Bill **once**; if re-passed (with or without amendment) he **must assent** | **Ordinary Bills** (and Financial Bills) – **not Money Bills** |
| **Pocket** | Neither assents, rejects nor returns – keeps the Bill pending **indefinitely** | Possible because **Art. 111 prescribes no time limit** |
| **Qualified** | Can be overridden only by a **higher (two-thirds) majority** | **USA** – not India |
- **Veto by type of Bill:**
| Bill | President (Union) | President (State Bill reserved, Art. 201) |
|---|---|---|
| Ordinary | Assent, withhold or **return once** – must assent if re-passed | Assent, withhold or direct return – **not bound** to assent even if re-passed |
| **Money** | Assent or withhold; **cannot return** (usually assents – introduced on his prior recommendation) | Assent or **withhold** – so he **can veto a State Money Bill** |
| **Constitutional Amendment** | **Must assent** (**24th Amendment, 1971**); cannot reject or return | Cannot be introduced in a State Legislature |
- The one-time return power can apply to **Financial Bills of Categories I and II**; a Money Bill and a Constitutional Amendment Bill cannot be returned.
- APPSC G1 2024 statements: "suspensive veto in Money Bills but not Finance Bills" – **false** (the reverse); "absolute veto on Government Bills at all times" – **false**; "**pocket veto indefinitely; the American President has a qualified veto**" – **true**; "objective = checks and balances between the President's office and Parliament" – **false** (the stated objects are to **prevent hasty, ill-considered or unconstitutional legislation**) → **only III**.
| Feature | India | USA |
|---|---|---|
| Absolute veto | **Yes** | No |
| Qualified veto | No | **Yes** – Congress overrides by **two-thirds** |
| Pocket veto | Yes – **no time limit** | Yes – President must act within **10 days** (the **10-day rule is American**) |
> Exam angle: Art. 111; Money Bill cannot be returned; amendment Bill must be signed; India has no qualified veto.

=== Vetoes Actually Used – Pocket Veto and the Indian Post Office Bill
- **Pocket veto** = the President **keeps a Bill pending with him, without any time limit**, neither approving, rejecting nor returning it – not the Speaker blocking a Bill, the PM dropping ministers or the Rajya Sabha rejecting a Bill.
| Veto | President | Bill |
|---|---|---|
| **Pocket** | **Giani Zail Singh** | **Indian Post Office (Amendment) Bill, 1986** (power to intercept mail) |
| **Absolute** | **Dr. Rajendra Prasad** (on the Council of Ministers' advice) | **PEPSU Appropriation Bill, 1954** |
| Absolute | R. Venkataraman | Salary, Allowances and Pension of MPs (Amendment) Bill, 1991 |
- The Post Office Bill question is asked in every form – "refused to sign / did not assent / did not give assent / first to use the power on it" → **Zail Singh** (not Radhakrishnan, V.V. Giri, Zakir Hussain, R. Venkataraman, K.R. Narayanan, Rajendra Prasad or A.P.J. Abdul Kalam). He **did not return** it; he simply kept it pending, and it was never enacted (later withdrawn).
- Pocket veto used **only once** at the Union level; "the only instance of the President's veto" in older keys means this Bill, though the **PEPSU (1954)** and **1991** Bills were absolute vetoes.
- **2002**: the **Representation of the People (Amendment) Ordinance** on **electoral reforms** was returned by **President Kalam**; the Union Cabinet sent it back unchanged and he **assented under Art. 123**.

=== Ordinance-making Power – Article 123: Basis, Nature, Scope
- **Art. 123** (Part V): the **President** promulgates ordinances during the **recess of Parliament** – not the Speaker, the RS Chairman or the Attorney-General. Governor: **Art. 213** (Part VI). Not Arts. 161, 200, 201, 356 or 360.
- Nature: the **most important legislative power of the President** – a **legislative power of the Executive** (President is part of Parliament, Art. 79), not an executive order. Among his legislative powers (summoning, proroguing, assent), the ordinance is the one examiners key; pardon is a judicial power; **adjourning** a sitting is the presiding officer's job.
- When: only when **both Houses, or either House, are not in session** and he is **satisfied** that circumstances need **immediate action**; none while both Houses sit. Exercised on the **Council of Ministers' advice**.
- Force: the **same force and effect as an Act of Parliament** and the same limits; **co-extensive** with Parliament's legislative power but **not a parallel** power.
| Can | Cannot |
|---|---|
| **Amend or repeal any Central Act** (or an earlier ordinance) | **Amend the Constitution** (Art. 368) |
| **Operate retrospectively** (from a back date) | **Abridge Fundamental Rights** – it is "law" under **Art. 13(2)** |
| Introduce or change tax provisions | Go beyond Parliament's legislative competence |
- UPSC 2025: "amend any Central Act" and "come into effect from a back date" true; "abridge a FR" false → **I and III only**.
- Origin: a **relic of the Government of India Act, 1935** (Governor-General's ordinance power).

=== Ordinance – Life, Lapse, Withdrawal and Judicial Review
- 1. Promulgated during recess. 2. **Laid before both Houses** on reassembly. 3. **Ceases six weeks after reassembly** unless approved – if the Houses reassemble on **different dates**, the six weeks run from the **later date**. 4. Ends **earlier** if **both Houses pass resolutions disapproving** it (within those six weeks – not "six months"), or if the President **withdraws** it.
- The President **can withdraw an ordinance at any time** – "once promulgated it cannot be withdrawn / the President can never repeal it" is **false**.
- **Maximum life = six months + six weeks**: maximum gap between sessions is **six months** (**Art. 85**; States **Art. 174**) + the six-week window – so neither "six months", "three months" nor "indefinitely".
| Item | Fact |
|---|---|
| Lapse | 6 weeks after reassembly |
| Maximum gap between sessions | 6 months |
| Maximum life | **6 months + 6 weeks** |
| Linked limit – **Art. 101(4)** | MP's unexcused absence for **60 days** vacates the seat (prorogation and adjournments of more than four consecutive days excluded) |
- **Satisfaction and judicial review:**
| Year | Event | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| 1975 | **38th Amendment** | Satisfaction under Arts. 123, 213, 352 made **final** |
| 1978 | **44th Amendment** | **Reversed** the 38th – satisfaction justiciable |
| **1987** | **D.C. Wadhwa v State of Bihar** | Routine **re-promulgation** without placing before the legislature = **fraud on the Constitution** |
| **2017** | **Krishna Kumar Singh v State of Bihar** | Seven judges; re-promulgation may be a fraud on the Constitution; **laying is mandatory** |
- Ordinances are **subject to judicial review**; criticism – they **bypass parliamentary deliberation**.
- In news (2026): the **Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Amendment Ordinance, 2026** (judges 33 → 37 besides the CJI) revived the debate on **ordinance misuse and executive overreach**.
- AP: the State used a Governor's ordinance (**Art. 213**) on **organ-transplant committees**.

=== Pardoning Power – Article 72: Forms and Nature
- **Art. 72**: the President may "grant **pardons, reprieves, respites or remissions** of punishment or **suspend, remit or commute** the sentence of any person convicted of any offence" – not Arts. 62, 63, 70, 71, 73, 74 or 75.
| Form | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Pardon** | Removes **both sentence and conviction** – as if the offence was never committed |
| **Commutation** | Substitutes a **lighter form** of punishment (death → rigorous life imprisonment) |
| **Remission** | **Reduces the period** without changing its character (2 years RI → 1 year RI) |
| **Respite** | A **lesser sentence** for special reasons – pregnancy, physical or mental disability |
| **Reprieve** | **Temporary stay of execution** (esp. death) to allow time to seek a pardon |
- Commutation changes the **form**; remission reduces the **duration**.
- Covers: (a) sentence by a **court-martial**; (b) offence against a **Union law**; (c) every **death sentence**. Amnesty is outside Art. 72.
- Nature: listed among the President's **judicial powers** (UPPCS key: judicial power); strictly it is an **executive power, independent of the judiciary** – he is not a court of appeal.
- Exercised on the **aid and advice of the Council of Ministers** (**Maru Ram, 1980**) – he **cannot** act without the Central Government's advice; subject to **limited judicial review** (mala fide, arbitrary, irrelevant grounds). So UPSC 2025 → **I only**.

=== President (Art. 72) vs Governor (Art. 161) and Clemency Cases
- **Both the President (Art. 72) and the Governor (Art. 161)** have pardoning power – not the Chief Justice.
| Point | President (Art. 72) | Governor (Art. 161) |
|---|---|---|
| Offences | Against **Union laws** | Against **State laws** |
| **Death sentence** | **Can pardon** | **Cannot pardon**; can suspend, remit or commute |
| Court-martial | **Yes** | **No** |
- So "the Governor can pardon a sentence of death" is **false**; the Governor is part of the State Legislature, does not appoint HC judges and has no emergency powers – those statements are true.
| Case | Holding |
|---|---|
| **Maru Ram (1980)** | Power exercised on the **Council of Ministers' advice** |
| **Kehar Singh (decided 1988, reported AIR 1989)** | President may examine the evidence afresh; no right of oral hearing |
| **Epuru Sudhakar v Govt of AP (2006)** | **Limited judicial review** – mala fide, arbitrary or irrelevant grounds |

=== State Bills Reserved for the President – Articles 200 and 201
- **Art. 200**: the Governor may **assent, withhold, return** (non-Money Bill) or **reserve** a Bill for the President; the **Governor has no pocket veto**.
- **Art. 201**: for a reserved Bill the President **declares or withholds assent**, or directs its return (non-Money Bill); no time limit is written in Arts. 200 or 201.
- **8 April 2025, State of Tamil Nadu v Governor**: indefinite inaction under **Art. 200** held **justiciable**; **ten** TN Bills deemed assented.
- **Nov 2025, Presidential reference (Art. 143, five judges)**: courts **cannot fix timelines** for the Governor or President; **no deemed assent**; the powers cannot be used arbitrarily.
