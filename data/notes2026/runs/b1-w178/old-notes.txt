## SUB 3 | Wood's Despatch 1854 and the Universities of 1857
[178:3:0] # The Despatch
[178:3:1] - Issued **19 July 1854** by **Sir Charles Wood**, **President of the Board of Control**, under Governor-General **Dalhousie**.
[178:3:2] - **"Magna Carta of English Education in India"** – the **first comprehensive plan** and the **most important education document** of British India (APPSC G1 2002; G1 PYQ).
[178:3:3] - **Aim:** spread of **European (Western) learning and culture**, with practical benefits over oriental knowledge (UPSC 2003).
[178:3:4] # Provisions
[178:3:5] - State to take responsibility for **education of the masses** – **rejected downward filtration** (on paper).
[178:3:6] - **Graded hierarchy:** vernacular primary schools → Anglo-vernacular high schools → affiliated colleges (district) → **universities** in the presidency towns.
[178:3:7] - **Medium:** **English for higher studies**, **vernaculars at school level** – not English at all levels (UPSC 2018: "A and B only").
[178:3:8] - **Grants-in-aid** to encourage private (mostly missionary) enterprise.
[178:3:9] - Stress on **female education**, **vocational** training and **teachers' training**; education departments in every province; secular education in government institutions.
[178:3:10] # Universities of 1857
[178:3:11] TABLE head: University | Year | Note
  [178:3:11.0] Calcutta | 1857 | Under Lord Canning
  [178:3:11.1] Bombay | 1857 | 
  [178:3:11.2] Madras | 1857 | 5 September 1857
[178:3:12] - **Model:** **University of London** – affiliating and examining universities; Calcutta University came from **Wood's Despatch**, not Hunter or Macaulay.
[178:3:13] - Bankim Chandra Chatterjee was among Calcutta's first two graduates (1858).
[178:3:14] ~~ Sources: Spectrum p.597-598; PMF Modern History Part 3 p.34-35; Rocket Indian History Part 1 p.61; app notes 1:178 (sub 5)
[178:3:15] - **Dalhousie's Minute (1853):** backed **vernacular education**; the Despatch followed in 1854.
## SUB 4 | Charter Act 1813, the Orientalist–Anglicist Debate and Macaulay's Minute
[178:4:0] # Steps that led to English education
[178:4:1] - **Charter Act 1813:** **Rs 1 lakh a year** for reviving literature, encouraging learned Indians and promoting **modern sciences**; also admitted **missionaries** and ended the Company's India trade monopoly.
[178:4:2]   - The grant was **not made available till 1823** – delayed by the debate over how to spend it.
[178:4:3] - **General Committee of Public Instruction (GCPI), 1823:** set up to **manage the education grant**; it split into Orientalists and Anglicists.
[178:4:4] - Led to English education: **Charter Act 1813 + GCPI 1823 + Orientalist–Anglicist controversy** – all three (UPSC 2018).
[178:4:5] # The two camps
[178:4:6] TABLE head: Orientalists | Anglicists
  [178:4:6.0] Grant for Sanskrit, Arabic, Persian (classical) learning, alongside some Western subjects | Spend exclusively on Western science and literature
  [178:4:6.1] Warren Hastings, William Jones, Jonathan Duncan, H.T. Colebrooke, H.H. Wilson, Halhed | T.B. Macaulay (leader), Bentinck, Charles Trevelyan, James Mill
  [178:4:6.2] In Madras Thomas Munro backed pathashalas and vernacular schooling | Anglicists were split on the medium: English vs vernaculars
[178:4:7] - **Munro's Survey of Indigenous Education (1826):** a wide network of **vernacular village schools** in the Madras Presidency; his collectorate and tahsildari schools were **closed in 1836** after the 1835 resolution.
[178:4:8] # Macaulay's Minute, 2 February 1835
[178:4:9] - Written by **Macaulay** as **Law Member** and **President of the GCPI**; approved by **William Bentinck** – "Father of Modern Western Education in India".
[178:4:10] - **Settled the row for the Anglicists:** funds for **Western science and literature through English alone**.
[178:4:11] - **Remarks:** "**a single shelf of a good European library** was worth the whole native literature of India and Arabia"; all the history in Sanskrit books "less valuable than ... the most paltry abridgement used at preparatory schools in England" (1835).
[178:4:12] - **Aim:** a class "**Indian in blood and colour, but English in taste, in opinions, in morals and in intellect**" – **interpreters** between rulers and ruled.
[178:4:13] - **Downward Filtration Theory:** educate a few of the upper and middle classes; learning would **filter down** to the masses – an **education** policy (not railways, irrigation or poverty), credited to **Macaulay**; criticised for neglecting mass literacy.
[178:4:14] - **English Education Act 1835 (Bentinck):** English the medium of higher education; promotion of oriental institutions **stopped** – so "stipends and printing of classics were to continue" is the **incorrect** statement.
[178:4:15] - Called the **foundation of modern education** in India (IAS 1993); English education came in **Bentinck's** reign.
[178:4:16] - **Follow-up:** 1837 English replaced Persian as official language; 1844 Hardinge made English knowledge a condition for government jobs.
[178:4:17] > Trap: 1835 = **Bentinck + Macaulay**; 1854 = **Charles Wood + Dalhousie**; downward filtration = Macaulay, rejected by Wood.

## SUB 5 | Aims and Effects of British Education Policy
[178:5:0] # Why the British spread modern education
[178:5:1] - **Main reason:** a **cheap supply of educated Indians for subordinate posts** in administration and British firms – hence English as the medium.
[178:5:2] - Also: pressure from enlightened Indians, missionaries, humanitarian officials; a market for British goods; reconciling Indians to British rule.
[178:5:3] # Effects
[178:5:4] - English education brought Indians **into contact with Western thought** – science, reason, liberty – which fed the socio-cultural renaissance and nationalism.
[178:5:5] - Mass education neglected (illiteracy 84% in 1911); women's and technical education neglected; education a monopoly of upper classes.
[178:5:6] # Technical education
[178:5:7] - **Engineering College, Roorkee (1847)** – later **Thomason College** – India's **first engineering college**; Calcutta College of Engineering 1856; Poona 1858.
[178:5:8] - First medical college: Calcutta, 1835; Curzon set up the agriculture college at **Pusa**.
[178:5:9] ~~ Sources: Spectrum p.597, p.604-605; app notes 1:178
## SUB 6 | Educational Thinkers – Pedagogy Basics
[178:6:0] - **John Dewey:** philosophy of **experimentalism** (pragmatism / instrumentalism) – **learning by doing**, ideas tested in experience.
[178:6:1] - **Pestalozzi:** the **first to integrate psychology with educational theory** – aimed to "psychologise education" (child's mental development; head, heart and hand).
[178:6:2] - **Society and the curriculum:** different socio-economic strata with different class interests **set up mutually incompatible tensions in the educational programme**.

## SUB 1 | Railways – First Lines, Purpose and the Guarantee System
[179:1:0] # Firsts

  [179:1:1.3] First line in North India | – | Delhi → Agra

[179:1:2] - **Governor-General:** **Lord Dalhousie** – railways laid in his time; he is linked with the first train (not Bentinck, Ripon, Curzon).
[179:1:3] - **Credit:** railways, post and telegraph = **Dalhousie**; **state railways = Mayo**.
[179:1:4] # Who built them and why
[179:1:5] - **Initially built and run by private British companies** under the **guarantee system** – a **5% minimum return** on capital, **paid from Indian revenues** whether or not the lines made a profit; the state built lines itself from **1869**.
[179:1:6] - **Official reasons:** faster movement, communication, administration, **troop movement**.
[179:1:7] - **Colonial economic purpose:** carry **raw materials to the ports** and **British goods into Indian markets**; market for British capital goods.
[179:1:8] - **Unintended effects:** a **national market**, shared travel across castes, faster nationalist mobilisation.
[179:1:9] - **Karl Marx (1853):** the railway system would become in India "truly the **forerunner of modern industry**".
[179:1:10] - **Indian Railways** – the **biggest enterprise of the Government of India** today.
[179:1:11] - Nationalist economic critique: **Indian capitalists** demanded "**Indian domination of Indian markets**".
[179:1:12] # Railways in Hyderabad State
[179:1:13] - **Wadi–Secunderabad line** (Nizam's Guaranteed State Railway): built **1871–74**, opened **9 October 1874** (NextGen says 1878; printed key 1872 – both wrong).
[179:1:14] - **Chanda Railway agitation (1883):** protest against extending the **Nizam's State Railway** to Chanda (Chandrapur) on terms favouring a London company; led by **Dr Aghorenath Chattopadhyaya** (Principal) – deported **20 May 1883**; the first public political agitation in Hyderabad.

[179:2:2] - **Acts to control the press:** **1835, 1867, 1878, 1908**.
[179:2:3] - Press censorship was **primarily implemented** by **Wellesley** (1799); Hastings relaxed it in 1818.
[179:2:4] - 1908 and 1910 Acts are both Minto II era but **not cause and effect**.
[179:2:5] - Annie Besant's papers had to deposit Rs 2,000, then Rs 10,000 under the Press Act (1916–17).
[179:2:6] # Vernacular Press Act, 1878
[179:2:7] - Passed **14 March 1878** under **Lord Lytton**, **modelled on the Irish Press Laws**, to **control Indian-language newspapers** (not to encourage them).
[179:2:8] - **Background:** criticism of Lytton over the **1876–77 famine** and the costly **Delhi Durbar (1877)**.
[179:2:9] - **Provisions:** the **district magistrate** (without prior government permission) could make printers/publishers sign a **bond**, demand **forfeitable security**, **seize presses** for repeat offences; **no appeal** to a court; exemption only by **submitting proofs to a government censor**.
[179:2:10] - **Discriminatory:** applied **only to vernacular papers**, not English ones; about 20 English and 200 vernacular papers in 1878 (textbook figure).
[179:2:11] - **Evasion:** **Amrita Bazar Patrika** turned into an **English paper overnight**.
[179:2:12] - Proceedings under it: Som Prakash, Bharat Mihir, Dacca Prakash, Samachar.
[179:2:13] - **Repeal:** **1882** by **Lord Ripon**; pre-censorship dropped, a **press commissioner** appointed.

## SUB 5 | Telegraph, Post and the Press Today
[179:5:0] # Telegraph and post
[179:5:1] - **First telegraph line:** Calcutta → **Diamond Harbour**, **W.B. O'Shaughnessy** (experimental 1850; opened **1851**); Calcutta–Agra 1853.
[179:5:2] - Telegraph trunk lines linked Calcutta, Bombay, Madras and Peshawar (Dalhousie); **India–Europe telegraph 1865** (John Lawrence, 1864–69).
[179:5:3] - **Post Office Act 1854 (Dalhousie):** **postage stamps** and a **uniform rate** (half anna a letter) regardless of distance.
[179:5:4] - **First stamps:** **Scinde Dawk (1852)**, Karachi; the first all-India stamps (1854) were printed at the **Survey of India** (Surveyor General's) office, Calcutta.
[179:5:5] # Press today (as of 2026)
[179:5:6] - **Press and Registration of Periodicals Act, 2023** replaced the **1867** Act (in force 1 March 2024); the RNI is now the **Press Registrar General of India**.
[179:5:7] - **World Press Freedom Index 2026 (RSF, 30 April 2026):** India **157th of 180**.
[179:5:8] - **Press Council** (Press Council Act, **1978**) has no punitive powers.

## SUB 3 | Ilbert Bill, 1883 – Indian Judges and European Offenders
[180:3:0] - **Viceroy:** **Ripon**; drafted by **Law Member C.P. Ilbert**.
[180:3:1] - **Aim:** remove the **disqualification of Indian magistrates** – let Indian **district magistrates and sessions judges** try **European British subjects** in **criminal cases**; Indians and Europeans **on par in criminal jurisdiction**.
[180:3:2] - **Not:** restrictions on carrying arms (Arms Act 1878), on vernacular newspapers (Vernacular Press Act 1878), civil service exams in India, or the cotton import duty.

=== NOTES 1:181 | Revolt of 1857 – Political Causes
## SUB 0 | Doctrine of Lapse – Dalhousie's Annexations (1848-56)
[181:0:0] - **Rule:** a dependent state whose ruler died **without a natural male heir** lapsed to the Company; an **adopted son** could inherit the ruler's **private property only**, not the throne.
[181:0:1] - **Lord Dalhousie** (1848-56) applied it with zeal, but he was **not its originator** (earlier cases existed). Announced/first applied **1848**.
[181:0:2] TABLE head: State | Year
  [181:0:2.0] Satara (FIRST) | 1848
  [181:0:2.1] Jaitpur (Bundelkhand) | 1849
  [181:0:2.2] Sambalpur (Orissa) | 1849
  [181:0:2.3] Baghat | 1850
  [181:0:2.4] Udaipur (Chhattisgarh) | 1852
  [181:0:2.5] Jhansi (adopted son Damodar Rao not recognised) | 1854
  [181:0:2.6] Nagpur (Bhonsle, Raghuji III) | 1854
[181:0:3] - **Chronology:** **Satara (1848) → Sambalpur (1849) → Jhansi (1854)**.
[181:0:4] - **Not annexed under lapse:** **Jaipur**, **Bharatpur**, "Karnataka" – and **Awadh** (misgovernment) and **Punjab** (conquest).
[181:0:5] - **Kittur (1824):** an earlier refusal to recognise an adopted son – **Rani Chennamma** fought the British.
[181:0:6] - **Withdrawn** by **Queen Victoria's Proclamation (1 Nov 1858)**, read by **Canning** at the **Allahabad Durbar**; adoption accepted; princes guaranteed in return for **paramountcy**.
[181:0:7] - Canning called the loyal princes **"breakwaters to the storm"**.
[181:0:8] > A-R (CDS 2009): the revolt broke out soon after Dalhousie left (1856), and his **annexationist policy** caused great discontent – R explains A.

## SUB 1 | Annexation of Awadh (1856) – the "Misgovernment" Plea
[181:1:0] - **Awadh annexed Feb 1856** by Dalhousie on the ground of **"misgovernment" (maladministration)** – **not** lapse, war or subsidiary alliance.
[181:1:1] - Nawab **Wajid Ali Shah** was **dethroned and exiled to Calcutta**; the British accused him of **failing to control the rebellious chiefs and taluqdars** (after the 1801 Subsidiary Alliance he had no army of his own).
[181:1:2] - He was in fact widely loved; his couplet **"Dar-o-deewar pe hasrat se nazar karte hain, khush raho ahle-watan hum to safar karte hain"** was later sung by revolutionaries.
[181:1:3] - **Summary Revenue Settlement 1856** – made directly with **village proprietors**; **taluqdars** were **disarmed, forts destroyed**, estates confiscated (taluqdars' share of villages fell from 67% to 38%).
[181:1:4] - **Bengal Army** sepoys hurt – Awadh was their home; their **families bore the new revenue burden**.

  [181:1:5.2] Jhansi | 1854 | Doctrine of Lapse
  [181:1:5.3] Awadh | 1856 | Misgovernment
[181:1:6] > Pairs: Satara **1848** (not 1849), Punjab **1849** (not 1848), Jhansi **1854** (not 1855), Awadh **1856** – correct.

=== NOTES 1:182 | Revolt of 1857 – Economic, Socio-religious, Administrative and Military Causes
## SUB 0 | Immediate Cause – Enfield Rifle and Greased Cartridges
[182:0:0] - **Immediate cause:** the new **Enfield rifle**; its cartridge paper, reportedly greased with **cow and pig fat**, had to be **bitten off** – offended Hindus and Muslims alike → suspicion that the British were **interfering in religion** and converting them.
[182:0:1] - Enfield **replaced the Brown Bess** musket; introduced **December 1856** (app notes; some accounts say late 1856 or Jan 1857); training at **Dum Dum, Ambala and Sialkot**.
[182:0:2] - **Bone-dust rumour:** the Company's **flour (atta)** was said to be mixed with **bone dust**.
[182:0:3] - **Chapatis** passed from village to village across **North India**; symbols of the revolt = **lotus and chapati**.
[182:0:4] TABLE head: Event | Date
  [182:0:4.0] 19th NI, Berhampur – refused cartridges | Feb 1857
  [182:0:4.1] Mangal Pandey (34th BNI), Barrackpore | 29 March 1857; hanged 8 April 1857
  [182:0:4.2] 85 sepoys of the 3rd Native Cavalry, Meerut – 10 years' prison | 9 May 1857
  [182:0:4.3] Meerut rising | 10 May 1857
  [182:0:4.4] Rebels at Delhi proclaim Bahadur Shah II Shahenshah-e-Hindustan | 11-12 May 1857
[182:0:5] - **First martyr:** **Mangal Pandey**.

## SUB 1 | Military Grievances – the Sepoys Start the Revolt
[182:1:0] - The revolt **began as a mutiny of the sepoys** (not zamindars, peasants or plantation workers); **discontent of soldiers** was the first trigger; civilians joined later.

  [182:1:1.0] General Service Enlistment Act 1856 (Canning) | New Bengal Army recruits to serve overseas – crossing the sea meant loss of caste

  [182:1:1.2] Promotion | An Indian could rise no higher than Subedar
  [182:1:1.3] Pay | Sepoy paid much less than his British counterpart of the same rank

[182:1:3] - European troops had been drawn off for the **Crimean and Persian wars**.
[182:1:4] - **Not a cause:** the **inefficiency of the officers** (BPSC 2018).
