## SUB 1 | Charter Act 1813, the Orientalist–Anglicist Debate and Macaulay's Minute
[1:0] # Steps that led to English education
[1:1] - **Charter Act 1813:** **Rs 1 lakh a year** for reviving literature, encouraging learned Indians and promoting **modern sciences**; also admitted **missionaries** and ended the Company's India trade monopoly.
[1:2]   - The grant was **not made available till 1823** – delayed by the debate over how to spend it.
[1:3] - **General Committee of Public Instruction (GCPI), 1823:** set up to **manage the education grant**; it split into Orientalists and Anglicists.
[1:4] - Led to English education: **Charter Act 1813 + GCPI 1823 + Orientalist–Anglicist controversy** – all three (UPSC 2018).
[1:5] # The two camps
[1:6] TABLE head: Orientalists | Anglicists
  [1:6.0] Grant for Sanskrit, Arabic, Persian (classical) learning, alongside some Western subjects | Spend exclusively on Western science and literature
  [1:6.1] Warren Hastings, William Jones, Jonathan Duncan, H.T. Colebrooke, H.H. Wilson, Halhed | T.B. Macaulay (leader), Bentinck, Charles Trevelyan, James Mill
  [1:6.2] In Madras Thomas Munro backed pathashalas and vernacular schooling | Anglicists were split on the medium: English vs vernaculars
[1:7] - **Munro's Survey of Indigenous Education (1826):** a wide network of **vernacular village schools** in the Madras Presidency; his collectorate and tahsildari schools were **closed in 1836** after the 1835 resolution.
[1:8] # Macaulay's Minute, 2 February 1835
[1:9] - Written by **Macaulay** as **Law Member** and **President of the GCPI**; approved by **William Bentinck** – "Father of Modern Western Education in India".
[1:10] - **Settled the row for the Anglicists:** funds for **Western science and literature through English alone**.
[1:11] - **Remarks:** "**a single shelf of a good European library** was worth the whole native literature of India and Arabia"; all the history in Sanskrit books "less valuable than ... the most paltry abridgement used at preparatory schools in England" (1835).
[1:12] - **Aim:** a class "**Indian in blood and colour, but English in taste, in opinions, in morals and in intellect**" – **interpreters** between rulers and ruled.
[1:13] - **Downward Filtration Theory:** educate a few of the upper and middle classes; learning would **filter down** to the masses – an **education** policy (not railways, irrigation or poverty), credited to **Macaulay**; criticised for neglecting mass literacy.
[1:14] - **English Education Act 1835 (Bentinck):** English the medium of higher education; promotion of oriental institutions **stopped** – so "stipends and printing of classics were to continue" is the **incorrect** statement.
[1:15] - Called the **foundation of modern education** in India (IAS 1993); English education came in **Bentinck's** reign.
[1:16] - **Follow-up:** 1837 English replaced Persian as official language; 1844 Hardinge made English knowledge a condition for government jobs.
[1:17] > Trap: 1835 = **Bentinck + Macaulay**; 1854 = **Charles Wood + Dalhousie**; downward filtration = Macaulay, rejected by Wood.
[1:18] ~~ Sources: Spectrum p.596-597; PMF Modern History Part 3 p.17, p.20, p.31-32; Rocket Indian History Part 2 p.69; app notes 1:178 (sub 2-4)
## SUB 2 | Wood's Despatch 1854 and the Universities of 1857
[2:0] # The Despatch
[2:1] - Issued **19 July 1854** by **Sir Charles Wood**, **President of the Board of Control**, under Governor-General **Dalhousie**.
[2:2] - **"Magna Carta of English Education in India"** – the **first comprehensive plan** and the **most important education document** of British India (APPSC G1 2002; G1 PYQ).
[2:3] - **Aim:** spread of **European (Western) learning and culture**, with practical benefits over oriental knowledge (UPSC 2003).
[2:4] # Provisions
[2:5] - State to take responsibility for **education of the masses** – **rejected downward filtration** (on paper).
[2:6] - **Graded hierarchy:** vernacular primary schools → Anglo-vernacular high schools → affiliated colleges (district) → **universities** in the presidency towns.
[2:7] - **Medium:** **English for higher studies**, **vernaculars at school level** – not English at all levels (UPSC 2018: "A and B only").
[2:8] - **Grants-in-aid** to encourage private (mostly missionary) enterprise.
[2:9] - Stress on **female education**, **vocational** training and **teachers' training**; education departments in every province; secular education in government institutions.
[2:10] # Universities of 1857
[2:11] TABLE head: University | Year | Note
  [2:11.0] Calcutta | 1857 | Under Lord Canning
  [2:11.1] Bombay | 1857 | 
  [2:11.2] Madras | 1857 | 5 September 1857
[2:12] - **Model:** **University of London** – affiliating and examining universities; Calcutta University came from **Wood's Despatch**, not Hunter or Macaulay.
[2:13] - Bankim Chandra Chatterjee was among Calcutta's first two graduates (1858).

## SUB 5 | Aims and Effects of British Education Policy
[5:0] # Why the British spread modern education
[5:1] - **Main reason:** a **cheap supply of educated Indians for subordinate posts** in administration and British firms – hence English as the medium.
[5:2] - Also: pressure from enlightened Indians, missionaries, humanitarian officials; a market for British goods; reconciling Indians to British rule.
[5:3] # Effects
[5:4] - English education brought Indians **into contact with Western thought** – science, reason, liberty – which fed the socio-cultural renaissance and nationalism.
[5:5] - Mass education neglected (illiteracy 84% in 1911); women's and technical education neglected; education a monopoly of upper classes.
[5:6] # Technical education
[5:7] - **Engineering College, Roorkee (1847)** – later **Thomason College** – India's **first engineering college**; Calcutta College of Engineering 1856; Poona 1858.
[5:8] - First medical college: Calcutta, 1835; Curzon set up the agriculture college at **Pusa**.
[5:9] ~~ Sources: Spectrum p.597, p.604-605; app notes 1:178
