## SUB 2 | Disposal and Treatment Methods
[2:0] TABLE head: Method | Process | Suited to
  [2:0.0] Open dumping | Untreated, uncovered, unsegregated waste on land | Not viable – flies, rats, leachate
  [2:0.1] Landfill | Most common and economic method in India; most urban household waste ends here | Residual waste; last resort
  [2:0.2] Sanitary landfill | Waste compacted in cells and covered with earth every day; liners (clay/plastic) + leachate collection protect groundwater; adopted as a substitute for open-burning dumps | Only residual inert waste; costly
  [2:0.3] Composting (aerobic) – Indore method | Microbes (bacteria, fungi) oxidise organics; material turned at intervals; 2–4 weeks | Wet/organic waste
  [2:0.4] Bangalore method (anaerobic) | Pits with alternate layers of MSW and night soil; 4–6 months | Organic waste
  [2:0.5] Vermicomposting | Earthworms break waste down (vermiculture) | Household organic waste
  [2:0.6] Incineration | Controlled burning at high heat in oxygen; cuts volume ~90%; steam for power; emits pollutants, toxic ash | Hazardous, infectious/biomedical
  [2:0.7] Pyrolysis | Thermo-chemical decomposition (heating) without oxygen → gas, oil, char (carbon-based fuels) | Mixed waste; costly
  [2:0.8] Plasma gasification | Extreme thermal process (electricity + very high temperature), no combustion → syngas (H2 + CO); inorganics → slag | All organic wastes
  [2:0.9] RDF | Shredded, dehydrated high-calorific dry MSW | Fuel in cement kilns and thermal plants
[2:1] - **Pyrolysis and plasma gasification = waste-to-energy technologies** (UPSC 2019); compost and vermicompost give manure, not energy.
[2:2] - **Compost:** biodegradable waste decomposed in pits; rich in organic matter and nutrients; earthworms and microbes **speed up** (not slow down) decomposition. In composting both aerobes and anaerobes degrade organic matter. **Vermiculture** = the science of **raising earthworms**.
[2:3] - **Biomining** uses bacteria, **fungi** or plants to extract metals from ores and solid wastes; composting also uses fungi.
[2:4] - Urban India uses **composting, incineration and landfilling – "all the above"**.
[2:5] - **Other pollution solutions:** **Oilzapper** = bacterial mix for oil spills and oily sludge; **bioremediation** uses organisms to remove pollutants; **UV purification** kills microbes in drinking water; **hydrogen fuel cells** emit only **water vapour**.
[2:6] - **Better method to reduce the solid-waste problem: recycling** (not burning, sea dumping or compressing). **Composting household waste** cuts methane and helps reduce global warming; **burning household waste** does not reduce GHG emissions.

## SUB 3 | Biodegradable vs Non-biodegradable; Kinds of Waste
[3:0] # Definitions
[3:1] - **Solid waste:** discarded or abandoned material in **solid, liquid, semi-solid or containerised gaseous** form; includes urban, agricultural, biomedical and radioactive wastes.
[3:2] - **Liquid waste (sewage):** fluids from washing, flushing or manufacturing. **Sullage** = waste water from **kitchens** and bathrooms (grey water), not from toilets (black water/sewage), factories or hospitals.
[3:3] - **E-waste (ITU):** discarded electrical and electronic equipment and parts **not meant for reuse**. **Biomedical waste:** waste from **diagnosis, treatment or immunisation of humans or animals** (blood, sharps, cultures, discarded medicines).
[3:4] - **Garbage** = putrescible kitchen/market waste; **rubbish** = household/commercial waste other than garbage and ashes.
[3:5] # Biodegradable vs non-biodegradable
[3:6] - **Biodegradable:** decomposed by micro-organisms (mainly **bacteria**) into simpler harmless substances. **Aerobic** decomposition → **compost**; **anaerobic** → **methane (biogas)**.
[3:7] - **Non-biodegradable:** mainly inorganic; cannot be broken down by microbes, so it accumulates; **recycling** is the ideal option for it.
[3:8] TABLE head: Biodegradable | Non-biodegradable
  [3:8.0] Sewage, faecal matter, urine, domestic garbage, food/kitchen waste, vegetable and fruit peels, cow dung, paddy husk | Plastic, polythene bags, plastic cups, DDT, insecticides/pesticides, asbestos
  [3:8.1] Paper, wood, grass, leaves, plant waste, cellulose, starch, proteins | Tin, aluminium foil, metal scrap, mercury, lead
  [3:8.2] Wool (natural keratin fibre), cotton, leather | Glass bottles, China clay cups, polystyrene cups
  [3:8.3] Natural rubber | Synthetic rubber/tyres, nylon, Teflon, PVC
  [3:8.4] Polymers: polylactic acid (PLA), PHBV, polyglycolic acid, Nylon-2-Nylon-6 | Polyethylene, polystyrene, polycarbonate
[3:9] - **Polythene** is a **polymer of ethylene (C2H4)** – carbon and hydrogen only; polythene bags cannot be destroyed by bacteria because they are synthetic **polymers**.
[3:10] - Biodegradable polymers are eco-friendly because microbes degrade them; non-biodegradable plastics persist and break into **microplastics**.
[3:11] - **Biodegradable plastics** (e.g. PLA) are a better alternative, but some need much energy and resources to make and can still pollute on disposal – both statements true (TSPSC 2023).
[3:12] - **Industrial wastes:** **thermal power plants** give fly ash (non-biodegradable) – they produce the most non-biodegradable waste; **gypsum** comes from **fertiliser** plants, not food processing. Food processing, paper, cotton, textile and sugar mills give mostly non-hazardous, biodegradable waste.
[3:13] TABLE head: Litter | Time to degrade
  [3:13.0] Paper | 10–30 days
  [3:13.1] Woollen items | about 1 year
  [3:13.2] Tin, aluminium and other metal items | 100–500 years
  [3:13.3] Plastic bags | up to 1 million years
[3:14] # Categories
[3:15] TABLE head: Category | What it holds
  [3:15.0] Municipal (MSW) | Household + commercial: organics, plastics, paper, metals, street and market waste
  [3:15.1] Agricultural | Crop residues, paddy husk, cattle dung (biodegradable; dung → biogas under GOBARdhan)
  [3:15.2] Construction & demolition | Mostly inert – concrete, bricks, dirt, rocks, debris
  [3:15.3] Hazardous | Paints, batteries, pesticides, chemicals, fluorescent lamps, aerosol cans
  [3:15.4] Biomedical / E-waste / Radioactive | Separate rules (see later sub-sections)
[3:16] - **Hazardous waste – four classic criteria:** **ignitability, corrosivity, reactivity, toxicity** (NOT biodegradability). **Household hazardous waste:** **paints and batteries**, bulbs, aerosol cans, tyres.
[3:17] - **Healthcare waste (WHO):** **~85% general non-hazardous, ~15% hazardous** (infectious, chemical, radioactive).
[3:18] - **Land pollution:** its significant cause is the **disposal of solid waste** (not rainfall or planting trees).

## SUB 6 | E-waste
[6:0] # Rules
[6:1] - **EPR in Indian law was first introduced by the e-Waste (Management and Handling) Rules 2011** (UPSC 2019; not the BMW Rules 1998, Recycled Plastic Rules 1999 or Hazardous Wastes Rules 2016). Rocket Environment p.45 credits the Plastic Waste (M&H) Rules 2011 – a source slip; UPSC's answer is the e-waste rules.
[6:2] - **E-Waste (Management) Rules 2016:** 21 products; PROs, deposit-refund scheme, e-waste exchange.
[6:3] - **E-Waste (Management) Rules 2022:** notified by **MoEFCC in November 2022**; in force **1 April 2023** (not Nov 2022); **106 EEE items incl. solar PV** waste; do NOT apply to packaging plastics or radioactive waste (separate rules).
[6:4] - Collection of e-waste is the **producer's** responsibility (EPR); only **registered** producers, manufacturers, refurbishers and recyclers may deal; producers register on the **CPCB portal** and buy **digital EPR certificates** from registered recyclers; annual targets on past sales.
[6:5] - **Battery Waste Management Rules 2022** replaced the 2001 rules.
[6:6] # Magnitude
[6:7] - India = **3rd-largest** e-waste generator (after **China, USA**); about **2.2 million tonnes (2025)**. **Mumbai** = largest e-waste-producing city (ASSOCHAM); Maharashtra the top State.
[6:8] - **3.54 lakh tonnes** collected and processed in **FY 2020-21** (of 13.46 lakh t generated); 11.59 lakh t recycled in FY 2024-25.
[6:9] - **Global E-waste Monitor** (**ITU and UNITAR**) 2024: **62 Mt** in 2022, only **22.3%** formally recycled; 82 Mt by 2030.
[6:10] # Recycling chain and hazards
[6:11] - Chain: consumer → collection centre / producer take-back → **authorised dismantler → authorised recycler** → recovered materials. Citizens should **sell or give old electronics to authorised recyclers** (not burn, bin or dump them).
[6:12] - **78%** processed informally; informal hubs **Seelampur (Delhi), Moradabad (UP)**. "Invisible e-waste": vapes, USB cables, LED accessories.
[6:13] - **R2 (Responsible Recycling) Code of Practices:** environmentally responsible practices in the **electronics recycling industry** (UPSC 2021).
[6:14] - Old computers release **beryllium, cadmium, chromium, mercury, lead**, PCBs, PBBs – NOT **heptachlor** (an insecticide) or **plutonium** (radioactive) (UPSC 2013).

  [6:15.1] Cadmium | Kidney and bone
  [6:15.2] Lead | Nervous system and kidney
  [6:15.3] PBBs / BFRs | Persistent; endocrine effects

## SUB 7 | Biomedical, Hazardous and C&D Waste; Conventions
[7:0] # Biomedical waste (BMW)
[7:1] - **BMW Management Rules 2016** replaced the **1998 Rules (10 categories)**; amended 2018, 2019; **4 colour categories**; **barcoding** and GPS tracking; no on-site facility if a common facility is within 75 km.
[7:2] TABLE head: Colour | Waste | Treatment
  [7:2.0] Yellow | Anatomical, soiled, expired medicines, chemical | Incineration / plasma pyrolysis / deep burial
  [7:2.1] Red | Contaminated recyclable plastics (tubes, IV sets, catheters, syringes without needles) | Autoclave/microwave + shredding
  [7:2.2] White (translucent) | Sharps – needles, blades | Autoclave, shredding, encapsulation
  [7:2.3] Blue | Glassware, metallic implants | Disinfection, recycling
[7:3] - BMW must be treated before disposal **to prevent the spread of infection and protect human health**. **COVID-19:** CPCB guidelines (2020) + **COVID19BWM** app.
[7:4] - **BMW generated (MoEF 2016): 0.17 million tonnes a year** (about 484 t/day).
