=== NOTES 2:14 | Right to Constitutional Remedies – Article 32; the Five Writs and Their Scope
## SUB 0 | The Five Writs – Literal Meaning and Purpose
[14:0:0] - **Article 32(2)** names **five writs**: **Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Certiorari, Quo Warranto** (not "Quid Warranto", not injunction). Both the **Supreme Court (Art. 32)** and the **High Courts (Art. 226)** issue **all five**.
[14:0:1] - Writs began as **prerogative writs of English common law**; before 1950 only the **Calcutta, Bombay and Madras** High Courts could issue them.
[14:0:2] TABLE head: Writ | Literal meaning | Purpose | Lies against
  [14:0:2.0] Habeas Corpus | "To have the body (of)" / "you may have the body" | Detained person produced before the court; release from illegal detention; court inquires into the legality of detention | Public authorities and private individuals
  [14:0:2.1] Mandamus | "We command" (an order) | Orders a public official, body, inferior court or government to perform a public duty it refused or neglected; can also order it not to enforce an unconstitutional law | Public officials, statutory bodies, govt companies, inferior courts, tribunals, government
  [14:0:2.2] Prohibition | "To forbid" / restrain | Higher court stops a lower court/tribunal exceeding its jurisdiction while the case is pending | Judicial and quasi-judicial bodies only
  [14:0:2.3] Certiorari | "To be certified" / informed | Higher court calls up the record of a lower court/tribunal for review; quashes an order already passed; also transfers a pending case | Judicial, quasi-judicial and (since 1991) certain administrative bodies
  [14:0:2.4] Quo Warranto | "By what authority (or warrant)?" / "what is your authority" | Court asks a holder to show his right to a public office; ousts an ineligible person / usurper | Holder of a substantive public office created by statute or the Constitution
[14:0:3] - **Habeas Corpus** = the writ of **personal liberty** – the "**bulwark of individual liberty**", the **greatest safeguard of personal liberty**; also used to produce a person **missing or in custody**. The term belongs to **law**.
[14:0:4] - **Mandamus** = Judiciary **orders the Executive to perform its duty**; "an order to act legally and abstain from an unlawful act". **Mandamus directs activity; Prohibition directs inactivity.**
[14:0:5] - **Certiorari** = the "**postmortem**" writ – examines the working of **subordinate courts**; brought to get an order **quashed**. Grounds: lack/excess of jurisdiction, **error of law on the face of the record**.
[14:0:6] - **Wrong pairs** asked: "Quo Warranto – certify the proceedings" (certify = Certiorari); "Prohibition – ban on liquor"; "Mandamus – produce the body".
[14:0:7] TABLE head: Match (APPSC G2 Mains 2025) | Meaning
  [14:0:7.0] Quo Warranto | ii. by what authority
  [14:0:7.1] Mandamus | iii. we command
  [14:0:7.2] Habeas Corpus | iv. to have the body of
  [14:0:7.3] Certiorari | i. to be certified

## SUB 1 | Heart and Soul – Article 32 as a Fundamental Right
[14:1:0] - **Right to Constitutional Remedies = Article 32** – the right to **move the Supreme Court directly** when a Fundamental Right is violated (not Arts. 14, 15, 17, 19, 21, 29-31, 33, 34 or 36). It is **itself a Fundamental Right**.
[14:1:1] - **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar**: Art. 32 is "**the very soul of the Constitution and the very heart of it**" – the Article without which the Constitution would be a **nullity** (not Freedom of Speech, Equality, Religion or Property).
[14:1:2] - **Why**: "a right without a remedy is a **worthless declaration**" – Art. 32 gives an **effective remedy** against violation of FRs (A-R: A true, R true, R explains A).
[14:1:3] - "**Soul**" alone is also used for the **Preamble** (Thakur Das Bhargava; Rocket: "key / soul of the Constitution"); with Ambedkar or "heart and soul" the answer is always **Art. 32 / Right to Constitutional Remedies**.
[14:1:4] - **Basic structure**: judicial review under **Art. 32** (SC) and **Arts. 226 and 227** (HCs) is part of the **inviolable basic structure** (Kesavananda Bharati 1973, 13 judges; **L. Chandra Kumar 1997**); Art. 245 is not.
[14:1:5] - The power of **judicial review** of the Supreme Court is exercised through **Art. 32** (not 131, 132, 134); the two provisions that most clearly express judicial review = **Arts. 32 and 226**. Judicial review rests on **Arts. 13, 32, 226** – **not Art. 225** (jurisdiction of existing HCs).

## SUB 2 | Writ Jurisdiction – Article 32 vs Article 226; Guardian of Fundamental Rights
[14:2:0] - Citizens move the **Supreme Court (Art. 32)** or the **High Court (Art. 226)** for infringement of FRs – **Arts. 32 and 226** (not 33/227, 34/228). **Only the SC and HCs** issue writs – not "all courts", Parliament or the President.
[14:2:1] - **Article 226** empowers the **High Courts** to issue writs (not 213, 225, 227, 228, 249, 262).
[14:2:2] - **Guardian / protector of Fundamental Rights = the Judiciary** (Supreme Court as "protector and guarantor") – **not the President**, Parliament, Executive, Cabinet or parties. The **Supreme Court** is also the **custodian / guardian of the Constitution**.
[14:2:3] - The SC's writ power is **original but not exclusive** – "SC has exclusive power to issue writs" is **false**; HCs also decide the **validity of Central laws** (A false, R true – UPPCS 2019; official key (a) was wrong).
[14:2:4] TABLE head: Point | Art. 32 (SC) | Art. 226 (HC)
  [14:2:4.0] Nature | Itself a Fundamental Right – SC cannot refuse when an FR is shown | Constitutional, discretionary remedy
  [14:2:4.1] Scope | Only Fundamental Rights (narrower) | FRs and "any other purpose" – constitutional and statutory (legal) rights (wider)
  [14:2:4.2] Territory | Whole of India (wider) | Own territory; beyond it if the cause of action arises inside (15th Amendment, 1963)
  [14:2:4.3] Writs | Five | Five
[14:2:5] - So **High Courts are in a better position** to protect citizens' rights, **because the SC issues writs only for FRs** (A and R true, R explains A). The HC writ power covers **constitutional, statutory and fundamental rights** (all).
[14:2:6] - A right that is **not an FR** – e.g. the **right to vote** – is enforced in the **High Court under Art. 226**, not Art. 32.
[14:2:7] - **Art. 226 is ordinarily refused** where an **alternative and equally efficacious remedy** exists (rule of prudence; T.R. Verma 1957) or where **disputed questions of fact** need trial; it is **not** barred merely because no FR is involved ("any other purpose").
[14:2:8] - **Match (APPSC G2 Mains 2025):** **Art. 137** = SC **reviews** its own judgment; **Art. 226** = HCs issue writs; **Art. 13** = SC declares a law **unconstitutional**; **Art. 32** = SC restores FRs by writs.
[14:2:9] - **Res judicata**: an HC dismissal **on merits** bars the same plea under Art. 32 (remedy: appeal).
[14:2:10] - **20 Jan 2026**: SC (Justices Dipankar Datta and S.C. Sharma) agreed to examine whether the **Enforcement Directorate** can invoke writ jurisdiction (appeals by Kerala and Tamil Nadu). **16 Jul 2026**: SC studied an **SOP for 24x7 urgent hearings** under Arts. 32 and 226 in threats to **life and liberty**.
[14:2:11] # Amendments
[14:2:12] TABLE head: Amendment | Year | Point
  [14:2:12.0] 42nd | 1976 | Inserted Art. 32A (no challenge to State laws under Art. 32); curbed Art. 226
  [14:2:12.1] 43rd | 1977 | Omitted Arts. 32A, 131A, 144A, 226A, 228A
  [14:2:12.2] 44th | 1978 | Restored Art. 226

## SUB 3 | Scope and Limits of Each Writ
[14:3:0] # Habeas Corpus
[14:3:1] - The **only writ that lies against private persons** as well as the State; safeguards the liberty of **all persons** in the territory, citizens or not.
[14:3:2] - Not issued where detention is **lawful**, by a **competent court** (judicial custody), for contempt of court/legislature, or outside the court's territory. A habeas corpus petition is **not dismissed for imperfect pleadings**.
[14:3:3] - **Wrong statement** (asked twice): "Habeas corpus lies against the executive only, not against private persons".
[14:3:4] # Mandamus
[14:3:5] - Lies against **public officials, government, public corporations and government companies** performing public duties, inferior courts and tribunals.
[14:3:6] - Does **not** lie against: a **private individual or body** (unless entrusted with a **public duty** or **colluding with a public authority**); the **President or Governors** (**Art. 361**); a **contractual** obligation; a **discretionary** duty; non-statutory instructions; a Chief Justice acting judicially.
[14:3:7] - **UPSC 2022**: A (no mandamus against a private body unless it has a public duty) – correct; B (no mandamus against a government company) – **wrong**; C (any public-minded person may seek Quo Warranto) – correct → **A and C only**.
[14:3:8] - "Mandamus cannot be issued against the **Government**" is the **incorrect** statement; it can lie against a public servant; it cannot against a private individual.
[14:3:9] - **Law and policy**: mandamus **cannot compel the Government to make a law or a policy** (e.g. to **appoint a commission to study climate change**), but **enforces a policy once laid down**; it does lie to compel statutory/public duties (filling seats, cleanliness, cyclone shelters). A-R: both true, R explains A.
[14:3:10] # Prohibition, Certiorari, Quo Warranto
[14:3:11] - **Prohibition**: only against **judicial and quasi-judicial bodies** – not administrative authorities, legislatures, private persons. It goes to the **lower court**, not to Parliament, an officer or the Government.
[14:3:12] - **Certiorari**: not against **legislative bodies** or **private persons**; since **1991** also against administrative bodies affecting rights.
[14:3:13] - **Quo Warranto**: **any public-minded person** may seek it (need not be aggrieved); not for **ministerial or private** office.
[14:3:14] TABLE head: Prohibition vs Certiorari | Prohibition | Certiorari
  [14:3:14.0] Stage | While proceedings are pending | After an order is passed (also transfers a pending case)
  [14:3:14.1] Nature | Preventive only | Preventive and curative
  [14:3:14.2] Effect | Stops the lower body | Quashes the order ("postmortem")
[14:3:15] - **Similarity**: both lie (traditionally) **only against judicial and quasi-judicial bodies**; both check excess of jurisdiction; both are issued by SC and HCs.
[14:3:16] ~~ Sources: NextGen Polity - APPSC Exams p.218-220; PMF Polity Notes Part 1 p.67-69 (UPSC 2022 Q with explanation p.72-73); Rocket Polity Part 1 p.111-112; Constitution of India, Art. 361
## SUB 4 | Clauses of Article 32 – What It Enforces
[14:4:0] TABLE head: Clause | Provision
  [14:4:0.0] 32(1) | Right to move the SC by appropriate proceedings for enforcement of Part III rights – itself guaranteed
  [14:4:0.1] 32(2) | SC may issue directions, orders or writs, including the five writs
  [14:4:0.2] 32(3) | Parliament may by law empower any other court to exercise these powers within its local limits – no such law made yet
  [14:4:0.3] 32(4) | The right shall not be suspended except as the Constitution provides (Art. 359)
[14:4:1] - **Only Fundamental Rights** can be enforced under Art. 32 – not constitutional, statutory or other rights. So the SC can **refuse relief** if **no Fundamental Right has been infringed** – not because another court can give relief, facts are disputed or the wrong writ was named.
[14:4:2] - **Right to property** is no longer an FR (**44th Amendment, 1978**; now **Art. 300A**) – its violation **cannot** be challenged by a **writ under Art. 32**, only under Art. 226, a civil suit etc.
[14:4:3] - Both SC and HCs share jurisdiction over **enforcement of Fundamental Rights** (Centre-State and inter-State disputes = SC only, Art. 131).
[14:4:4] ~~ Sources: NextGen Polity - APPSC Exams p.216-218; PMF Polity Notes Part 1 p.67-68; Rocket Polity Part 1 p.111
## SUB 5 | Suspension during Emergency; Articles 33-35
[14:5:0] - **Who suspends FRs? The President** – under **Art. 359**, during a National Emergency, by order suspending the right to move courts for named FRs (not Parliament, SC or PM). **Art. 358** suspends Art. 19 automatically (war/external aggression).
[14:5:1] - **Arts. 20 and 21 cannot be suspended** – added by the **44th Amendment (1978)** to undo **ADM Jabalpur v. Shivkant Shukla (1976)**, which had upheld suspension of habeas corpus/Art. 21 in the Emergency.
[14:5:2] - Limits on FRs are imposed by **Parliament** by law; the **Judiciary only judges their reasonableness** – "the power to limit FRs is with the Supreme Court" is false.
[14:5:3] - **Art. 33**: **Parliament** (not State legislatures) may restrict or abrogate FRs of the **armed forces, paramilitary/forces for public order (police), intelligence or counter-intelligence bodies** and persons in **telecommunication systems set up for those forces** – not people of the communication sector in general.
[14:5:4] - **Art. 34**: restrictions while **martial law** is in force; Parliament may **indemnify** acts done under it. Martial law is not defined in the Constitution.
[14:5:5] - **Art. 35**: **Parliament alone** legislates on matters under Arts. 16(3), 32(3), 33, 34 and **prescribes punishment for Part III offences** – a **State legislature cannot**.
[14:5:6] ~~ Sources: NextGen Polity - APPSC Exams p.218, p.223-224; PMF Polity Notes Part 1 p.69-70, p.129 (Arts. 358-359); Constitution of India, Arts. 33-35 (legislative.gov.in)
## SUB 6 | Public Interest Litigation
[14:6:0] - **No Article mentions PIL** – it is a judge-made tool (borrowed from the **USA**), filed under **Arts. 32 and 226**; PIL is **not itself an FR**.
[14:6:1] - PIL relaxes **locus standi** (only the aggrieved may sue): any **public-spirited person** may move the court for those unable to – giving the **poor and weak access to justice**, essential for the **rule of law** (A and R true, R explains A).
[14:6:2] - **Epistolary jurisdiction**: letters/postcards treated as writ petitions – **Sunil Batra** (prisoner's letter). **S.P. Gupta (1981)** – key PIL case; Justices **P.N. Bhagwati** and **V.R. Krishna Iyer**.
[14:6:3] - Judicial review lets the SC strike down laws that **violate the Fundamental Rights of Scheduled Tribes** and weaker sections; courts do not enact laws.

## SUB 2 | Part IV Basics – Articles 36-51, Welfare State and Aims
[15:2:0] # Where and what
[15:2:1] - **Part IV**, **Articles 36 to 51** (16 Articles) – not Part III (12-35, FRs), Part I (1-4), Part II (5-11), Part IVA (FDs).
[15:2:2] - **Article 36:** "State" has the **same meaning as in Part III** (Art. 12).
[15:2:3] - **Aim:** **social and economic democracy** (not political democracy, which FRs secure) and a **welfare state** – a **secular** welfare state, **not a religious State** and not a capitalist, police or communist State.
[15:2:4] - **Welfare state:** its ideal is enshrined in the **DPSPs** (UPSC 2015, 2020); "**The State is everywhere: it leaves hardly a gap**" describes a welfare State; it gives **special care to the welfare of weaker sections**.
[15:2:5] - **Economic justice:** in the **Preamble and the DPSPs** (UPSC 2013); **economic democracy** is envisaged through the DPSPs; **social justice** runs through the Preamble, FRs, DPSPs and the special provisions for Scheduled and Tribal Areas.
[15:2:6] - **Economic goals:** from the DPSPs flow two dominant goals of economic policy – **increasing national income** and **improving its distribution**.
[15:2:7] - **Nature:** **positive** and **expansive** (they widen the State's role; FRs are restrictive/negative); their spirit is welfare and **humanism** (APPSC key).
[15:2:8] > Asked as: "Which Part?" (IV – ~10 times), "Articles 36 to 51 cover…", "goal of DPSP" (welfare state), "not an objective" (religious State), "purpose" (social and economic democracy – UPSC 2002).

## SUB 3 | Article 37 – Non-justiciable, yet Fundamental in Governance
[15:3:0] # The text
[15:3:1] - **Article 37:** the provisions of Part IV "**shall not be enforced by any court**", but the principles are "**fundamental in the governance of the country**" and "it shall be the **duty of the State to apply these principles in making laws**".
[15:3:2] - So DPSPs are **non-justiciable** – not enforceable, no justiciable rights for individuals, **no legal duty** on the State; their sanction is **moral and political** (public opinion, elections).
[15:3:3] - They are **not limitations** on the legislative or executive function (UPSC 2017: "neither") – they are **guide-posts for legislation** and a **positive mandate** to the State, not prohibitions or "mere moral precepts".
[15:3:4] - "Fundamental in the governance of the country" = **DPSPs** (UPSC 2013) – not FRs or FDs.
[15:3:5] # FR vs DPSP
[15:3:6] TABLE head: Basis | Fundamental Rights | Directive Principles
  [15:3:6.0] Place | Part III, Arts. 12-35 | Part IV, Arts. 36-51
  [15:3:6.1] Courts | Justiciable | Non-justiciable (Art. 37)
  [15:3:6.2] Character | Negative – restrain the State | Positive – exhort the State to act
  [15:3:6.3] Aim | Political democracy | Social and economic democracy, welfare state
  [15:3:6.4] Sanction | Legal | Moral and political
  [15:3:6.5] Conflict | Generally prevail (Champakam Dorairajan 1951) | Subordinate; laws for 39(b), (c) protected by Art. 31C
[15:3:7] - **Minerva Mills (1980):** **harmony and balance** between Part III and Part IV is part of the **basic structure**.
[15:3:8] - **Kesavananda Bharati (1973):** Justices Shelat and Grover listed "the **mandate to build a welfare State contained in Part IV**" among basic features – the basis for NDA 2012's "all of the above".
[15:3:9] > A-R (APPSC G2): "DPSPs are not independently enforceable" + "they serve as guide-posts for legislation" → both true, R explains A (official key). Odd one out: "They impose a legal duty on the State" ✗; "directed at making India an advanced capitalist country" ✗.

## SUB 5 | Implementation – Panchayats, Legal Aid, UCC, Cow Protection
[15:5:0] # Article 40 – village panchayats

[15:5:3] # Article 39A – legal aid

[15:5:5] # Article 41 – work

[15:5:7] # Article 44 – Uniform Civil Code
[15:5:8] - **Goa** follows the **Portuguese Civil Code of 1867**; **Uttarakhand** – first State UCC (in force 27 Jan 2025, STs exempt); **Gujarat** – second (Gujarat UCC Bill, 2026: Scheduled Tribes exempted).

[15:5:10] - **2026:** Pavini Shukla v. Union of India (Jun 2026) – SC suggested a UCC to end gender discrimination; Mar 2026 – a 3-judge bench considered deferring to Parliament on Art. 44. Art. 44 is **not enforceable** in court.

=== NOTES 2:16 | Conflict and Harmony Between Fundamental Rights and Dpsp
## SUB 0 | Landmark Cases – Champakam to Minerva Mills
[16:0:0] TABLE head: Case | Year | Held
  [16:0:0.0] A.K. Gopalan v. State of Madras | 1950 | Preventive detention; narrow reading of Art. 21 (personal liberty) – not a property case
  [16:0:0.1] State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan | 1951 | First FR–DPSP conflict case: in a conflict FRs prevail; DPSPs "run as subsidiary" to Part III; communal G.O. struck down under Art. 29(2)
  [16:0:0.2] In re Kerala Education Bill | 1958 | Harmonious construction – give effect to both as far as possible (FRs still prevail in a direct clash)
  [16:0:0.3] Golaknath v. State of Punjab | 1967 | 11 judges; Parliament cannot amend/abridge FRs (FRs "transcendental and immutable"); prospective overruling – the first time the SC limited Parliament's amending power
  [16:0:0.4] Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala | 1973 | 13 judges (largest bench); FRs can be amended but not the basic structure
  [16:0:0.5] Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India | 1978 | Widened Art. 21 – procedure must be fair, just and reasonable
  [16:0:0.6] Minerva Mills v. Union of India | 1980 | Struck down Art. 368(4) and 368(5) and the 42nd Amendment's extension of Art. 31C; harmony and balance between FRs and DPSPs is part of the basic structure
  [16:0:0.7] Sanjeev Coke | 1983 | Art. 39(b) includes privately owned resources (decided after Minerva Mills)
[16:0:1] - **Chronology**: **Gopalan (1950) → Champakam (1951) → Golaknath (1967) → Kesavananda (1973) → Minerva Mills (1980)** → Sanjeev Coke (1983).
[16:0:2] - **Golaknath** (Subba Rao CJ): Fundamental Rights "**enable a man to chalk out his life in the manner he likes best**".
[16:0:3] - **Bench size**: Kesavananda **13** is the largest; Golaknath **11** is the usual "second largest" answer – but R.C. Cooper (Bank Nationalisation, 1970) and T.M.A. Pai (2002) also sat **11** judges; Minerva Mills had **5**.
[16:0:4] - **Property and amending power**: Golaknath, Kesavananda and Minerva Mills; **Gopalan** is about personal liberty.
[16:0:5] - **Champakam → 1st Amendment, 1951** inserted **Art. 15(4)** – the right changed was the **right against discrimination (Art. 15)**, overcoming the **Art. 29(2)** bar; 15(4) enables special provisions for **socially and educationally backward classes, SCs and STs**.

## SUB 1 | FRs vs DPSPs – Differences and Relationship
[16:1:0] TABLE head: Point | Fundamental Rights | DPSPs
  [16:1:0.0] Courts | Justiciable – law violating an FR is void under Art. 13 (to the extent of inconsistency) | Non-justiciable (Art. 37) – a law cannot be struck down merely for breaking a DPSP
  [16:1:0.1] Nature | Negative – restrain the State | Positive – directions to the State
  [16:1:0.2] Aim | Political democracy | Social and economic democracy (welfare State)
  [16:1:0.3] Sanction | Legal | Moral and political
  [16:1:0.4] Role | Means ("lay down the means") | Ends / goals (Justice Grover: "prescribe the goal")
[16:1:1] - **Art. 37**: DPSPs "shall not be enforced by any court", but are "**fundamental in the governance of the country**" and it is "**the duty of the State to apply these principles in making laws**".
[16:1:2] - By and large FRs and DPSPs are **complementary** (supplementary) to each other, not contradictory; DPSPs **supplement FRs** in achieving a welfare State. SC: DPSPs are the "**conscience of the Constitution**".
[16:1:3] - **Parliament can amend FRs to implement DPSPs**, provided the **basic structure** is not damaged – so "Parliament cannot amend FRs for implementing DPSPs" is the **wrong** statement.
[16:1:4] - **Who amends FRs**: **Parliament** under **Art. 368** (special majority).
[16:1:5] - The **Preamble** may be used to determine the ambit of both FRs and DPSPs.
[16:1:6] ~~ Sources: NextGen Polity - APPSC Exams p.246-248 (Art. 37, FR vs DPSP table); PMF Polity Notes Part 1 p.82-83; Rocket Polity Part 1 p.46
## SUB 2 | First Amendment, Article 31B and the Ninth Schedule
[16:2:0] - **1st Amendment, 1951** (PM **Jawaharlal Nehru**): added **Arts. 31A, 31B** and the **Ninth Schedule** for the **protection of agrarian (land) reforms in certain States**; also Art. 15(4).
[16:2:1] - **Art. 31B**: Acts in the Ninth Schedule are not void for inconsistency with Part III – **retrospective**; it is **still operative** (I.R. Coelho did not end it, only opened such laws to review).
[16:2:2] - Ninth Schedule protects **Central and State laws** from challenge on the ground of FRs; entries are **not limited to land reform** (grown to **over 280**); entries are added only by **Parliament through a constitutional amendment**, not by the Central Government.
[16:2:3] TABLE head: Case | Year | Rule
  [16:2:3.0] Waman Rao | 1981 | Laws added after 24 April 1973 (Kesavananda date) open to challenge
  [16:2:3.1] I.R. Coelho | 2007 | No blanket immunity – such laws tested on basic structure and Arts. 14, 19, 21 ("golden triangle")

## SUB 3 | Article 31C and the Amendments of 1971–1976
[16:3:0] TABLE head: Stage | What happened
  [16:3:0.0] 24th Amendment, 1971 | Answer to Golaknath: Parliament can amend any part including FRs; added Art. 13(4) and 368(3); President's assent to amendment bills made mandatory
  [16:3:0.1] 25th Amendment, 1971 | Answer to R.C. Cooper (Bank Nationalisation, 1970): "compensation" → "amount"; inserted Art. 31C – (1) laws for Art. 39(b) and (c) not void for violating Arts. 14, 19 (and 31); (2) a declaration that a law gives effect to that policy cannot be questioned in court
  [16:3:0.2] 26th Amendment, 1971 | Abolished privy purses and privileges of former rulers (answer to the Privy Purses case, 1970)
  [16:3:0.3] Kesavananda, 1973 | First provision of 31C upheld; second provision (bar on judicial review) struck down
  [16:3:0.4] 42nd Amendment, 1976 | Extended the first provision of 31C to laws implementing any DPSP – gave DPSPs primacy over Arts. 14 and 19
  [16:3:0.5] Minerva Mills, 1980 | Struck down that extension; 31C back to 39(b) and (c) only
[16:3:1] - **Primacy of DPSPs over FRs** = the **42nd Amendment** (the usual answer; the 25th gave only Art. 39(b)-(c) laws limited primacy; the 44th did not do this).
[16:3:2] - Earlier: **4th Amendment, 1955** – adequacy of compensation made non-justiciable; **17th, 1964** – widened "estate" in Art. 31A.
[16:3:3] - Minerva Mills also held that a **proclamation of Emergency is subject to judicial review**.
[16:3:4] - **Present position**: 31C protects only **39(b)-(c)** laws; **Property Owners Association (2024)** reaffirmed that the original 31C (as upheld in Kesavananda) continues.
[16:3:5] > APPSC G1 Prelims 2024: I (first provision struck in Kesavananda) – **wrong**, it was the second; II ("extension of the second provision") – **wrong**, the 42nd extended the first; III (Minerva = harmony) – correct; IV (present position = Champakam) – **wrong**. Incorrect = **I, II and IV**.

## SUB 4 | Harmonious Construction and Article 39(b)
[16:4:0] - **Harmonious construction**: read Part III and Part IV together so both operate – **In re Kerala Education Bill (1958)**, **Minerva Mills (1980)**.
[16:4:1] - **Minerva Mills**: the balance between Parts III and IV is part of the **basic structure**; absolute primacy to either destroys it; "FRs and DPSPs are **complementary**".
[16:4:2] - DPSPs strengthen FRs: **48A + Art. 21** = healthy environment; **45 + Art. 21** → **Art. 21A** (**86th Amendment, 2002**). Non-justiciability does **not** exclude DPSPs from interpretation.
[16:4:3] TABLE head: Case | Year | View on Art. 39(b)
  [16:4:3.0] Ranganatha Reddy | 1977 | Justice V.R. Krishna Iyer (minority): includes all privately owned resources
  [16:4:3.1] Sanjeev Coke | 1983 | Followed the Krishna Iyer view
  [16:4:3.2] Property Owners Association v. State of Maharashtra | 5 Nov 2024 | 9 judges, 7:2, CJI D.Y. Chandrachud: not every private resource is a "material resource of the community"
[16:4:4] - Test (2024): the resource's **nature, scarcity, effect on community well-being** and the consequences of its **concentration in private hands**.

=== NOTES 2:17 | Fundamental Duties – Article 51A, Swaran Singh Committee, Enforceability
## SUB 0 | Basics – Part IVA, Article 51A, 42nd and 86th Amendments
[17:0:0] - **Fundamental Duties = Part IVA**, which has **only one Article – Article 51A** (not Part IV, VI-A, III; not Arts. 49, 50, 51, 52). Opening words: "It shall be the duty of **every citizen of India**".
[17:0:1] - The **original Constitution had no Fundamental Duties** (not in the text completed in Nov 1949 or on 26 Jan 1950); they are a **separate Part**, **not part of the Fundamental Rights**.
[17:0:2] - Added by the **42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976** (Indira Gandhi govt; "Mini Constitution"), on the recommendation of the **Swaran Singh Committee**; in force **3 January 1977**. Not the 24th, 31st, 38th, 41st, 44th or 46th.
[17:0:3] - **Number**: **10** duties in 1976 (clauses a–j – the "**10 commandments**") → **11 at present**; the **11th duty, 51A(k)**, added by the **86th Amendment Act, 2002** (not 87th, 96th or "2001").
[17:0:4] TABLE head: Particular | 42nd CAA, 1976 | 86th CAA, 2002
  [17:0:4.0] Duties | Inserted Part IVA, Art. 51A, clauses (a)–(j) | Added 51A(k) – parent/guardian to give education to child 6–14 years
  [17:0:4.1] Same Act also | Added DPSP Art. 48A; moved forests and protection of wild animals and birds to the Concurrent List (Entries 17A, 17B) | Inserted Art. 21A (Right to Education as an FR); substituted Art. 45
[17:0:5] - **51A(g)** (environment) came in 1976, after the **1972 Stockholm Conference**.
[17:0:6] - Part IVA is one of **four Parts added after 1950**: **IVA, IXA, IXB, XIVA**. Other Parts: **Part VI = The States**; **Part IXA = The Municipalities** (Part XI is Centre–State relations).
[17:0:7] - Number game: **8** suggested (Swaran Singh) – **10** added (1976) – **11** now (2002).

## SUB 4 | Nature, Enforceability, Source and Criticism
[17:4:0] - **Non-justiciable** (like DPSPs): **cannot be enforced by writs**; the Constitution gives **no legal sanction** for violation – **only FRs are justiciable** (enforceable in courts; not DPSPs, the Preamble or Duties).
[17:4:1] - **Parliament may enforce them by legislation**; courts use them to **interpret ambiguous laws**, test **constitutionality** and judge the **reasonableness** of a law restricting an FR.
[17:4:2] - **UPSC 2017**: "a legislative process has been provided to enforce them" and "they are correlative to legal duties" – **both false**.
[17:4:3] - **Citizens only** – not foreigners (no exception, not even 51A(i)); FRs such as Arts. 14, 20, 21, 25 extend to foreigners too. **51A(k)** is the only duty addressed to **parents/guardians**.
[17:4:4] - **Source: Constitution of the former USSR** (Russian); among major democracies only **Japan** has a comparable list. India was **not** the first country to adopt duties.
[17:4:5] # Justice J.S. Verma Committee (1999) – laws already giving effect to duties
[17:4:6] TABLE head: Law | Duty
  [17:4:6.0] Prevention of Insults to National Honour Act, 1971 – insult to Flag/Constitution: imprisonment up to 3 years, or fine, or both (Sec. 2) | 51A(a) – Constitution, Flag, Anthem
  [17:4:6.1] Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955 | 51A(e) – harmony
  [17:4:6.2] Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act, 1967 | 51A(c) – sovereignty and integrity
  [17:4:6.3] Wild Life (Protection) Act, 1972; Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980 | 51A(g) – environment
  [17:4:6.4] Representation of the People Act, 1951 | Corrupt practices on religion / enmity
[17:4:7] - The Verma Committee was set up to **operationalise** duties and **identified existing legal provisions** – it did not ask for implementation "irrespective of" them.
[17:4:8] - Imputations prejudicial to national integration (51A(c)): now **Sec. 197, BNS 2023** (earlier IPC 153B). **Environment (Protection) Act, 1986** rests on **Art. 253**.
[17:4:9] # Criticism and significance
[17:4:10] - Criticism: **not exhaustive** (omits voting, taxes, family planning); **vague** ("noble ideals", "scientific temper"); a "**code of moral precepts**" (non-justiciable); **superfluous**; an **appendage to Part IV** instead of following Part III.
[17:4:11] - Significance: reminder of obligations; warning against anti-national acts; **help courts on reasonableness**; promote discipline and participation.

## SUB 5 | Rights and Duties – Correlation and Cases
[17:5:0] - **Rights are correlative with duties** (UPSC 2017) – every right implies a duty; **Holland**: others' forbearance from interfering with a right is called a duty. "**Rights without duties are like men without shadows**."
[17:5:1] - Example: **Art. 19(1)(a)** (free speech) goes with **51A(c)** (sovereignty, unity, integrity).

=== NOTES 2:18 | Amendment of the Constitution – Article 368, Majorities, Procedure, Limitations
## SUB 0 | Article 368 – Where It Is, What It Says, Nature of the Procedure
[18:0:0] - **Article 368**, the **only Article of Part XX**, gives Parliament the **power to amend the Constitution and the procedure** for it (not Arts. 14, 320, 330, 352, 356, 360, 366, 367, 369, 370 or 395).
[18:0:1] - Wording: Parliament may, "in exercise of its **constituent power**, amend by way of **addition, variation or repeal** any provision of this Constitution in accordance with the procedure laid down" – subject to the **basic structure** (Kesavananda Bharati, 1973).
[18:0:2] - **Art. 368 itself provides for two types** of amendment – **special majority**, and **special majority + ratification by half the States**. The **simple-majority** changes (Arts. 2-4, 169 etc.) lie **outside Art. 368**, so books also speak of "three ways" of amending the Constitution.
[18:0:3] - **Art. 13(4)** (added by the **24th Amendment, 1971**): an amendment made under Art. 368 is **not "law"** under Art. 13, so **Art. 13(2) does not apply** to it (it still applies to laws of Parliament, ordinances and executive orders).
[18:0:4] - Art. 368 has itself been amended by the **24th (1971)** and the **101st (2016**, GST Council Art. 279A added to the ratification list) Amendments – both statements true (MPPSC 2020).
[18:0:5] - Borrowed from **South Africa** (with the election of Rajya Sabha members).
[18:0:6] - Nature: **neither rigid nor flexible but a synthesis of both** – "partly rigid, partly flexible". Dual nature shown by: some provisions need a **special majority**, some only a **simple majority** like ordinary laws, and the **federal structure cannot be altered easily** (State ratification). FRs are **not** amendable "without judicial review".
[18:0:7] - **K.C. Wheare**: the amending process strikes a **good balance between rigidity and flexibility** ("variety in the amending process is wise but rarely found"); he called the Constitution **quasi-federal**.
[18:0:8] - **Granville Austin**: the process "appears **complicated**" but is "**merely diverse**" – one of the most ably conceived aspects of the Constitution.
[18:0:9] - Latest enacted amendment (as of Nov 2026): the **106th (2023)**, women's reservation; the 129th and 130th are pending Bills and the 131st Bill was defeated (Apr 2026).
[18:0:10] > Exam angle: "Which Article/Part deals with amendment?" is the single most repeated PYQ – Art. 368, Part XX.
[18:0:11] ~~ Sources: NextGen Polity - APPSC Exams p.161, p.256 (src/polity/nextgen-polity-full.txt); PMF Polity Notes Part 1 p.92-95; Rocket Polity Part 1 p.53-55; Constitution of India Arts. 13(4), 368
## SUB 1 | Procedure under Article 368 – Step by Step
[18:1:0] - 1. Initiated **only by a Bill in either House of Parliament** (Lok Sabha **or** Rajya Sabha) – **not** in a State Legislature and **not** by the President.
[18:1:1] - 2. Moved by a **minister or a private member**; **no prior permission/recommendation of the President**.
[18:1:2] - 3. Passed in **each House separately** by a **special majority** – a majority of the **total membership** and **two-thirds of members present and voting**.
[18:1:3] - 4. **No joint sitting** (Art. 108 does not apply): if either House rejects it, the Bill **fails**.
[18:1:4] - 5. If it touches a **federal provision**, it must also be **ratified by the legislatures of half the States** by a **simple majority** (not all States).
[18:1:5] - 6. The **President must give assent** – he can neither withhold assent nor return the Bill; made obligatory by the **24th Amendment, 1971** (368(2) "**shall give his assent**").
[18:1:6] - So "prior recommendation of the President" is **false**, "President must assent" is **true**, "special majority in both Houses, no joint sitting" is **true** (UPSC 2022). "Initiated in Lok Sabha only" and "ratified by all the States" are **false** (UPSC 2013).

  [18:1:7.0] Articles | 107, 108 | 109, 110 | 368
  [18:1:7.1] Origin | Either House | Lok Sabha only | Either House
  [18:1:7.2] President's prior recommendation | Not required | Required | Not required
  [18:1:7.3] Majority | Simple | Simple | Special, in each House separately
  [18:1:7.4] Deadlock | Joint sitting (Art. 108) | No joint sitting (LS prevails) | No joint sitting – Bill fails
  [18:1:7.5] President | Assent, withhold or return once | Assent or withhold, never return | Must assent
[18:1:8] - **Financial Bill (I)**: starts only in Lok Sabha on the President's recommendation, but the Rajya Sabha **may reject or amend** it.

## SUB 3 | Three Kinds of Amendment and the Simple-Majority Provisions
[18:3:0] TABLE head: Route | Requirement | Examples

## SUB 5 | Limits on the Amending Power – FRs and the Basic Structure
[18:5:0] TABLE head: Case / Act | Year | Held
  [18:5:0.0] Shankari Prasad | 1951 | Parliament can amend FRs; an amendment is not "law" under Art. 13(2); upheld the 1st Amendment
  [18:5:0.1] Sajjan Singh | 1965 | Reaffirmed Shankari Prasad
  [18:5:0.2] Golaknath | 1967 | Parliament cannot abridge FRs; an amendment is law – first to limit the power
  [18:5:0.3] 24th Amendment | 1971 | Parliament can amend any part incl. FRs; Art. 13(4), 368(3); assent obligatory
