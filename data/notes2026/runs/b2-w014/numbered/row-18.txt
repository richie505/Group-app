## SUB 0 | Article 368 – Where It Is, What It Says, Nature of the Procedure
[0:0] - **Article 368**, the **only Article of Part XX**, gives Parliament the **power to amend the Constitution and the procedure** for it (not Arts. 14, 320, 330, 352, 356, 360, 366, 367, 369, 370 or 395).
[0:1] - Wording: Parliament may, "in exercise of its **constituent power**, amend by way of **addition, variation or repeal** any provision of this Constitution in accordance with the procedure laid down" – subject to the **basic structure** (Kesavananda Bharati, 1973).
[0:2] - **Art. 368 itself provides for two types** of amendment – **special majority**, and **special majority + ratification by half the States**. The **simple-majority** changes (Arts. 2-4, 169 etc.) lie **outside Art. 368**, so books also speak of "three ways" of amending the Constitution.
[0:3] - **Art. 13(4)** (added by the **24th Amendment, 1971**): an amendment made under Art. 368 is **not "law"** under Art. 13, so **Art. 13(2) does not apply** to it (it still applies to laws of Parliament, ordinances and executive orders).
[0:4] - Art. 368 has itself been amended by the **24th (1971)** and the **101st (2016**, GST Council Art. 279A added to the ratification list) Amendments – both statements true (MPPSC 2020).
[0:5] - Borrowed from **South Africa** (with the election of Rajya Sabha members).
[0:6] - Nature: **neither rigid nor flexible but a synthesis of both** – "partly rigid, partly flexible". Dual nature shown by: some provisions need a **special majority**, some only a **simple majority** like ordinary laws, and the **federal structure cannot be altered easily** (State ratification). FRs are **not** amendable "without judicial review".
[0:7] - **K.C. Wheare**: the amending process strikes a **good balance between rigidity and flexibility** ("variety in the amending process is wise but rarely found"); he called the Constitution **quasi-federal**.
[0:8] - **Granville Austin**: the process "appears **complicated**" but is "**merely diverse**" – one of the most ably conceived aspects of the Constitution.
[0:9] - Latest enacted amendment (as of Nov 2026): the **106th (2023)**, women's reservation; the 129th and 130th are pending Bills and the 131st Bill was defeated (Apr 2026).
[0:10] > Exam angle: "Which Article/Part deals with amendment?" is the single most repeated PYQ – Art. 368, Part XX.

## SUB 1 | Procedure under Article 368 – Step by Step
[1:0] - 1. Initiated **only by a Bill in either House of Parliament** (Lok Sabha **or** Rajya Sabha) – **not** in a State Legislature and **not** by the President.
[1:1] - 2. Moved by a **minister or a private member**; **no prior permission/recommendation of the President**.
[1:2] - 3. Passed in **each House separately** by a **special majority** – a majority of the **total membership** and **two-thirds of members present and voting**.
[1:3] - 4. **No joint sitting** (Art. 108 does not apply): if either House rejects it, the Bill **fails**.
[1:4] - 5. If it touches a **federal provision**, it must also be **ratified by the legislatures of half the States** by a **simple majority** (not all States).
[1:5] - 6. The **President must give assent** – he can neither withhold assent nor return the Bill; made obligatory by the **24th Amendment, 1971** (368(2) "**shall give his assent**").
[1:6] - So "prior recommendation of the President" is **false**, "President must assent" is **true**, "special majority in both Houses, no joint sitting" is **true** (UPSC 2022). "Initiated in Lok Sabha only" and "ratified by all the States" are **false** (UPSC 2013).
[1:7] TABLE head: Basis | Ordinary Bill | Money Bill | Constitution Amendment Bill
  [1:7.0] Articles | 107, 108 | 109, 110 | 368
  [1:7.1] Origin | Either House | Lok Sabha only | Either House
  [1:7.2] President's prior recommendation | Not required | Required | Not required
  [1:7.3] Majority | Simple | Simple | Special, in each House separately
  [1:7.4] Deadlock | Joint sitting (Art. 108) | No joint sitting (LS prevails) | No joint sitting – Bill fails
  [1:7.5] President | Assent, withhold or return once | Assent or withhold, never return | Must assent
[1:8] - **Financial Bill (I)**: starts only in Lok Sabha on the President's recommendation, but the Rajya Sabha **may reject or amend** it.

## SUB 2 | Three Kinds of Amendment and the Simple-Majority Provisions
[2:0] TABLE head: Route | Requirement | Examples
  [2:0.0] Simple majority of Parliament (outside Art. 368) | Majority of members present and voting; such laws are not deemed amendments under Art. 368 | Arts. 2, 3, 4; 169; citizenship; Fifth and Sixth Schedules; quorum
  [2:0.1] Special majority of Parliament | Majority of total membership + two-thirds present and voting, each House | Fundamental Rights (incl. Art. 32), DPSPs, all provisions not in the other two groups
  [2:0.2] Special majority + ratification by half the States | As above + simple majority in half the State legislatures | Federal provisions (next sub-section)
[2:1] - **New States** (admission/establishment, Art. 2), **formation of new States and alteration of areas, boundaries or names** (Art. 3) – by a **simple majority**, ordinary law; **Art. 4(2)**: such laws (and the consequent changes to the **First and Fourth Schedules**) are **not amendments for the purpose of Art. 368**. No State ratification is needed.
[2:2] - **Art. 3 proviso**: the Bill needs the **President's recommendation** and is **referred to the State legislature** for its views – so "boundaries may be altered without intimation to the States" is **false**.
[2:3] - **Legislative Council** (Art. 169): the **State Assembly passes a resolution by special majority** (majority of total membership + two-thirds present and voting), **followed by an Act of Parliament** by simple majority – not an Art. 368 amendment.
[2:4] - Other simple-majority matters: **acquisition and termination of citizenship**; **Second Schedule** emoluments; **quorum**; MPs' salaries; rules of procedure; privileges; use of English in Parliament; **number of puisne judges of the SC** and more SC jurisdiction; **official language**; elections and **delimitation**; **Union Territories**; **Fifth Schedule** and **Sixth Schedule**.
[2:5] - **Article 32** is amended by a **special majority ("two-thirds")**, **without** State ratification (APPSC G2 2017) – though the SC's power under it is part of the basic structure.
[2:6] - Provisions that **cannot** be amended by simple majority and need **no** ratification → **special majority** (not ordinances, Cabinet decisions or Presidential orders).
[2:7] ~~ Sources: NextGen Polity - APPSC Exams p.256-257 (src/polity/nextgen-polity-full.txt); PMF Polity Notes Part 1 p.92; Constitution of India Arts. 3, 4, 169
## SUB 3 | Federal Provisions Needing Ratification by Half the States
[3:0] - Special majority **plus** ratification by the legislatures of **not less than one-half of the States**, by **simple majority**:
[3:1] TABLE head: Provision | Articles / Schedule
  [3:1.0] Election of the President and its manner | 54, 55
  [3:1.1] Extent of the executive power of the Union and the States | 73, 162
  [3:1.2] Supreme Court and High Courts | Part V ch. IV, Part VI ch. V, 241
  [3:1.3] Distribution of legislative powers between the Union and States | Part XI ch. I
  [3:1.4] Any of the Lists in the Seventh Schedule (e.g. the Union List) | Seventh Schedule
  [3:1.5] Representation of States in Parliament | Fourth Schedule
  [3:1.6] GST Council (added by the 101st Amendment, 2016) | 279A
  [3:1.7] Article 368 itself | 368
[3:2] - **Not** in the list: **qualifications, tenure, conditions of office of the President**; **conditions of the Governor's office**; Fundamental Rights; DPSPs; formation of new States; abolition of Legislative Councils; creation of UTs.
[3:3] - So for **I. Union List, II. extent of a State's executive power, III. conditions of the Governor's office**, only **I and II** need ratification (UPSC 2025).
[3:4] - **Not all States**: half the States are enough – it is **not necessary that all State legislatures act** on the Bill; **no time limit** for ratification.
[3:5] - **First amendment ratified by the States: the 3rd Amendment (1954)** – re-enacted **Entry 33 of the Concurrent List** (trade and commerce in essential commodities).
[3:6] - **Rajendra N. Shah (2021)**: Part IXB (97th, co-operatives) **struck down for State co-operatives** for **want of ratification**.

## SUB 4 | Majorities in Parliament
[4:0] TABLE head: Majority | Meaning | Used for
  [4:0.0] Simple | Majority of members present and voting | Ordinary, Money and Financial Bills; confidence motions; Arts. 2-4, 169 laws
  [4:0.1] Absolute | Majority of the total membership | Never alone – always with another majority
  [4:0.2] Effective | Majority of the House's effective strength (total minus vacancies) | Removal of the Vice-President (RS), Deputy Chairman, Speaker/Deputy Speaker
  [4:0.3] Special – Art. 249 type | Two-thirds of members present and voting, Rajya Sabha only | Parliament legislating on a State List subject in the national interest (Art. 249); new All-India Services (Art. 312)
  [4:0.4] Special – Art. 368 type | Majority of total membership + two-thirds present and voting, each House | Amendment; removal of SC/HC judges (124, 217), CAG (148), CEC (324), SEC (243K); approval of National Emergency (352)
  [4:0.5] Special – Art. 61 type | Two-thirds of the total membership of the House | Impeachment of the President – the strictest
[4:1] - **High Court judges** (incl. the CJ) are removed by the **President** after an **address by Parliament** passed by a special majority (total membership + two-thirds present and voting) in each House – not by the Governor or the CJI.
[4:2] - Worked example (2026): the 131st Amendment Bill got **298 for, 230 against**; two-thirds of **528 present and voting = 352**, so it **failed**.

## SUB 5 | Limits on the Amending Power – FRs and the Basic Structure
[5:0] TABLE head: Case / Act | Year | Held
  [5:0.0] Shankari Prasad | 1951 | Parliament can amend FRs; an amendment is not "law" under Art. 13(2); upheld the 1st Amendment
  [5:0.1] Sajjan Singh | 1965 | Reaffirmed Shankari Prasad
  [5:0.2] Golaknath | 1967 | Parliament cannot abridge FRs; an amendment is law – first to limit the power
  [5:0.3] 24th Amendment | 1971 | Parliament can amend any part incl. FRs; Art. 13(4), 368(3); assent obligatory
  [5:0.4] Kesavananda Bharati | 24 April 1973 | 13 judges, 7:6; FRs amendable but not the basic structure; upheld the 24th; overruled Golaknath
  [5:0.5] Indira Gandhi v. Raj Narain | 1975 | Doctrine first applied to strike down an amendment clause (39th)
  [5:0.6] 42nd Amendment | 1976 | Added 368(4), (5): no limit on constituent power, no judicial review
  [5:0.7] Minerva Mills | 1980 | Struck down 368(4), (5); limited amending power is itself a basic feature
  [5:0.8] Waman Rao | 1981 | Doctrine applies to amendments after 24 April 1973
[5:1] - So the power of Parliament to amend is **limited by the Constitution itself** (its basic structure); the "**unquestioned right of Parliament to amend any part**" is **not** a basic feature – FRs, independence of the judiciary and federalism are.
[5:2] - **Who amends FRs?** **Parliament** (special majority) – not the Lok Sabha or Rajya Sabha alone, and not the Supreme Court, which only reviews.
[5:3] - Sequence: **Shankari Prasad → Golaknath → 24th Amendment → Kesavananda**.
[5:4] ~~ Sources: NextGen Polity - APPSC Exams p.258-262 (src/polity/nextgen-polity-full.txt); PMF Polity Notes Part 1 p.93-94
## SUB 6 | Criticism of the Procedure and Amendment Bills in News
[6:0] - **No special body** (convention, as in the USA) and **no referendum**; constituent power lies with **Parliament**.
[6:1] - **States cannot initiate** an amendment (only a resolution for a Legislative Council under Art. 169); their role is **only to ratify** certain Bills; most of the Constitution is amended by Parliament alone.
[6:2] - **No time limit** for ratification; silent on **withdrawal** of ratification; **no joint sitting**; provisions **too sketchy**.
