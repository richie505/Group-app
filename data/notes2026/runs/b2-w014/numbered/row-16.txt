## SUB 0 | Landmark Cases – Champakam to Minerva Mills
[0:0] TABLE head: Case | Year | Held
  [0:0.0] A.K. Gopalan v. State of Madras | 1950 | Preventive detention; narrow reading of Art. 21 (personal liberty) – not a property case
  [0:0.1] State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan | 1951 | First FR–DPSP conflict case: in a conflict FRs prevail; DPSPs "run as subsidiary" to Part III; communal G.O. struck down under Art. 29(2)
  [0:0.2] In re Kerala Education Bill | 1958 | Harmonious construction – give effect to both as far as possible (FRs still prevail in a direct clash)
  [0:0.3] Golaknath v. State of Punjab | 1967 | 11 judges; Parliament cannot amend/abridge FRs (FRs "transcendental and immutable"); prospective overruling – the first time the SC limited Parliament's amending power
  [0:0.4] Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala | 1973 | 13 judges (largest bench); FRs can be amended but not the basic structure
  [0:0.5] Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India | 1978 | Widened Art. 21 – procedure must be fair, just and reasonable
  [0:0.6] Minerva Mills v. Union of India | 1980 | Struck down Art. 368(4) and 368(5) and the 42nd Amendment's extension of Art. 31C; harmony and balance between FRs and DPSPs is part of the basic structure
  [0:0.7] Sanjeev Coke | 1983 | Art. 39(b) includes privately owned resources (decided after Minerva Mills)

[0:2] - **Golaknath** (Subba Rao CJ): Fundamental Rights "**enable a man to chalk out his life in the manner he likes best**".
[0:3] - **Bench size**: Kesavananda **13** is the largest; Golaknath **11** is the usual "second largest" answer – but R.C. Cooper (Bank Nationalisation, 1970) and T.M.A. Pai (2002) also sat **11** judges; Minerva Mills had **5**.
[0:4] - **Property and amending power**: Golaknath, Kesavananda and Minerva Mills; **Gopalan** is about personal liberty.
[0:5] - **Champakam → 1st Amendment, 1951** inserted **Art. 15(4)** – the right changed was the **right against discrimination (Art. 15)**, overcoming the **Art. 29(2)** bar; 15(4) enables special provisions for **socially and educationally backward classes, SCs and STs**.

## SUB 1 | FRs vs DPSPs – Differences and Relationship
[1:0] TABLE head: Point | Fundamental Rights | DPSPs
  [1:0.0] Courts | Justiciable – law violating an FR is void under Art. 13 (to the extent of inconsistency) | Non-justiciable (Art. 37) – a law cannot be struck down merely for breaking a DPSP
  [1:0.1] Nature | Negative – restrain the State | Positive – directions to the State
  [1:0.2] Aim | Political democracy | Social and economic democracy (welfare State)
  [1:0.3] Sanction | Legal | Moral and political
  [1:0.4] Role | Means ("lay down the means") | Ends / goals (Justice Grover: "prescribe the goal")
[1:1] - **Art. 37**: DPSPs "shall not be enforced by any court", but are "**fundamental in the governance of the country**" and it is "**the duty of the State to apply these principles in making laws**".
[1:2] - By and large FRs and DPSPs are **complementary** (supplementary) to each other, not contradictory; DPSPs **supplement FRs** in achieving a welfare State. SC: DPSPs are the "**conscience of the Constitution**".
[1:3] - **Parliament can amend FRs to implement DPSPs**, provided the **basic structure** is not damaged – so "Parliament cannot amend FRs for implementing DPSPs" is the **wrong** statement.
[1:4] - **Who amends FRs**: **Parliament** under **Art. 368** (special majority).
[1:5] - The **Preamble** may be used to determine the ambit of both FRs and DPSPs.
[1:6] ~~ Sources: NextGen Polity - APPSC Exams p.246-248 (Art. 37, FR vs DPSP table); PMF Polity Notes Part 1 p.82-83; Rocket Polity Part 1 p.46
## SUB 2 | First Amendment, Article 31B and the Ninth Schedule
[2:0] - **1st Amendment, 1951** (PM **Jawaharlal Nehru**): added **Arts. 31A, 31B** and the **Ninth Schedule** for the **protection of agrarian (land) reforms in certain States**; also Art. 15(4).
[2:1] - **Art. 31B**: Acts in the Ninth Schedule are not void for inconsistency with Part III – **retrospective**; it is **still operative** (I.R. Coelho did not end it, only opened such laws to review).
[2:2] - Ninth Schedule protects **Central and State laws** from challenge on the ground of FRs; entries are **not limited to land reform** (grown to **over 280**); entries are added only by **Parliament through a constitutional amendment**, not by the Central Government.
[2:3] TABLE head: Case | Year | Rule
  [2:3.0] Waman Rao | 1981 | Laws added after 24 April 1973 (Kesavananda date) open to challenge
  [2:3.1] I.R. Coelho | 2007 | No blanket immunity – such laws tested on basic structure and Arts. 14, 19, 21 ("golden triangle")
[2:4] > APPSC G1 Prelims 2024: Ninth Schedule – only statements I (protects Central and State laws) and II (not only land reform) are correct.
[2:5] ~~ Sources: NextGen Polity - APPSC Exams p.217 (Arts. 31A-31C table), p.258; PMF Polity Notes Part 1 p.82; UPSC CSE 2019 (Nehru); Constitution of India Art. 31B
## SUB 3 | Article 31C and the Amendments of 1971–1976
[3:0] TABLE head: Stage | What happened
  [3:0.0] 24th Amendment, 1971 | Answer to Golaknath: Parliament can amend any part including FRs; added Art. 13(4) and 368(3); President's assent to amendment bills made mandatory
  [3:0.1] 25th Amendment, 1971 | Answer to R.C. Cooper (Bank Nationalisation, 1970): "compensation" → "amount"; inserted Art. 31C – (1) laws for Art. 39(b) and (c) not void for violating Arts. 14, 19 (and 31); (2) a declaration that a law gives effect to that policy cannot be questioned in court
  [3:0.2] 26th Amendment, 1971 | Abolished privy purses and privileges of former rulers (answer to the Privy Purses case, 1970)
  [3:0.3] Kesavananda, 1973 | First provision of 31C upheld; second provision (bar on judicial review) struck down
  [3:0.4] 42nd Amendment, 1976 | Extended the first provision of 31C to laws implementing any DPSP – gave DPSPs primacy over Arts. 14 and 19
  [3:0.5] Minerva Mills, 1980 | Struck down that extension; 31C back to 39(b) and (c) only
[3:1] - **Primacy of DPSPs over FRs** = the **42nd Amendment** (the usual answer; the 25th gave only Art. 39(b)-(c) laws limited primacy; the 44th did not do this).
[3:2] - Earlier: **4th Amendment, 1955** – adequacy of compensation made non-justiciable; **17th, 1964** – widened "estate" in Art. 31A.
[3:3] - Minerva Mills also held that a **proclamation of Emergency is subject to judicial review**.
[3:4] - **Present position**: 31C protects only **39(b)-(c)** laws; **Property Owners Association (2024)** reaffirmed that the original 31C (as upheld in Kesavananda) continues.
[3:5] > APPSC G1 Prelims 2024: I (first provision struck in Kesavananda) – **wrong**, it was the second; II ("extension of the second provision") – **wrong**, the 42nd extended the first; III (Minerva = harmony) – correct; IV (present position = Champakam) – **wrong**. Incorrect = **I, II and IV**.
[3:6] ~~ Sources: NextGen Polity - APPSC Exams p.217, p.258-262; PMF Polity Notes Part 1 p.82-83; Rocket Polity Part 1 p.59
## SUB 4 | Harmonious Construction and Article 39(b)
[4:0] - **Harmonious construction**: read Part III and Part IV together so both operate – **In re Kerala Education Bill (1958)**, **Minerva Mills (1980)**.
[4:1] - **Minerva Mills**: the balance between Parts III and IV is part of the **basic structure**; absolute primacy to either destroys it; "FRs and DPSPs are **complementary**".
[4:2] - DPSPs strengthen FRs: **48A + Art. 21** = healthy environment; **45 + Art. 21** → **Art. 21A** (**86th Amendment, 2002**). Non-justiciability does **not** exclude DPSPs from interpretation.
[4:3] TABLE head: Case | Year | View on Art. 39(b)
  [4:3.0] Ranganatha Reddy | 1977 | Justice V.R. Krishna Iyer (minority): includes all privately owned resources
  [4:3.1] Sanjeev Coke | 1983 | Followed the Krishna Iyer view
  [4:3.2] Property Owners Association v. State of Maharashtra | 5 Nov 2024 | 9 judges, 7:2, CJI D.Y. Chandrachud: not every private resource is a "material resource of the community"
[4:4] - Test (2024): the resource's **nature, scarcity, effect on community well-being** and the consequences of its **concentration in private hands**.
