=== NOTES 4:11 | Weathering, Mass Wasting and Soil Formation; Denudation and the Cycle of Erosion
## SUB 0 | Weathering – Physical, Chemical and Biological
[11:0:0] - **Weathering:** the natural **breaking down and decay of rocks in situ** (at or near the surface) into fragments, clays and dissolved matter; **no transport** is involved. Erosion then carries the loosened material away.
[11:0:1] - **Geomorphic processes:** the processes that shape the Earth's surface – weathering, mass wasting, erosion, deposition (endogenic forces build relief, exogenic forces wear it down).
[11:0:2] TABLE head: Type | Process | What happens / example
  [11:0:2.0] Physical (mechanical) | Exfoliation, frost wedging (freeze-thaw), salt-crystal growth, block disintegration | Breaks rock without chemical change; driven mainly by temperature variation – typical of hot deserts and cold mountains
  [11:0:2.1] Chemical | Solution | Soluble minerals (rock salt, gypsum) dissolve
  [11:0:2.2] Chemical | Carbonation | CO₂ in rain forms carbonic acid, dissolves limestone (karst) and stores carbon as bicarbonate
  [11:0:2.3] Chemical | Oxidation | Oxygen dissolved in water reacts with iron minerals – rusting → red/yellow soils
  [11:0:2.4] Chemical | Hydration | Minerals absorb water and swell: anhydrite → gypsum
  [11:0:2.5] Chemical | Hydrolysis | Water (H⁺/hydronium) reacts with silicates: feldspar → clay; muscovite → vermiculite when interlayer potassium is replaced by hydronium (APPSC FRO)
  [11:0:2.6] Biological | Living things | Plant roots widen cracks; burrowing animals (earthworms, termites, rodents); lichens release acids
[11:0:3] - **Climate control:** chemical weathering is **fastest in hot and humid (wet)** climates – heat and abundant water speed up reactions (tropics); it is slowest in cold or dry climates.
[11:0:4] - **Rain as a weathering agent (UPSC 2024):** rainwater holds **CO₂ in solution** (→ carbonic acid, carbonation) **and atmospheric oxygen** (→ oxidation); **both** are correct and **both explain** why rainfall weathers rocks.
[11:0:5] - **NDA A–R:** "decay and disintegration of rock in situ is weathering" (true) and "mechanical weathering is mainly caused by temperature variation" (true) – but R does **not** explain A.
[11:0:6] - Exfoliation is **physical**, not chemical; hydration, oxidation, solution, carbonation, hydrolysis are chemical. Oxidation is weathering, **not mass wasting**.
[11:0:7] TABLE head: Weathering landform | Meaning
  [11:0:7.0] Exfoliation dome | Onion-like peeling of granite (unloading or heating-cooling)
  [11:0:7.1] Tor | Residual granite blocks standing above the weathered surface where joints are widely spaced
  [11:0:7.2] Block disintegration | Physical break-up of jointed rock into blocks
[11:0:8] - **AP:** granite/gneiss weathers to **red soil**; basalt to **black soil**. Glacier-fed Himalayan catchments release CO₂ through **pyrite oxidation** (app CA, Sep 2026). Freeze-thaw breaks rocks in the high Himalaya.
[11:0:9] > Most-asked: hot-humid = fastest chemical weathering; rain = CO₂ + O₂; exfoliation = physical; muscovite → vermiculite = hydrolysis.

## SUB 1 | Soil Texture, Water-holding and Soil Properties
[11:1:0] TABLE head: Particle | Diameter (USDA)
  [11:1:0.0] Sand (fine sand) | 0.05–2 mm
  [11:1:0.1] Silt | 0.002–0.05 mm
  [11:1:0.2] Clay | Less than 0.002 mm
[11:1:1] - **Water-holding capacity:** **clay > silt > sand** – the smaller the particle, the more water held; sand drains fastest.
[11:1:2] - **Clay** holds the **maximum** water, but its pores are **small, not large** (high total porosity, tiny pores). So in the UPPSC A–R: A true, **R false**.
[11:1:3] - **Loam:** contains **all three** particle types in roughly balanced proportions ("equal sand, silt and clay" = loamy soil); a typical loam ≈ **40% sand, 42% silt, 18% clay**; best for farming.
[11:1:4] - Clay binds organic carbon; sandy soils lose it by leaching (app CA, Nov 2025).
[11:1:5] - **Rainforest soils are poor** despite lush forest: heavy rain **leaches** nutrients and dead matter decomposes rapidly.
[11:1:6] - **UPSC 2018 (agricultural soils):** high organic matter **raises** (not reduces) water-holding capacity; soil **does** take part in the **sulphur cycle**; **long irrigation can cause salinisation** – only the third statement is correct.
[11:1:7] > Clay < 0.002 mm (asked twice); clay > silt > sand.

## SUB 2 | Soil Formation – Pedology, Factors and Profile
[11:2:0] - **Pedology** = the study of soils in their natural setting (soil science); **petrology** = study of rocks; geology = study of the Earth.
[11:2:1] - **Five classical factors:** **parent material, climate, relief/topography, organisms (biological activity), time** – **latitude is NOT** one.
[11:2:2] TABLE head: Active factors | Passive factors
  [11:2:2.0] Climate, biological activity (vegetation, micro-organisms) – supply energy and drive weathering and organic processes | Parent material, topography (relief), time
[11:2:3] - **APPSC G1:** active factors = climate and biological activity (**III and IV only**).
[11:2:4] - Soils are **surveyed and classified** on the basis of the distinctive characteristics of their **soil horizons** (the soil profile).
[11:2:5] TABLE head: Horizon | Character
  [11:2:5.0] O | Surface organic layer of decaying plant matter
  [11:2:5.1] A | Topsoil: minerals + organic matter; most plant roots
  [11:2:5.2] E | Light-coloured leached layer (not in every profile)
  [11:2:5.3] B | Subsoil: leached minerals collect
  [11:2:5.4] C | Weathered parent material, little organic matter
  [11:2:5.5] R | Unweathered bedrock
[11:2:6] - **AP:** climate, altitude and **bedrock** shape the soils; river silt of the **Godavari, Krishna and Pennar** is among AP's parent materials.

## SUB 3 | World Soil Types – Zonal, Intrazonal, Azonal; Residual and Transported
[11:3:0] TABLE head: Group | Meaning | Examples
  [11:3:0.0] Zonal | Mature soils reflecting climate and vegetation | Podzol, chernozem, laterite, sierozem (desert grey)
  [11:3:0.1] Intrazonal | Local conditions (drainage, salts) dominate | Solonchak (saline), bog soils
  [11:3:0.2] Azonal | Young, no developed profile | Lithosol (thin soil on rock), alluvium, regosol
[11:3:1] - Major zonal and other world soils:
[11:3:2] TABLE head: Soil | Climate / setting
  [11:3:2.0] Podzol | Cold taiga (boreal conifer); thin, acidic, leached, humus-poor
  [11:3:2.1] Laterite | Hot, heavy-rain tropics; silica and lime leached, iron and aluminium oxides left; acidic
  [11:3:2.2] Terra rossa | Mediterranean; red clay from the weathering of limestone (and dolomite)
  [11:3:2.3] Chernozem | Temperate grasslands (steppes, prairies); black, humus-rich
  [11:3:2.4] Oxisols | Equatorial-tropical surfaces stable for very long (APPSC 2019)
[11:3:3] - **In-situ / residual = sedentary** soils (formed where the rock weathered; mainly Peninsular India). **Transported (ex-situ)** by rivers, wind, ice – mainly North India; alluvium ≈ **45.6%** of India.
[11:3:4] - **AP cultivated land (Jul 2026):** **red ~60%** (Rayalaseema), **black (regur) ~25%**, deltaic alluvium ~6%, **laterite ~1%**.
[11:3:5] - **FAO Status of the World's Soil Resources 2026** (3 Sep 2026): threats led by **soil erosion**; soil biodiversity loss ranked **4th of seven**, after erosion, loss of soil organic carbon and nutrient mismanagement.

## SUB 4 | Denudation and the Cycle of Erosion
[11:4:0] - **Denudation** = weathering + mass wasting + erosion + transportation; order: **weathering** (first stage) → mass wasting/erosion → transportation → **deposition**.
[11:4:1] - Agents: **running water** (most powerful), wind, ice, waves, gravity. Water = strongest cause of soil erosion (**sheet → rill → gully**; Chambal ravines = gully erosion); **wind** leads in arid areas like the **Thar**.
[11:4:2] TABLE head: Person | Contribution
  [11:4:2.0] W.M. Davis (1899) | "Father of (American) geomorphology"; geographical cycle of erosion: landscape = structure + process + stage; youth → maturity → old age; ends in a near-flat peneplain with monadnocks; renewed uplift = rejuvenation
  [11:4:2.1] Walther Penck | Uplift and erosion act simultaneously; landform depends on their ratio; slopes retreat parallel
[11:4:3] - Davis put forward the cycle in **1899**.
[11:4:4] TABLE head: Term | Meaning
  [11:4:4.0] Peneplain | Low, almost featureless plain worn by rivers almost to base level (Davis's term)
  [11:4:4.1] Monadnock | Resistant residual hill on a peneplain
  [11:4:4.2] Inselberg | Isolated residual hill of arid/semi-arid regions (Uluru)
[11:4:5] - **Peninsular plateau** of India = old peneplain, **rejuvenated** in places. Wegener = continental drift, Dutton = isostasy – not geomorphology's father.
[11:4:6] ~~ Sources: NCERT Class 11 Fundamentals of Physical Geography ch.6-7 (OnlyIAS NCERT Physical Geography 05 p.4-5; 06 p.1-2); PMF Physical Geography 02 p.106 (book p.216, peneplain); Britannica "William Morris Davis"; app notes 4:11 sub 0, 4
## SUB 5 | Mass Wasting
[11:5:0] - Downslope movement of rock and earth material **under gravity** (no transporting medium) = mass movement; the common term in exams = **landslide** (APPSC 2019).
[11:5:1] TABLE head: Type | Nature
  [11:5:1.0] Rockfall | Free fall of rocks from steep cliffs
  [11:5:1.1] Slide | Whole coherent mass slips along a surface
  [11:5:1.2] Slump | Rotational slip along a curved surface in weak, wet soils
  [11:5:1.3] Debris flow / mudflow / earthflow | Fast water-soil-rock mix after heavy rain or snowmelt
  [11:5:1.4] Creep | Very slow movement from wetting-drying, heating-cooling
[11:5:2] - Conditions: slopes **above ~30 degrees**, weak/fractured rock, bare slopes; saturated clay and silt.
[11:5:3] - India's main trigger: **SW monsoon rainfall**, then **earthquakes**. Most at risk: **Himalaya and North-East** (also Western Ghats).
[11:5:4] ~~ Sources: NCERT Class 11 Fundamentals of Physical Geography ch.6 (OnlyIAS NCERT Physical Geography 05 p.9-11, mass movements); PMF Physical Geography 01 p.83 (earthflow, slump); app notes 4:11 sub 3

## SUB 0 | Deltas and Estuaries
[12:0:0] TABLE head: Basis | Delta | Estuary
  [12:0:0.0] Process | Deposition dominates | Erosion / tidal flushing dominates
  [12:0:0.1] Mouth | River splits into many distributaries | Single funnel-shaped (drowned) mouth with free connection to the sea
  [12:0:0.2] Example | Ganga-Brahmaputra, Godavari, Krishna, Mahanadi | Narmada, Tapi (Tapti), Western Ghats rivers; world's largest = St. Lawrence
[12:0:1] - **Conditions for a delta:** heavy sediment load, slow flow, shallow sea, **weak tides and currents** (strong tides remove sediment).
[12:0:2] - **West-flowing Deccan rivers** form **estuaries, not deltas**: **steep gradient** (fast flow), hard rock, short courses, little silt; the Narmada and Tapi also flow through rift valleys.
[12:0:3] - An estuary is an **ecotone** (river–sea transition), among the most productive habitats.
[12:0:4] # Largest delta
[12:0:5] - **World's largest (greatest) delta:** **Ganga-Brahmaputra(-Meghna) = Sundarbans / Bengal delta** (~1 lakh sq km), **arcuate**; about **two-thirds in Bangladesh**; the **world's largest mangrove forest**; also the largest delta of the Indian subcontinent.
[12:0:6] - In Bangladesh the **Ganga = Padma**; the **Brahmaputra = Jamuna** (so "Ganga is called Jamuna in Bangladesh" is false).
[12:0:7] - **Moribund delta:** the old western part of the **Bengal delta** (Murshidabad–Nadia) whose distributaries are dead/inactive; the mature and active deltas lie further south-east.
[12:0:8] TABLE head: Delta type | Shape / condition | Example
  [12:0:8.0] Arcuate (fan) | Arc-shaped; most common; sediment denser than sea water | Nile, Ganga
  [12:0:8.1] Bird's foot | Long branching distributaries; sediment lighter, carried far | Mississippi (Gulf of Mexico)
  [12:0:8.2] Cuspate | Tooth-shaped; densities equal | Tiber (Italy)
  [12:0:8.3] Estuarine | Drowned funnel mouth | Narmada, Tapi
[12:0:9] - **Sinking deltas (Apr 2026):** 7 deltas (Ganga-Brahmaputra, Nile, Mekong, Yangtze, Amazon, Irrawaddy, Mississippi) ≈ **57%** of sinking delta area; causes **groundwater extraction + less silt** (app CA).
[12:0:10] # AP coast
[12:0:11] - Coastline ~974 km; built by the **Godavari** (delta = **Konaseema**) and **Krishna** deltas; minor deltas: **Vamsadhara** (Srikakulam), **Penna** (Nellore).
[12:0:12] - **Kolleru**: freshwater lake between the Godavari and Krishna deltas; WLS **308.55 sq km**. **Pulicat**: saltwater lagoon on the **AP–Tamil Nadu border**.
[12:0:13] > Asked again and again: largest delta = Ganga-Brahmaputra (Sundarbans); bird's foot = Mississippi; Tapti forms an estuary; west-flowing rivers – steep gradient.

## SUB 1 | Plains and River-plain Terms – Doab, Prayag, Bhabar, Terai, Lacustrine Plains
[12:1:0] TABLE head: Term | Meaning
  [12:1:0.0] Doab | Land between two confluent rivers (Persian do-ab = two waters); Ganga-Yamuna doab, Bist Doab (Beas–Sutlej)
  [12:1:0.1] Prayag | A sacred river confluence
  [12:1:0.2] Bhabar | Porous belt of pebbles and boulders, 8–16 km wide, along the Shiwalik foothills at the break of slope – a piedmont deposit; streams sink underground
  [12:1:0.3] Terai | Wet, marshy, forested belt south of the Bhabar (15–30 km) where streams re-emerge
  [12:1:0.4] Bhangar | Older alluvium above flood level; contains kankar
  [12:1:0.5] Khadar | Newer floodplain alluvium, renewed every flood; more fertile

  [12:1:2.1] Flood plain | Fine silt dropped when the river overflows | Along river valleys
  [12:1:2.2] Lacustrine plain | Lake filled with sediment, then drained | Kashmir Valley (Karewas), Imphal Basin (Manipur)

## SUB 2 | River Course, Erosional Landforms, Gorge and Canyon
[12:2:0] TABLE head: Course (stage) | Dominant work | Typical features
  [12:2:0.0] Upper (youth) | Vertical erosion, steep gradient | V-shaped valleys, gorges, canyons, waterfalls, rapids, potholes
  [12:2:0.1] Middle (maturity) | Lateral erosion + transport | Wider valleys, meanders, floodplains begin
  [12:2:0.2] Lower (old age) | Deposition, gentle gradient | Broad floodplains, levees, oxbow lakes, braided channels, deltas/estuaries
[12:2:1] - **Order downstream (CDS 2014):** falls → meanders → oxbow lake → delta (**II, I, IV, III**). Middle course is not vertical erosion alone.
[12:2:2] TABLE head: Landform | Formation
  [12:2:2.0] V-shaped valley | Rills → gullies → valleys by vertical erosion
  [12:2:2.1] Waterfall | Drop over a resistant rock band or fault step
  [12:2:2.2] Potholes | Eddy-whirled pebbles drill holes; large one below a waterfall = plunge pool
  [12:2:2.3] Incised (entrenched) meanders | Uplift rejuvenates the river; meanders cut deep into hard rock
  [12:2:2.4] River terraces | Stream cuts down into its own floodplain (erosional); paired = same height both banks; unpaired = one bank
[12:2:3] - River-profile terms:
[12:2:4] TABLE head: Term | Meaning
  [12:2:4.0] Graded profile | Smooth concave long profile; erosion and deposition roughly balanced
  [12:2:4.1] Base level | Lowest level a river can erode to; sea level = ultimate; lakes and dams = local
  [12:2:4.2] Stream capture (river piracy) | Headward erosion breaches a divide and takes a neighbour's upper waters
  [12:2:4.3] Peneplain | Near-featureless gentle plain worn by rivers almost to base level (W.M. Davis)
[12:2:5] - Gorge vs canyon:
[12:2:6] TABLE head: Basis | Gorge | Canyon
  [12:2:6.0] Nature | Deep, narrow, steep-sided valley | Very deeply / widely eroded gorge – a variant of a gorge
  [12:2:6.1] Width | Almost same at top and bottom | Wider at the top, step-like sides
  [12:2:6.2] Rock | Hard rocks | Horizontally bedded sedimentary rocks
  [12:2:6.3] Example | Chisapani (Karnali, Nepal) | Grand Canyon (Colorado River, Arizona)
[12:2:7] - Both gorges and canyons are **V-shaped valleys** (true); "gorges are extended forms of canyons" is **wrong** (APPSC G1).
[12:2:8] - **Grand Canyon**: a gorge carved by the **Colorado** River in **Arizona, USA**, ~446 km long, over 1.8 km deep.
[12:2:9] - **Gandikota canyon**: **Pennar (Penna) River**, YSR Kadapa, AP – "**Grand Canyon of India**" (UPSC 2022).
[12:2:10] - **Dihang gorge**: Brahmaputra enters India (Arunachal); Yarlung Tsangpo Grand Canyon near **Namcha Barwa**.

## SUB 3 | Drainage Patterns
[12:3:0] TABLE head: Pattern | Control | Example
  [12:3:0.0] Dendritic | Uniform rock, no structural control; tree-like, tributaries join at acute angles | Ganga plains, Assam Valley, Indus, Mahanadi, Godavari – most rivers of South India
  [12:3:0.1] Trellis | Folded rocks; primary tributaries parallel, secondary ones join at right angles | Appalachians, Singhbhum, upper Himalayan rivers
  [12:3:0.2] Radial | Streams flow outward from a central high | Sri Lanka central highlands; Amarkantak (Narmada, Son, Mahanadi)
  [12:3:0.3] Centripetal | Streams flow into a central lake/depression | Loktak Lake, Manipur
  [12:3:0.4] Rectangular | Joints and faults – main stream bends at right angles and tributaries join at right angles | Joint-controlled plateaus
  [12:3:0.5] Annular | Rings on an eroded dome | Structural domes
[12:3:1] - "Branches of a tree" = **dendritic**, not radial.
[12:3:2] - **Antecedent drainage:** river older than the uplift, keeps its course by cutting gorges as the land rises – **most Himalayan rivers** (Indus, Sutlej, Brahmaputra).
[12:3:3] - Genetic stream types: consequent (follows initial slope), subsequent (along weak strata), obsequent (opposite the dip), **insequent** – **no apparent control**, no clear reason for its course (dendritic on uniform rock).
[12:3:4] ~~ Sources: NCERT Class 11 India: Physical Environment ch.3 (OnlyIAS NCERT Indian Geography 04 p.2-3, drainage patterns); PMF Indian Physical Geography 02 p.14-20; NextGen Indian Geography 02 p.9-12; app notes 4:12 sub 9
## SUB 4 | Depositional Landforms, Ravines and Badlands
[12:4:0] TABLE head: Landform | How it forms
  [12:4:0.0] Alluvial fan | Gently sloping, fan-shaped coarse alluvium dropped by (often braided) streams at the break of slope (hill foot)
  [12:4:0.1] Natural levee | Raised sandy banks along the channel
  [12:4:0.2] Floodplain | Fine alluvium dropped when the river overflows
  [12:4:0.3] Braided channel | Sand bars split the channel into many strands with riverine islands
  [12:4:0.4] Oxbow lake | Meander loop cut off; stagnant water left
[12:4:1] - Meander: **outer bend concave, erosional** (cliff); **inner slip-off side convex, depositional**.
[12:4:2] - **APPSC G1 2023:** depositional work of Himalayan rivers = **braided channels, alluvial fans, natural levees**; **river terraces** are products of vertical **erosion** (NCERT) – so II, III and IV only.
[12:4:3] - Bajada = coalesced alluvial fans of deserts; diara = riverine sand islands of Bihar.
[12:4:4] - **Gully erosion → ravines**: rills deepen into gullies; dense gully network = **badland topography** – **Chambal ravines** (central India); **Chos** of Hoshiarpur (Punjab).

=== NOTES 4:13 | Major Landforms – Glacial, Arid/aeolian, Karst and Coastal Landforms
## SUB 0 | Karst Landforms – Groundwater Work
[13:0:0] - **Karst**: landscape made by **groundwater dissolving limestone/dolomite** (carbonation); named after the Karst region of **Slovenia**. Sinkholes form in **limestone topography**, not sandstone, polar or desert areas.
[13:0:1] TABLE head: Landform | Key feature
  [13:0:1.0] Lapiaz / karren | Grooved, ridged limestone surface
  [13:0:1.1] Sinkhole (doline) | Closed funnel depression – the commonest karst landform
  [13:0:1.2] Uvala | Joined (coalesced) dolines
  [13:0:1.3] Polje | Large enclosed karst depression
  [13:0:1.4] Swallow hole | Point where a surface stream disappears underground
  [13:0:1.5] Blind valley | Valley that ends abruptly where its stream sinks (steephead valley) – a karst feature
  [13:0:1.6] Stalactite | Calcium-carbonate deposit hanging from the cave roof
  [13:0:1.7] Stalagmite | Deposit rising from the cave floor
  [13:0:1.8] Pillar (column) | Stalactite and stalagmite joined
[13:0:2] - Rising size: **sinkhole → doline → uvala → polje**.
[13:0:3] - Stalactites, stalagmites and pillars are **depositional landforms of underground (ground) water** – water dissolves CaCO₃ and re-deposits it as it drips; not wind, glacier or river features.
[13:0:4] - Pot hole = river bed; blow hole = coast; playa = desert.
[13:0:5] - **Springs** are common in **well-jointed rocks**, **karst (limestone) regions** (resurgences / vauclusian springs) and **tilted strata** where permeable and impermeable beds alternate – **not** in arid areas (deep water table).

Leong ch.4 Springs (Part 5.1 p.1) and ch.8 Limestone landforms (Part 7.2-8.1); app notes 4:13 sub 6

## SUB 2 | Coastal Landforms
[13:2:0] - **Main agent of coastal erosion: waves** (also tides, currents, tsunamis, humans). Processes: hydraulic action, abrasion, attrition, solution (**saltation is wind**).
[13:2:1] TABLE head: Type | Landform | Formation
  [13:2:1.0] Erosional | Sea cliff, notch | Wave erosion; notch cut at the cliff base
  [13:2:1.1] Erosional | Wave-cut platform | Cliff retreat
  [13:2:1.2] Erosional | Sea cave → arch → stack → stump | Weak zones eroded; cave cuts a headland; arch collapses → stack
  [13:2:1.3] Depositional | Beach | Sand/shingle between tides
  [13:2:1.4] Depositional | Spit / hook | Ridge joined to land at one end (longshore drift); a hooked spit = hook
  [13:2:1.5] Depositional | Bar | Ridge across a bay; closes it into a lagoon
  [13:2:1.6] Depositional | Tombolo | Bar joining an island to the mainland
  [13:2:1.7] Depositional | Lagoon | Shallow water cut off by a bar (Chilika, Pulicat)
[13:2:2] - **NDA 2018:** stack is **not** depositional; notch, arch, cliff are erosional, hook is depositional.
[13:2:3] - Defences: soft = beach nourishment, dunes, **mangroves**; hard = seawalls, **groynes** (across longshore drift), breakwaters. Dams, harbours and sand mining cut sediment and worsen erosion.
[13:2:4] - East coast erodes more: soft low alluvium, no rocky cliffs. AP: severe at **Uppada (Kakinada)** and the Krishna coast (Jul 2026). Pulicat = saltwater; Kolleru = freshwater.
[13:2:5] TABLE head: Coast | Formed by | Example

[13:2:7] - Geo-heritage: **Erra Matti Dibbalu** (red dunes, Visakhapatnam) and **Tirumala Silathoranam** (quartzite arch) on the **UNESCO Tentative List (Sep 2025)**.

## SUB 4 | Arid (Aeolian) Landforms
[13:4:0] - Desert landforms are made **mainly by wind** (deflation, abrasion, deposition) with help from sheet floods. Wind transport: deflation, **saltation**, surface creep.

  [13:4:1.2] Sand dune | Deposition | Accumulation of wind-blown sand
  [13:4:1.3] Yardang | Erosion | Streamlined ridge parallel to the wind
  [13:4:1.4] Zeugen | Erosion | Table-like ridge, hard cap over soft rock
  [13:4:1.5] Mushroom rock | Erosion (abrasion) | Base abraded more than top
  [13:4:1.6] Ventifact | Erosion | Wind-faceted pebble
  [13:4:1.7] Deflation hollow | Erosion | Wind removal
  [13:4:1.8] Inselberg | Erosion (residual) | Isolated residual hill (Uluru)

=== NOTES 4:14 | Atmosphere – Structure (layers) and Composition
## SUB 0 | Ozone Layer – Location, Formation and UV Shield
[14:0:0] # Where the ozone layer lies (asked 17 times)
[14:0:1] - **Ozone (O₃) = three oxygen atoms** (APPSC BC Welfare). It lies mainly in the **stratosphere** – the standard answer to "ozone layer is located in / found mainly in / maximum concentration in / rich in ozone" (UPPCS ×8, CGPSC, SSC, CDS 2009, APPSC AEE, APPSC AMVI) – not the troposphere, mesosphere, ionosphere or exosphere.
[14:0:2] - **Lower stratosphere:** main layer **~15–30 km**, densest **~15–35 km** (NCERT/Rocket); exam heights "**15–20 km above the surface**" (State PSC) are the accepted option – never "below the surface", never 40–50 or 70–80 km.
[14:0:3] - About **90%** of all ozone is **stratospheric**, ~10% tropospheric.
[14:0:4] - Formation (**Chapman cycle**): **UV + O₂ → O + O; O + O₂ → O₃**; O₃ absorbs UV and splits again.
[14:0:5] - Column ozone is measured in **Dobson Units (DU)**; 1 DU = 0.01 mm of pure ozone at 0°C, 1 atm.
[14:0:6] # What it does (asked 13 times)
[14:0:7] - **Absorbs harmful ultraviolet (UV) radiation** of the Sun and acts as **Earth's shield** (APPSC FBO, APPSC ASO; MPPSC 1994 ×2; UKPSC 2002, 2006; UPPCS 2006, 2013; CGPSC 2013) – it does **not** supply oxygen, control Earth's temperature, trap heat, reflect sunlight, cause rain or stop earthquakes/monsoon failure.
[14:0:8] - It does **not** absorb cosmic rays, infrared, visible light or microwaves – only **UV**.

  [14:0:9.1] UV-B | 280–315 nm | Mostly absorbed, part reaches the ground

[14:0:10] - Excess UV → **skin (dermal) cancer** (UPPSC 2014), **cataract**, weaker immunity, lower crop yield → ozone is **essential for biotic life** because it screens high-energy radiation (UPPSC Mains 2010: A and R true, R explains A).
[14:0:11] - Stratosphere **warms with height** because ozone turns absorbed UV into heat.

## SUB 1 | Ozone Depletion, the Ozone Hole and Treaties
[14:1:0] # Cause (asked 13 times)
[14:1:1] - Depletion happens in the **stratosphere** (APPSC AEE; APPSC Degree Lecturers).
[14:1:2] - **Main cause: halocarbons** – **CFCs** (most damaging), HCFCs, halons – that release **chlorine and bromine** (APPSC 2023 Lecturers ×2; APPSC 2025 Welfare Organiser and Sainik Welfare Officer: **CFCs**, not UV rays, heavy metals or reactive oxygen species).
[14:1:3] - **Carbon dioxide has no direct role** in ozone depletion (APPSC Sainik Welfare) – nor do CO, nitrogen, acetylene, ethylene, SO₂ or O₂.
[14:1:4] TABLE head: ODS | Use
  [14:1:4.0] CFCs | Refrigeration, ACs, aerosols, foam
  [14:1:4.1] HCFCs | CFC substitute – also deplete ozone
  [14:1:4.2] Halons | Fire extinguishers
  [14:1:4.3] Methyl bromide | Fumigant
  [14:1:4.4] Carbon tetrachloride, methyl chloroform | Solvents
[14:1:5] - CFCs, halons **and** carbon tetrachloride are all ozone-depleting (UPPSC 2012 → I, II and III).
[14:1:6] - **HFCs** spare ozone but have high global-warming potential; CFCs destroy ozone **and** are strong GHGs.
[14:1:7] # The Antarctic ozone hole (asked 8 times)
[14:1:8] - Maximum over **Antarctica** – the **poles**, not the equator or tropics (MPPSC 2008; CDS 2012); forms in the southern spring **Aug–Nov**.
[14:1:9] - Reason (UPSC 2011): **a prominent polar front (polar vortex) and polar stratospheric clouds (PSCs), plus the inflow of CFCs** – PSCs (12–22 km, nitric acid ice) turn inactive chlorine into reactive forms that destroy ozone when sunlight returns.
[14:1:10] - First reported in **1985** by a **British team** (British Antarctic Survey: **Farman, Gardiner, Shanklin**) (RAS 2012; UPPSC Mains 2013).
[14:1:11] - 2025 hole peaked at just over **21 million sq km** and closed early (record ~29 million sq km, 2006).

  [14:1:12.2] Kigali Amendment | 2016 (Kigali, Rwanda) | HFC phase-down 80–85%; in force 1 Jan 2019

[14:2:0] # Gases of dry air (asked 10 times)
[14:2:1] TABLE head: Gas | Share by volume | Type
  [14:2:1.0] Nitrogen | 78.08% (~78%) – most abundant | Permanent
  [14:2:1.1] Oxygen | 20.95% (~21%, rounded "20%" – APPSC G1 2012) | Permanent
  [14:2:1.2] Argon | 0.93% – third, inert | Permanent

  [14:2:1.4] Water vapour | 0–4% | Variable
  [14:2:1.5] Neon, helium, krypton, xenon, hydrogen, ozone | Traces | —
[14:2:2] - **N₂ > O₂ > Ar > CO₂** (APPSC G1 2023). **Nitrogen + oxygen = 99%** – the air near the surface is "mainly nitrogen and oxygen" (RAS 1997).
[14:2:3] - **Permanent gases** keep a constant proportion: nitrogen, oxygen, argon (and the noble gases); **variable:** CO₂, water vapour, ozone → UPPCS Mains 2017 answer **nitrogen**.
[14:2:4] - **Chlorine** is **not** a normal constituent of air (APPSC G2 2008); neon, helium and CO₂ are.

[14:2:11] - Light gases rise highest: the **exosphere** is mostly **hydrogen** (with helium) (NDA 2015).
[14:2:12] - **Early atmosphere:** **reducing** – methane, ammonia, hydrocarbons, little free oxygen; methane and hydrocarbons were oxidised and replaced by **carbon dioxide** (BPSC 1995); photosynthesis later made it oxygen-rich.

## SUB 3 | Layers – Basis, Sequence and Temperature Trend
[14:3:0] # Sequence (asked 10 times)
[14:3:1] - Layers are divided on the **vertical profile of air temperature** (APPSC G1) – not wind, humidity or pressure.
[14:3:2] TABLE head: Order | Layer | Height | Temperature | Identity
  [14:3:2.0] 1 | Troposphere – lowest (SSC CGL 2019; UKPSC 2016) | 0 to 8 km (poles) / 18 km (equator); avg 13 km | Falls | Weather
  [14:3:2.1] 2 | Stratosphere – second layer (APPSC AMVI) | to 50 km | Rises | Ozone
  [14:3:2.2] 3 | Mesosphere | 50–80 km | Falls to −100°C – coldest (Astt. Comm. 2018) | Meteors burn
  [14:3:2.3] 4 | Thermosphere (ionosphere 80–400/640 km) | from 80 km | Rises sharply | Ions, radio waves, aurora
  [14:3:2.4] 5 | Exosphere | outermost | Rarefied | Merges into space
[14:3:3] - Upward order **Troposphere → Stratosphere → Mesosphere → Thermosphere/Ionosphere** (UPSC 1998, CDS 2015, UPPCS 2005, UPPCS 2015).
[14:3:4] - The troposphere is the layer of **smallest height** (SSC 2011).
[14:3:5] - The **atmosphere** = the thin layer of gases around the Earth (APPSC Jr Asst); it has **no definite upper limit** and **thins gradually** until imperceptible (CDS 2015).
[14:3:6] - Temperature trend upward: **falls, rises, falls, rises**; reversal layers: **stratosphere and thermosphere**.

  [14:3:7.0] Tropopause | 8–18 km
  [14:3:7.1] Stratopause | ~50 km

## SUB 4 | Troposphere and Tropopause
[14:4:0] # Troposphere (asked 12 times)
[14:4:1] - **Average height 13 km**; ~8 km at the poles, ~**18 km at the equator** (State PSC: "average height 13 km" is the true statement; it is not the top layer and its temperature falls with height).
[14:4:2] - **Thickest at the equator** because **high insolation and strong convection** carry heat to great heights → UPSC 2004 (A and R true, R explains A; "thickness of the atmosphere" in the stem means the troposphere) and UPSC 2024 (I and II correct, II explains I).
[14:4:3] - **Almost all weather** – clouds, rain, fog, hail, storms – happens here (RAS 1999; MPPSC 2023); the **cloud mass of a cyclone** rises only within the troposphere (APPSC AE 2017).
[14:4:4] - Contains nearly all **water vapour and dust**; most important for **biological activity**.
[14:4:5] - **Normal lapse rate ~6.5°C per km** (≈ **1°C per 165 m**) – temperature **decreases** with height (NDA 2018 option "6.4°C per km" = the approximate normal rate).
[14:4:6] - **Agra and Darjeeling** lie on about the same latitude; Darjeeling is much colder in January because temperature falls with **height** (UPPCS 2022: A and R true, R explains A).
[14:4:7] # Tropopause
[14:4:8] - **Transition zone between troposphere and stratosphere**, lying **below the stratosphere**; its height **varies with season** (higher in summer) and **latitude** (higher at the equator) → APPSC G1 2018: **i, ii and iii all correct**.
[14:4:9] - It is a **zone of mixing** (NDA 2012) – not formed by meteoric dust, not 5–10 km thick, and it is ~18 km (not 8 km) over the equator.
[14:4:10] - Temperature at the tropopause: about **−80°C over the equator**, **−45°C over the poles**.
[14:4:11] ~~ Sources: NextGen Indian Geography 03 p.6 (p.34); NextGen Rocket General Geography #6 p.43-44; app notes 4:14 sub 4

## SUB 6 | Carbon Dioxide, Water Vapour, Dust and the Greenhouse Property
[14:6:0] # CO₂ as a greenhouse gas (asked 5 times)
[14:6:1] - CO₂ is **transparent to incoming solar (short-wave) radiation** but **opaque to outgoing terrestrial (long-wave, infrared) radiation** – it **absorbs infrared** and re-radiates heat (UPSC 2012; CDS 2012; RAS 1992) – not UV, not all radiation, not water vapour.
[14:6:2] - **Greenhouse effect** = heating of the atmosphere by absorption of infrared by CO₂ and other gases; **CO₂** is the gas that contributes most (APPSC FBO) and whose rise enhances it (UPPSC 2015) – not CO, O₂, N₂ or argon.
[14:6:3] - Other GHGs: **methane, nitrous oxide, water vapour (most abundant GHG), CFCs**; not hydrogen, krypton, neon, helium or argon.
[14:6:4] # Water vapour and dust
[14:6:5] - **Water vapour:** up to **4%** in warm wet tropics, **under 1%** in deserts and polar air; decreases with height and from equator to poles; absorbs insolation and Earth's heat (**blanket**); decides air stability.
[14:6:6] - **Dust:** sea salt, fine soil, smoke-soot, ash, pollen, meteor dust; mostly in lower layers; **more in subtropical and temperate regions because of dry winds** than in equatorial and polar regions → UPSC 2025: **Statement I correct, Statement II ("less dry winds") wrong**.
[14:6:7] - Dust and salt act as **hygroscopic nuclei** for condensation → clouds.
[14:6:8] ~~ Sources: NextGen Indian Geography 03 p.3-6 (pp.31-34); PMF Physical Geography 04 p.21-22; app notes 4:14 sub 0-1

=== NOTES 4:15 | Insolation and Heat Budget; Distribution of Temperature, Inversion
## SUB 0 | Heating of the Atmosphere – Radiation, Conduction, Convection, Advection
[15:0:0] # How the air is heated (asked 8 times)
[15:0:1] TABLE head: Process | Mechanism
  [15:0:1.0] Insolation | Direct heating by incoming short-wave solar radiation
  [15:0:1.1] Conduction | Heats the lower layers of air in contact with the warm ground
  [15:0:1.2] Convection | Vertical transfer – warm air rises, cool air sinks (troposphere)
  [15:0:1.3] Advection | Horizontal transfer by wind (e.g. the Loo)
  [15:0:1.4] Terrestrial radiation | Long-wave infra-red from the Earth = main heater of the air
[15:0:2] - APPSC Group-II 2024 match: **Advection – horizontal; Convection – vertical; Insolation – direct; Conduction – lower layers** (a-I, b-II, c-III, d-IV).
[15:0:3] - The atmosphere is **heated from below**: the troposphere is warmest near the ground because it is **heated by the Earth's surface** (SSC 2011) – not because it is closest to the Sun or makes its own heat. It is **not** mainly heated by incoming sunlight (UPSC 2024: that statement is wrong).
[15:0:4] - Sun's radiation = **short-wave**: mostly **visible light + short-wave (near) infra-red** (NDA 2019); the Earth re-radiates **long-wave infra-red**, not ultraviolet (Asst. Comm. 2009: both "receives at infra-red end" and "re-radiates as UV" are wrong → **neither**).
[15:0:5] - Long-wave terrestrial radiation is absorbed mainly by **carbon dioxide and water vapour** (concentrated in the lower atmosphere) – UPSC 2023 correct statement; infra-red is **not** part of the visible spectrum and is **not** two-thirds of insolation (about half of insolation is infra-red).
[15:0:6] - **Conduction, convection and radiation** are tied to long-wave (terrestrial) heat transfer; **scattering** is a short-wave process (IAS 2018 odd one out).
[15:0:7] - **Radiation needs no medium** (UPRO 2017); conduction and convection need matter.
[15:0:8] - Wind on a hot summer day = **convection current** of air (CDS 2011); trade winds too arise from **convection** (Hadley cell).
[15:0:9] ~~ Sources: NCERT Fundamentals of Physical Geography ch.9 (via PMF Physical Geography 04 p.16, 29-31); NextGen Rocket General Geography #7 p.52; app notes 4:15 sub 0
## SUB 1 | Albedo, Heat Budget and Greenhouse Effect
[15:1:0] # Albedo (asked 7 times)
[15:1:1] - **Albedo = reflecting power** of a surface – the share of incoming radiation it reflects (UPPCS 2019).
[15:1:2] - Highest albedo: **fresh snow** (APPSC Group-I 2019; APPSC Group-1; UPSC 2010 ×2) – reflects **75–95%**; then sand > grass > forest > **water (lowest)**.
[15:1:3] TABLE head: Surface | Albedo (approx.)
  [15:1:3.0] Fresh snow | 75–95%
  [15:1:3.1] Dry sand / desert | 20–40%
  [15:1:3.2] Grass | 20–25%
  [15:1:3.3] Crop land (paddy) | 15–25%
  [15:1:3.4] Water (sun high) | below 10%
[15:1:4] - **Low, thick clouds reflect** sunlight (cooling); **high, thin clouds trap heat** (warming).
[15:1:5] - **Ice-albedo feedback**: melting ice exposes darker ocean that absorbs more heat; the **Arctic warms 3–4 times** the global average (Apr 2025).
[15:1:6] # Heat budget – 100 units
[15:1:7] TABLE head: Of 100 units of insolation | Units
  [15:1:7.0] Reflected to space (clouds 27, scattering 6, snow/ice 2) = planetary albedo | 35
  [15:1:7.1] Absorbed by the atmosphere | 14
  [15:1:7.2] Absorbed by the surface | 51
[15:1:8] - About **30–35%** of insolation goes back to space without heating the Earth = **Earth's albedo** (CDS 2013).
[15:1:9] - Surface gives back all 51: **17 directly to space**, 34 to the atmosphere; atmosphere radiates **48** (14 + 34) to space → 35 + 17 + 48 = 100, so the Earth neither warms nor cools.
[15:1:10] # Greenhouse effect
[15:1:11] - Atmosphere is **transparent to short waves**, **opaque to long waves**; greenhouse gases send part of the long-wave heat back.
[15:1:12] - Without the greenhouse effect / atmosphere: **about −18°C** instead of **+15°C** (UPPSC 2020); day–night extremes would **increase** (CDS 2010).
[15:1:13] TABLE head: Gas | Share of effect
  [15:1:13.0] CO₂ | ~60%
  [15:1:13.1] Methane ("marsh gas") | ~20%
  [15:1:13.2] CFCs | ~15%
  [15:1:13.3] N₂O | ~5%
[15:1:14] - **Water vapour** = most abundant GHG, acts as a feedback. **GWP (100 yr)**: CO₂ 1; methane **28–36**; N₂O **265–298**.
[15:1:15] - Global **land** temperature up **1.59°C** since pre-industrial (UNEP Emissions Gap Report 2025).

## SUB 2 | Cloudy and Clear Nights; Range of Temperature
[15:2:0] # Cloudy vs clear nights (asked 5 times)
[15:2:1] - **Cloudy nights are warmer**: clouds **reflect / send back the heat given off by the Earth** (UPSC 2001) and **hinder terrestrial radiation** (NDA 2010) – a blanket.
[15:2:2] - **Clear nights are colder** because of free **(terrestrial) radiation** to space (State PSC; RRB 2016) – not conduction or condensation; clear skies give the greatest night cooling, dew and frost.
[15:2:3] # Range of temperature (asked 3 times)
[15:2:4] - **Interior / continental** = large daily and annual range; **coasts** = equable, small range.
[15:2:5] - **Highest daily range in India: desert areas of Rajasthan** (CGPSC 2011); highest winter diurnal range **NW India**.
[15:2:6] - **Chennai** (IAS 2002): annual mean **~26°C** and an annual range of only **~9°C** fit a tropical coastal station – Allahabad has a large range, Cherrapunji far more rain, Kolkata a cooler winter.
[15:2:7] - **Seasonal range maximum in high latitudes**; largest annual range in **continental interiors such as Siberia**; **minimum at the Equator**.
[15:2:8] - AP: **interior Rayalaseema** high daily range; coast (Visakhapatnam, Kakinada) small range – sea breezes.
[15:2:9] ~~ Sources: NextGen Rocket General Geography #7 p.52-53 (cloudy vs clear sky); PMF Physical Geography 04 p.37; app notes 4:15 sub 7
## SUB 3 | Insolation – Meaning, Factors and Distribution
[15:3:0] # Meaning and factors (asked 7 times)
[15:3:1] - **Insolation = incoming solar radiation** (APPSC Group-1) – energy received by the Earth's surface as short waves.
[15:3:2] - Factors: **angle of the Sun's rays (set by latitude)**, **length of the day**, **transparency of the atmosphere** (clouds, dust, water vapour), Earth–Sun distance, land–sea distribution, slope and aspect.
[15:3:3] - Intensity depends mainly on **latitude** (NDA 2011) – not on **longitude** (APSLPRB SI 2023: longitude has no influence).
[15:3:4] - **Perihelion ~3 January, ~147 million km**; **aphelion ~4 July, ~152 million km** – distance matters little; tilt and angle matter more.
[15:3:5] # Distribution
[15:3:6] - Equator gets **direct** rays, poles **oblique** rays → insolation **decreases** from the Equator to the poles.
[15:3:7] - **Least seasonal variation: equatorial regions** (CDS 2013 – near-constant day length and high Sun); **greatest seasonal contrast: polar zones** (NDA 2013 – months of continuous day and night).
[15:3:8] - At the same latitude insolation is **higher over continents than oceans**; **maximum insolation and highest temperatures** over the **subtropical deserts** (clear skies, dry air), not the Equator.
[15:3:9] - Equator receives on the whole only about **2.5 times** (not 10 times) the annual insolation of the poles.
[15:3:10] - Heat **surplus in the tropics**, deficit at the poles; winds and ocean currents carry heat poleward.
[15:3:11] ~~ Sources: PMF Physical Geography 04 p.4, 16, 29-31; NextGen Rocket General Geography #7 p.53; app notes 4:15 sub 1

## SUB 5 | Distribution of Temperature – Isotherms, Lapse Rate, Inversion
[15:5:0] # Horizontal distribution (asked 4 times)
[15:5:1] - **Isotherms** join places of equal temperature (reduced to sea level); more regular in the **Southern Hemisphere** (mostly ocean).
[15:5:2] TABLE head: Month (NH) | Over land | Over oceans
  [15:5:2.0] January | Bend equatorward | Bend poleward (oceans warmer)
  [15:5:2.1] July | Bend poleward | Bend equatorward
[15:5:3] - UPSC 2025: in January isotherms bend equatorward over land and poleward over oceans **because air over the oceans is warmer than over land** → both statements true, II explains I.
[15:5:4] - **Highest temperatures** are recorded not at the Equator but around **20° N** (MPPCS 2010) – subtropical deserts.
[15:5:5] - **Thermal equator** (line of highest mean temperature) lies **north of the geographical equator** (CDS 2011) – more land in the NH; it moves with the Sun.
[15:5:6] - India: **January 18°C isotherm** divides India into tropical (south) and sub-tropical (north) (UPSC 1997); winter **20°C isotherm** runs from the Tapi mouth to the **Mahanadi delta**.
[15:5:7] - Controls: latitude, **altitude** (Himalayas cool), distance from sea, **ocean currents** (cold currents lower coastal temperature), relief, cloud cover.
[15:5:8] # Vertical distribution
[15:5:9] - **Normal lapse rate 6.5°C per km** – the ground heats air from below; higher air is thinner, with less water vapour and dust.
[15:5:10] # Temperature inversion
[15:5:11] - Temperature **rises with height** instead of falling. Favoured by **long winter nights, clear skies, calm dry air, snow cover** (not cloudy skies).
[15:5:12] TABLE head: Type | Feature
  [15:5:12.0] Surface (radiation) | Night cooling of the ground
  [15:5:12.1] Valley (air drainage) | Cold air drains downslope → frost pockets on valley floor; orchards on upper slopes
  [15:5:12.2] Frontal | Warm air over a cold-air wedge along a front
  [15:5:12.3] Permanent | Stratosphere = permanent inversion layer
[15:5:13] - Effects: **fog, frost, smog**; pollutants trapped in the planetary boundary layer (north Indian winter fog).
