## SUB 0 | Zero Hour and Question Hour – Timing, Nature, Duration
[0:0] TABLE head: Basis | Question Hour | Zero Hour
  [0:0.0] Lok Sabha timing | 11 AM – 12 Noon – the first hour of every sitting | 12 Noon onwards – immediately after Question Hour, till the regular agenda (business) of the day is taken up
  [0:0.1] Rajya Sabha (since 2014) | 12 Noon – 1 PM | 11 AM – the RS day begins with Zero Hour
  [0:0.2] Basis | Formal – in the Rules of Procedure | Informal – not in the Rules of Procedure and not in the Constitution
  [0:0.3] Origin | Traditional (Westminster) device | Indian innovation, in use since 1962 – not borrowed from the UK
  [0:0.4] Notice | 15 clear days for an ordinary question | No advance notice like a question; member intimates the Presiding Officer the same day (LS notices balloted on the day)
  [0:0.5] Reply | Ministers must answer | Ministers not bound to reply
  [0:0.6] Duration | One hour | Not fixed (unspecified) – lasts until the agenda is taken up; in practice about an hour
[0:1] - **Zero Hour – definition**: the **time gap between the Question Hour and the agenda** (regular business); members raise **matters of urgent public importance** – "members are free to raise any matter that they think is important". Not "the first hour", not "the hour before Question Hour", not "when no members are present", not a lunch break.
[0:2] - **Formally prescribed?** Zero Hour is the device **not formally prescribed** in the Rules – Calling Attention, Half-an-hour and Short-duration discussions are all in the Rules.
[0:3] - **Indian contribution** to parliamentary procedure: **Zero Hour** (also Calling Attention, 1954) – not cut motion, adjournment motion or guillotine (British devices).
[0:4] - **Ballot**: in the Lok Sabha **20 matters a day**, in order of their priority in the ballot, may be raised in Zero Hour (Lok Sabha Bulletin Part II).
[0:5] - Directions given by the Chair in Zero Hour reach the ministry concerned through the **Ministry of Parliamentary Affairs**.
[0:6] - **Question Hour aim**: **accountability of the Council of Ministers**; maximum attendance; asking and answering questions addressed to Ministers. **Interpellation** = the formal right of members to ask questions of the Executive.
[0:7] - APPSC G1 2003 / 2008, G2, UPPCS 2021, SSC 2019: Zero Hour = **the time immediately following Question Hour**.
[0:8] > Exam angle: "maximum duration of Zero Hour" = **unspecified / indefinite** (no time fixed in the Rules; some keys say "one hour"); statements – "in the Rules ✗, from UK ✗, starts after QH till agenda ✓".

## SUB 1 | No-confidence, Confidence and Censure Motions
[1:0] # No-confidence motion (Rule 198)
[1:1] - **Not mentioned in the Constitution** – flows from **Art. 75(3)** (Council of Ministers collectively responsible to the **Lok Sabha**). The Constitution mentions **neither** a confidence nor a no-confidence motion.
[1:2] - Moved **only in the Lok Sabha** (not the Rajya Sabha, not "both Houses", not a committee) – an **exclusive power of the Lok Sabha** (UPSC 2022: Emergency approval and impeachment need both Houses).
[1:3] - Against the **entire Council of Ministers / the Government** – not the PM alone, an individual minister or the Speaker.
[1:4] - Leave needs the support of **at least 50 members**; written notice to the **Secretary-General**; **no reasons** need be stated.
[1:5] - **Speaker** decides admissibility – **discretion to admit or reject**; he does not resign or dissolve the House.
[1:6] - If passed, the **whole Council of Ministers must resign**. "Six months must lapse between two motions" and "100 members" are **false**.
[1:7] - **Most powerful weapon** for executive accountability = the **no-confidence motion** (NextGen); the power to dismiss the government is real only when coalitions break (post-1989).
[1:8] - A PM who is a Rajya Sabha member **cannot vote** in the Lok Sabha, even on a no-confidence motion against his own government (UPSC 1997).
[1:9] - **First** no-confidence motion: **J.B. Kripalani** against **Nehru** (**1963**).
[1:10] - **AP angle**: Andhra State, Nov 1954 – **Gouthu Latchanna's** no-confidence motion carried **69–68**; **Prakasam ministry fell by one vote**; President's Rule from **November 1954**.
[1:11] # Comparison
[1:12] TABLE head: Motion | Moved by / where | Reasons | Against | If passed
  [1:12.0] No-confidence | Opposition; Lok Sabha only; 50 members | Not needed | Entire CoM | CoM must resign
  [1:12.1] Confidence | Government (Council of Ministers), to prove majority; Lok Sabha | – | – | If defeated, the Government falls
  [1:12.2] Censure | Lok Sabha | Must be stated | A minister, a group or the whole CoM | Not bound to resign – disapproval of policies
[1:13] - **Censure motion is different from the no-confidence motion** (aim, reasons, effect).
[1:14] - The motion the **Council of Ministers can move** = **Confidence Motion** (no-confidence, censure and adjournment come from members).

## SUB 2 | Instruments of Parliamentary Control over the Executive
[2:0] - NextGen lists four instruments: **(1) deliberation and discussion, (2) approval or refusal of laws, (3) financial control, (4) no-confidence motion**. A **walkout** is a protest, **not** an instrument.
[2:1] - Deliberation includes **Question Hour, Zero Hour, half-an-hour discussion, adjournment motion** etc.
[2:2] - **Accountability triangle**: **Question Hour + Supplementary Questions + Adjournment Motions** – UPSC 2017: all three (1, 2 and 3) are ways Parliament controls the Council of Ministers.
[2:3] - **Most effective day-to-day vigil**: **Question Hour** – "perhaps the most effective method of keeping vigil on the executive" (NextGen).
[2:4] - UPSC 2001: Parliament controls the administration **through Parliamentary Committees** – not Consultative Committees (set up by the Ministry of Parliamentary Affairs), periodic reports or writs (a court's power).
[2:5] - **Not a tool**: **dissolution of the House** (an executive power), **judicial review** (judiciary), **walkout**; the **Budget session** is only a period of sitting, and bills are formulated by the executive.
[2:6] - Other tools: calling attention, censure motion, half-an-hour and short-duration discussions, **Motion of Thanks**, resolutions; State Legislatures also use **estimates and public accounts committees**.

## SUB 3 | Adjournment Motion and Calling Attention Motion
[3:0] TABLE head: Basis | Adjournment Motion | Calling Attention Motion
  [3:0.0] Purpose | Draw attention of the House to a definite matter of urgent public importance | Call the attention of a Minister to an urgent matter and seek an authoritative statement
  [3:0.1] Origin | Traditional device | Indian innovation, since 1954; in the Rules
  [3:0.2] House | Lok Sabha only (RS cannot use it) | Either House
  [3:0.3] Support | Presiding Officer's consent + 50 members | Presiding Officer's permission
  [3:0.4] Business | Interrupts normal business – "extraordinary device"; debate at least 2 h 30 min | Does not disrupt business
  [3:0.5] Censure | Element of censure of the Government | No censure
[3:1] - **Adjournment motion conditions**: definite, factual, urgent, of public importance; **one matter** only; **specific matter of recent occurrence**; **must not raise a question of privilege**; not revive a matter discussed in the session; not sub judice; **not more than one such motion at a sitting**; admissible only when other tools (e.g. short-duration discussion) would be too late. RAS 2018 (Rajasthan Assembly) → **I, III and IV** (privilege statement wrong).
[3:2] - It is **not** a motion to adjourn the session or the day, to collect information or to cut a demand.
[3:3] - **Calling Attention notices restricted the scope of the Adjournment Motion** – not of Question Hour or Zero Hour.
[3:4] - Calling attention rules: introduced **1954**; notices in writing **by 10 AM**; a member may give **not more than two** notices for a sitting – so "not more than one" (MPPSC 2015 statement III) is **wrong**.
[3:5] - **Peculiar to Indian Legislatures**: **Calling Attention Motion** (with Zero Hour).

## SUB 4 | Closures, Guillotine, Point of Order and Yielding the Floor
[4:0] TABLE head: Device | Meaning
  [4:0.0] Closure motion | Cuts short the debate – stoppage of debate on a motion – and puts the matter to vote; four kinds
  [4:0.1] Simple closure | "Matter sufficiently discussed; be now put to vote"
  [4:0.2] Closure by compartments | Clauses grouped into parts; debate and vote part by part
  [4:0.3] Kangaroo closure | Only important clauses debated and voted; intervening clauses taken as passed
  [4:0.4] Guillotine | Allotted time over – all remaining clauses / demands, discussed or not, put to vote (in the Budget the Speaker puts all remaining demands to vote without discussion)
  [4:0.5] Point of order | Raised when proceedings depart from the rules (written or unwritten) or the articles regulating business; no debate; Presiding Officer rules
  [4:0.6] Yielding the floor | The Speaker asks a member to stop speaking and let another member speak (member gives way to another member or the Chair) – not decorum, crossing the floor or interpellation
[4:1] - **Guillotine** in loose exam wording = "approving bills / demands **without any discussion**" – not a walkout or a budget cut.
[4:2] - **Crossing the floor** = changing sides (party); **decorum** = dignified conduct.
[4:3] ~~ Sources: Laxmikanth, Indian Polity p.351, p.353 (src/polity/laxmikanth-indian-polity-01.txt); PMF Polity 2 p.37-38 (src/polity/pmf-polity-02.txt); Rocket Polity Sheet #29 p.74 (src/polity/rocket-polity-01.txt)

## SUB 6 | Rules of Procedure – Discussions and Rule Numbers
[6:0] - Day-to-day work of the Lok Sabha runs on the **Rules of Procedure and Conduct of Business in Lok Sabha**; the Constitution gives only the framework. **No-confidence motion, Zero Hour, Rule 377** are **not in the Constitution**.
[6:1] TABLE head: Rule | Device
  [6:1.0] Rule 184 (LS) | Discussion with a formal motion and voting – governments dislike it, losing may embarrass or topple them (UPPCS A-R: A and R true, R explains A)
  [6:1.1] Rule 193 (LS) | Short-duration (two-hour) discussion – no formal motion, no voting; max 2 hours; since 1953
  [6:1.2] Rule 377 (LS) | Special mention – matters that are not points of order and cannot be raised under questions, calling attention or motions; written notice to the Secretary-General + Speaker's consent
  [6:1.3] Rules 180A–E (RS) | Special Mentions in the Rajya Sabha
  [6:1.4] Rule 198 (LS) | No-confidence motion
  [6:1.5] Rule 222 (LS) / Rule 187 (RS) | Privilege motion
[6:2] - **Rule 377 – matters a day**: **20** in most books and keys (RAS 2018 key); NextGen Rocket says it was raised to **30** a day in 2015.
[6:3] - **Half-an-hour discussion**: a matter already sufficiently debated whose answer needs **factual elucidation**; Speaker can allot 3 days a week; no formal motion or vote.

## SUB 7 | Motions, Privilege Motion, Resolutions and the Whip
[7:0] - **Motion** = a **formal proposal by a member** for the decision of the House (to do something permissible); no discussion on a matter of public importance except on a motion with the Presiding Officer's consent.
[7:1] - Kinds: **substantive** (self-contained, e.g. impeachment), **substitute**, **subsidiary** (ancillary, superseding, amendment).
[7:2] - **Privilege motion**: moved when a **Minister withholds facts of a case or gives wrong / distorted facts** – breach of privilege; purpose: **censure the minister**; **either House**; referred to the **Committee of Privileges**; does **not** force the Government to resign.
[7:3] # Resolutions
[7:4] - Always a **substantive motion**; mover cannot withdraw it except by **leave of the House**; same subject barred for **one year**.
[7:5] TABLE head: Type | Feature
  [7:5.0] Private member's | Alternate Fridays, afternoon sitting
  [7:5.1] Government | Moved by a minister – treaties, policies, committee recommendations
  [7:5.2] Statutory | Moved under a constitutional or statutory provision
[7:6] # The Whip
[7:7] - Office of Whip is **not in the Constitution, the Rules or a statute** – rests on **parliamentary conventions**. The **1998 Act** (Leaders and Chief Whips of Recognised Parties and Groups in Parliament (Facilities) Act) only gives **facilities** – it does not put the office into the Rules.
[7:8] - **Every party** – ruling or opposition – has its own whips (APPSC G1 2024: **only statement III** valid).
[7:9] - Government Chief Whip in the Lok Sabha = the (Cabinet) **Minister of Parliamentary Affairs**, who keeps contact with the Chief Whips of parties in both Houses.
[7:10] - Defying the whip → disqualification under the **Tenth Schedule**.
[7:11] TABLE head: Whip | Meaning
  [7:11.0] One-line | Informative
  [7:11.1] Two-line | Direction to attend
  [7:11.2] Three-line | Vote strictly as the party directs
