=== Charter Act 1813, the Orientalist–Anglicist Debate and Macaulay's Minute
# Steps that led to English education
- **Charter Act 1813:** **Rs 1 lakh a year** for reviving literature, encouraging learned Indians and promoting **modern sciences**; also admitted **missionaries** and ended the Company's India trade monopoly.
  - The grant was **not made available till 1823** – delayed by the debate over how to spend it.
- **General Committee of Public Instruction (GCPI), 1823:** set up to **manage the education grant**; it split into Orientalists and Anglicists.
- Led to English education: **Charter Act 1813 + GCPI 1823 + Orientalist–Anglicist controversy** – all three (UPSC 2018).
# The two camps
| Orientalists | Anglicists |
|---|---|
| Grant for **Sanskrit, Arabic, Persian** (classical) learning, alongside some Western subjects | Spend **exclusively on Western science and literature** |
| Warren Hastings, William Jones, Jonathan Duncan, **H.T. Colebrooke**, H.H. Wilson, Halhed | **T.B. Macaulay** (leader), Bentinck, **Charles Trevelyan**, James Mill |
| In Madras **Thomas Munro** backed pathashalas and **vernacular** schooling | Anglicists were split on the medium: English vs vernaculars |
- **Munro's Survey of Indigenous Education (1826):** a wide network of **vernacular village schools** in the Madras Presidency; his collectorate and tahsildari schools were **closed in 1836** after the 1835 resolution.
# Macaulay's Minute, 2 February 1835
- Written by **Macaulay** as **Law Member** and **President of the GCPI**; approved by **William Bentinck** – "Father of Modern Western Education in India".
- **Settled the row for the Anglicists:** funds for **Western science and literature through English alone**.
- **Remarks:** "**a single shelf of a good European library** was worth the whole native literature of India and Arabia"; all the history in Sanskrit books "less valuable than ... the most paltry abridgement used at preparatory schools in England" (1835).
- **Aim:** a class "**Indian in blood and colour, but English in taste, in opinions, in morals and in intellect**" – **interpreters** between rulers and ruled.
- **Downward Filtration Theory:** educate a few of the upper and middle classes; learning would **filter down** to the masses – an **education** policy (not railways, irrigation or poverty), credited to **Macaulay**; criticised for neglecting mass literacy.
- **English Education Act 1835 (Bentinck):** English the medium of higher education; promotion of oriental institutions **stopped** – so "stipends and printing of classics were to continue" is the **incorrect** statement.
- Called the **foundation of modern education** in India (IAS 1993); English education came in **Bentinck's** reign.
- **Follow-up:** 1837 English replaced Persian as official language; 1844 Hardinge made English knowledge a condition for government jobs.
> Trap: 1835 = **Bentinck + Macaulay**; 1854 = **Charles Wood + Dalhousie**; downward filtration = Macaulay, rejected by Wood.

=== Wood's Despatch 1854 and the Universities of 1857
# The Despatch
- Issued **19 July 1854** by **Sir Charles Wood**, **President of the Board of Control**, under Governor-General **Dalhousie**.
- **"Magna Carta of English Education in India"** – the **first comprehensive plan** and the **most important education document** of British India (APPSC G1 2002; G1 PYQ).
- **Aim:** spread of **European (Western) learning and culture**, with practical benefits over oriental knowledge (UPSC 2003).
# Provisions
- State to take responsibility for **education of the masses** – **rejected downward filtration** (on paper).
- **Graded hierarchy:** vernacular primary schools → Anglo-vernacular high schools → affiliated colleges (district) → **universities** in the presidency towns.
- **Medium:** **English for higher studies**, **vernaculars at school level** – not English at all levels (UPSC 2018: "A and B only").
- **Grants-in-aid** to encourage private (mostly missionary) enterprise.
- Stress on **female education**, **vocational** training and **teachers' training**; education departments in every province; secular education in government institutions.

- **Model:** **University of London** – affiliating and examining universities; Calcutta University came from **Wood's Despatch**, not Hunter or Macaulay.
- Bankim Chandra Chatterjee was among Calcutta's first two graduates (1858).

=== Hunter to Sargent – Later Commissions and Plans
# Education bodies after 1857
| Body | Year | Viceroy / head | Focus |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hunter Commission** (first Indian Education Commission) | **1882** | **Lord Ripon**; W.W. Hunter | Reviewed progress since 1854; **primary education** through vernacular; primary schools to **district and municipal boards**; secondary in literary + vocational streams; female education |
| **Raleigh (Universities) Commission** | **1902** | **Curzon**; Sir Thomas Raleigh; Indian members **Syed Hussain Bilgrami** and **Justice Gurudas Banerjee** | → **Indian Universities Act 1904** – more government control |
| Resolution on Education Policy | 1913 | Hardinge II | Answer to Gokhale's demand for compulsory primary education |
| **Sadler (Calcutta University) Commission** | **1917–19** | Dr M.E. Sadler (VC, Leeds); members Ashutosh Mukherjee, Zia-ud-din Ahmad | **12-year school**, intermediate stage, **3-year degree** |
| **Hartog Committee** | **1929** | Sir Philip Hartog; Simon Commission sub-committee; Irwin (not Wavell) | Growth of education; **wastage and stagnation** in primary schooling |
| **Sargent Plan** | **1944** | Central Advisory Board of Education; Sir John Sargent; Wavell | Free compulsory education **6–14**; target: England's level in 40 years |
| University Education Commission | 1948–49 | S. Radhakrishnan | Moral education – start the day with **silent meditation** |
- **Chronology:** Wood (1854) → Hunter (1882) → Sadler (1917) → Sargent (1944) = "C A B D" (UPPCS 2018).
- **First decentralisation of primary education:** Hunter Commission (control to local boards).
- **Wastage** = leaving school before finishing the primary course; **stagnation** = repeating a class; first described by **Hartog**.
- **3H (Head, Heart, Hand):** keys say **Gandhi** (Nai Talim / basic education); the idea comes from **Pestalozzi**.
- **Madan Mohan Malaviya** (BHU 1916) pressed for **religious education** in Indian universities.

# Coastal Andhra
- **Noble School, Machilipatnam (1843):** Rev. **Robert Noble** (CMS; arrived 1841); college by **1864** – today **Noble College** (not Rajahmundry).
- Central Collegiate Institute, Madras (1841) → forerunner of **Presidency College**.

- English education created a **reformist middle class** (e.g. **Kandukuri Veeresalingam** at Rajahmundry; Vivekavardhini 1874).
- Early centres of English education in Andhra: **Machilipatnam and Rajahmundry**.

=== Aims and Effects of British Education Policy
# Why the British spread modern education
- **Main reason:** a **cheap supply of educated Indians for subordinate posts** in administration and British firms – hence English as the medium.
- Also: pressure from enlightened Indians, missionaries, humanitarian officials; a market for British goods; reconciling Indians to British rule.
# Effects
- English education brought Indians **into contact with Western thought** – science, reason, liberty – which fed the socio-cultural renaissance and nationalism.
- Mass education neglected (illiteracy 84% in 1911); women's and technical education neglected; education a monopoly of upper classes.
# Technical education
- **Engineering College, Roorkee (1847)** – later **Thomason College** – India's **first engineering college**; Calcutta College of Engineering 1856; Poona 1858.
- First medical college: Calcutta, 1835; Curzon set up the agriculture college at **Pusa**.
