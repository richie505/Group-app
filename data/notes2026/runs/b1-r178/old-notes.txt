## SUB 2 | Steps Introducing English Education
[178:2:0] - **Charter Act 1813:** Rs 1 lakh a year for **revival of Indian literature** and **promotion of science**; also ended the India trade monopoly and admitted missionaries.
[178:2:1] - **GCPI 1823:** handled the grant; the debate inside it led to English education.
[178:2:2] - Led to English education: **Charter Act 1813, GCPI 1823 and the Orientalist–Anglicist controversy**.
[178:2:3] - **English Education Act 1835** (Bentinck, on Macaulay's Minute): **English = medium of higher education**.
[178:2:4] - English education introduced under **Lord William Cavendish Bentinck**.
[178:2:5] - 1835 resolution was a **fatal blow** to Madras district/tahsildari vernacular schools.
## SUB 3 | Orientalist–anglicist Debate
[178:3:0] TABLE head: Orientalists | Anglicists
  [178:3:0.0] Wanted the grant for Sanskrit, Arabic, Persian learning | Wanted Western science and literature through English
  [178:3:0.1] Warren Hastings, Sir William Jones, Jonathan Duncan, H.T. Colebrooke, H.H. Wilson | T.B. Macaulay (leader), Bentinck, Charles Trevelyan
  [178:3:0.2] In Madras, Thomas Munro backed pathashalas and vernacular schooling | Western science and English literature
[178:3:1] - Result: Anglicists won in **1835**.
[178:3:2] - **Munro's Survey of Indigenous Education (1826)** found a wide network of **vernacular village schools** in the Madras Presidency.
[178:3:3] - Munro's collectorate and tahsildari schools (2 in Kadapa, 1 Nellore, 2 Vizag by 1830) were **closed in 1836** after the 1835 resolution.
[178:3:4] Thomas Munro supported **vernacular** schooling, not English-only education.
## SUB 4 | Macaulay's Minute, 1835
[178:4:0] - Date: **2 February 1835**; Governor-General **William Bentinck**.
[178:4:1] - Written by **Macaulay** as **Law Member** and **President of the GCPI**; approved by Bentinck, the **"Father of Modern Western Education in India"**.
[178:4:2] - Called the **foundation of modern education** in India.
[178:4:3] - **Recommendation:** education funds **exclusively** for **English-medium teaching of Western science and literature**; higher education put before mass primary schooling.
[178:4:4] - **Famous remark:** "a **single shelf of a good European library** was worth the whole native literature of India and Arabia."
[178:4:5] - **Aim:** a class of Indians to act as **interpreters** between the rulers and the ruled.
[178:4:6] - **Downward Filtration Theory:** education to flow from the **higher classes to the masses**.
[178:4:7] - Criticism: downward filtration **neglected mass literacy**; Hunter and Hartog kept returning to **primary education**.
[178:4:8] **1835 = Bentinck + Macaulay**; **1854 = Charles Wood + Dalhousie**.
## SUB 5 | Wood's Despatch, 1854
[178:5:0] - Issued **19 July 1854** by **Sir Charles Wood**, **President of the Board of Control**, under Governor-General **Dalhousie**.
[178:5:1] - Epithet: **"Magna Carta of English Education in India"**; the most important education document.
[178:5:2] - **Aim:** spread of **Western culture**; foundation of modern education.
[178:5:3] # Provisions
[178:5:4] - **Graded hierarchy:** vernacular primary schools → Anglo-vernacular high schools → affiliated colleges → universities.
[178:5:5] - **Grants-in-aid** to private (mostly **missionary**) enterprise.
[178:5:6] - **English for higher** education; **vernacular for lower**.
[178:5:7] - Universities in the presidency towns; government supervision of vernacular education; **teacher training**.
[178:5:8] - Stressed **female education** and **vocational training**.
[178:5:9] - Asked the Company to take up **mass instruction** – it **rejected downward filtration**.
[178:5:10] # Three Universities of 1857
[178:5:11] TABLE head: University | Place | Year
  [178:5:11.0] Calcutta | Calcutta (Lord Canning) | 1857
  [178:5:11.1] Bombay | Bombay | 1857
  [178:5:11.2] Madras | Madras – 5 September 1857 | 1857
[178:5:12] - Model: **University of London** – affiliating and examining universities.

[178:6:3] Hunter Commission 1882 did **not** deal with famine (Strachey did). The **Hunter Committee 1919** (Disorders Inquiry, **Lord William Hunter**) = **Jallianwala Bagh** and Punjab disturbances.

## SUB 1 | Railways – Purpose, Guarantee System, Effects
[179:1:0] - **Official reasons:** faster movement, communication, administration and **troop movement**.
[179:1:1] - **Colonial economic purpose:** carry **raw materials to ports** and **British goods into Indian markets**.
[179:1:2] - **Guarantee system:** private British companies got a guaranteed **5% return** on capital, **paid from Indian revenues** whether or not lines made a profit.
[179:1:3] TABLE head: Factor | Effect
  [179:1:3.0] Motive | Mainly colonial exploitation
  [179:1:3.1] Drain | Nationalists counted the guaranteed interest in the drain of wealth
  [179:1:3.2] Famine | Railways also moved grain for famine relief
  [179:1:3.3] Unintended effects | National market; shared public travel across castes; faster nationalist mobilisation
[179:1:4] - Vernacular press, railways and telegraph built the **all-India public sphere** for nationalism.
[179:1:5] - **Chanda Railway agitation (1883):** protest in **Hyderabad State** against extending the **Nizam's State Railway** to **Chanda** with a guaranteed return to a London company; **Dr Aghorenath Chattopadhyaya** (Principal, Hyderabad College) called a public meeting and was removed from the State.

## SUB 6 | Vernacular Press Act, 1878
[179:6:0] - **"Gagging Act"** of **Lord Lytton**, passed amid anger over the **1876–77 famine** and the costly **Delhi Durbar (1877)** (⚠ APPSC key (2010) gave Warren Hastings).
[179:6:1] - **Provisions:**
[179:6:2]   - District magistrates could make printers/publishers sign **bonds** not to publish seditious matter.
[179:6:3]   - Demand **security deposits** (forfeitable); **seize presses** for repeat offences.
[179:6:4]   - **No appeal to a court**.
[179:6:5]   - Exemption only by **submitting proofs to a government censor** before publication.
[179:6:6] - **Discriminatory:** applied **only to Indian-language papers**, not English ones.
[179:6:7] - **Evasion:** **Amrita Bazar Patrika** became an **English paper overnight**.
[179:6:8] - **Repeal:** **1882** by **Lord Ripon**; pre-censorship dropped, a **press commissioner** appointed.
[179:6:9] - **Order:** Dramatic Performances Act (1876) → **VPA (1878)** → Bengal Tenancy Act (1885) → North-Western Provinces and Oudh Act.
[179:6:10] - **Order:** Ban on Sati (1829) → Wood's Despatch (1854) → VPA (1878) → Rowlatt Act (1919).

## SUB 9 | Macaulay's Minute and Downward Filtration
[179:9:0] - **Macaulay's Minute (2 Feb 1835):** written as **Law Member** and **President of the GCPI**.
[179:9:1] - **Recommendation:** education funds **exclusively** to **English-medium** teaching of Western science and literature; higher education over mass primary schooling.
[179:9:2] - **Famous remark:** "a **single shelf of a good European library** was worth the whole native literature of India and Arabia" (APPSC G2 2025).
[179:9:3] - **Aim:** a class "**Indian in blood and colour, but English in taste, in opinions, in morals, and in intellect**" – interpreters between rulers and ruled.
[179:9:4] - **Downward Filtration Theory:** educate a small upper class first → learning **filters down** to the masses (related to **education**, APPSC G2 2024); criticised for neglecting mass literacy.
[179:9:5] - **Wood's Despatch rejected downward filtration**; recommended **grants-in-aid** and mass teaching.
[179:9:6] - **Madras impact:** the **1835 resolution banned regional-language instruction** in Madras district schools; Munro's collectorate and tahsildari schools (2 in Kadapa, 1 Nellore, 2 Vizag by 1830) **closed in 1836**.
