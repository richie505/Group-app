## SUB 0 | Winter Season, Retreating and North-East Monsoon, Western Disturbances
[0:0] # Retreating monsoon (October–November/December)
[0:1] - Caused by the **southward movement of the Sun's vertical rays**; the NW low disappears, the **subtropical westerly jet returns south of the Himalayas**, the TEJ disappears.
[0:2] - Withdrawal is gradual (about three months): from the extreme **north-west first** – NextGen/NCERT: from the beginning of **October**; PMF/IMD: from **mid-September** – out of the peninsula by October and from the south-east tip by **December**.
[0:3] - **Fine weather and clear skies**; land temperature rises again → hot, humid "**October heat**".
[0:4] - **Tropical cyclones** are the hallmark: they form mostly in the **Bay of Bengal** (rarely the Arabian Sea), and their frequency peaks in **October–November**; they bring heavy rain to the east coast → **bimodal** rainfall in the south.
[0:5] - The retreating winds blow from land **towards the Bay of Bengal**, pick up moisture there and rain on the Coromandel coast.
[0:6] - About **13%** of annual rainfall comes in this season.
[0:7] > "Declining monsoon is indicated by clear sky, pressure change over the Bay of Bengal and rising temperature on land" – all three (BPSC).
[0:8] # North-east monsoon and Tamil Nadu
[0:9] - The winter high over NW India sends winds NW → SE down the plains; over the Bay of Bengal they become the **north-east monsoon** and pick up moisture.
[0:10] - **Tamil Nadu / Coromandel coast** gets its **main rain in October–December (Nov–Dec)** from the NE monsoon; South Arcot and Ramanathapuram get **over 50%** of their rain then. Also **Rayalaseema, Nellore, south coastal AP, SE Karnataka, SE Kerala**; **not the Gujarat coast**.
[0:11] - **Why Tamil Nadu is dry in the SW monsoon:** it lies in the **rain shadow** of the Western Ghats for the Arabian Sea branch, and the Bay branch blows **parallel to the coast**. So **Chennai** gets the least SW-monsoon rain among Kolkata, Mangalore, Chennai, Delhi.
[0:12] - States **not** fed mainly by the June–September monsoon: **Tamil Nadu** (NE monsoon) and **Jammu & Kashmir** (western disturbances).
[0:13] # Western disturbances
[0:14] - **Temperate (extra-tropical) cyclones from the Mediterranean**, carried east by the westerlies through Iran–Afghanistan–Pakistan into **north-west India**.
[0:15] - Give **10–15 cm** of winter rain to the **north-western plains** – **beneficial for rabi crops (wheat)** – and **snowfall** in J&K, Himachal Pradesh, Uttarakhand.
[0:16] - Winter rain in the NW plains **decreases from west to east** (and from north to south).
[0:17] - **Northern plains' winter rain = western disturbances**, not the NE monsoon (which is active in winter but is not the cause) and not the retreating monsoon.
[0:18] - They are **not** part of the monsoon system (CDS: "not associated with monsoon climate" = western disturbances).
[0:19] # Winter season (December/January–February)
[0:20] - **High pressure over NW India** (NCERT: the **1019 mb** isobar passes through north-west India, **1013 mb** in the far south) → **anticyclonic**, dry, outblowing winds; cold northern plains, cold waves, fog; **no loo or thunderstorms**.
[0:21] - **Anticyclonic conditions** form because the land is **cold and the air dense** – not because of winter rain (UPSC 2001: A true, R false).
[0:22] - Winter is the **driest season (~2%)**: most of India is dry under the **north-east trade winds**, which blow from land to sea.
[0:23] - **Dry winters** (Koeppen Cwg, "monsoon with dry winter"): **Bengal / Ganga plain**; J&K and Punjab–Haryana get western-disturbance rain, Tamil Nadu gets NE-monsoon rain.
[0:24] - **South India:** maritime – **low diurnal and annual range**, high temperature all year, **no extremes**; the 20 °C January isotherm runs from the Tapi mouth to the Mahanadi delta.
[0:25] ~~ Sources: NextGen Indian Geography 07 p.2-4 (book p.86-88); NextGen Indian Geography 06 p.10 (book p.80); PMF Indian Physical Geography 03 p.22, p.32-33; OnlyIAS NCERT Indian Geography 05 p.5-6; app notes 4:38 (sub 7)
## SUB 1 | Monsoon – Meaning, Characteristics and Controls of Indian Climate
[1:0] # Meaning and characteristics
[1:1] - India has a **tropical monsoon** climate; "monsoon" from Arabic **Mausim** = season; **Al Masudi** (Arab geographer) described the seasonal rhythm of winds.
[1:2] - Monsoons are **seasonal (secondary/periodic) winds** that **reverse direction** with the season – **not primary (planetary) winds**.
[1:3] - They are confined to the tropics, roughly **20°N–20°S** (textbook statement).
[1:4] - The distribution of SW-monsoon rain is governed mainly by **relief**.
[1:5] - Monsoon rain is **not steady**, **unevenly (not equally) distributed**, has **breaks** and is **uncertain/variable**.
[1:6] - India is a "**thirsty land**" despite abundant rain: rain is **concentrated in a few months**, **runs off rapidly** and **evaporates quickly** – all three.
[1:7] - Main cause of the monsoon: the **temperature (pressure) contrast between the land and the Indian Ocean**.
[1:8] - **Early maritime links** of India with South-East Asia across the Bay of Bengal were helped by the **monsoon winds** (UPSC 2011).
[1:9] # Controls (factors) of Indian climate
[1:10] - **Latitude** – the **Tropic of Cancer** crosses the middle of India; the south is tropical, the north sub-tropical. India lies about **8°4'–37°6'N**, so it is **not wholly between the tropics** (Jharkhand PCS: A true, R false).
[1:11] - **Himalayas** – block cold Central Asian winds, trap the monsoon and give India its **climatic distinctiveness**.
[1:12] - **Distance from the sea (continentality)**, **altitude**, **relief**, **pressure and winds**, **upper-air circulation (jet streams)**, **ocean currents**, **cyclones/western disturbances**.
[1:13] - CDS 2015: **location, thermal contrast, upper-air circulation and the Himalayas** are all major factors.
[1:14] - **Amritsar and Shimla** lie on nearly the same latitude; Shimla is cooler because of **altitude** (~2,200 m vs ~230 m). Likewise **Ooty vs Coimbatore**, **Agra (16 °C) vs Darjiling (4 °C)** in January.
[1:15] - At a similar latitude **Mumbai** is moderate while **Nagpur** is extreme – **distance from the sea**. In **Kerala and the Andaman Islands** the day–night range is only about **7–8 °C**.
[1:16] > A–R (NDA 2011): "Monsoon winds are driven by the size of the Asian landmass" and "upper-air circulation is associated with the monsoon" – both true, R not the explanation of A.
[1:17] ~~ Sources: NextGen Indian Geography 06 p.8-9, p.13 (book p.78-79, 83); PMF Indian Physical Geography 03 p.18-19; app notes 4:38 (sub 0)
## SUB 2 | Onset, Advance, Branches and Breaks of the South-West Monsoon
[2:0] # Onset and advance
[2:1] - Season: **June–September** (IMD); also stated as **mid-June to mid-September**; gives **~75%** of annual rain.
[2:2] - Sudden onset = "**burst**" in the first week of June; rain in Kerala called **Varsha**.
[2:3] TABLE head: Stage | Normal date
  [2:3.0] Andaman & Nicobar Islands (first Indian territory) | ~25 May
  [2:3.1] Kerala (first state, Malabar coast) | 1 June (2026: 4 June)
  [2:3.2] Bhopal / Delhi | ~15 June / ~25 June
  [2:3.3] Whole country | 8 July (IMD revised normal)
[2:4] - IMD declares the Kerala onset after **10 May** when **60% of 14 stations** get **2.5 mm** on two consecutive days (5-day **pentads** used).
[2:5] - Order of onset: **Kerala → West Bengal → Uttar Pradesh → Rajasthan** (CDS 2010).
[2:6] - Duration: **> 120 days** in the SW peninsula, **< 75 days** in west Rajasthan; the monsoon reaches **west Rajasthan last**.
[2:7] # Arabian Sea branch
[2:8] - Strikes the **Western Ghats** and Malabar first; heaviest rain at **Agumbe, Hulikal** on the crest; the **windward west coast** gets very heavy **orographic** rain because the **Western Ghats** lie across the winds.
[2:9] - **Mumbai** (windward) gets far more than **Pune** (leeward); the eastern side of the Ghats is a **rain-shadow** area.
[2:10] - Enters central India via the **Narmada–Tapi** valleys; one arm runs **parallel to the Aravallis** → scanty rain in Rajasthan.
[2:11] - The **Chhattisgarh Basin** is **not much affected** by the Arabian Sea branch (it gets Bay branch rain).
[2:12] - The **Palghat Gap** lets only part of the Arabian Sea branch through to Tamil Nadu – the **majority** of monsoon winds do **not** enter through it.
[2:13] # Bay of Bengal branch
[2:14] - Strikes Myanmar and the **funnel-shaped Garo–Khasi–Jaintia hills** → orographic rain in **Meghalaya** (Mawsynram, Cherrapunji).
[2:15] - Deflected **westward by the Himalayas** along the Ganga plain → rain **decreases east to west**; **Jharkhand** and the east get their rain from the **SW monsoon**.
[2:16] > APPSC FRO: "Kerala is the first state to get the SW monsoon in early June" and "the Bay branch causes heavy rain in Meghalaya" – **both correct**.
[2:17] # Breaks
[2:18] - A **break** occurs when the **monsoon trough (ITCZ) shifts north to the Himalayan foothills**: the plains stay dry, the hills get heavy rain, and temperature rises.
[2:19] - One or two breaks a season; most often in the **second week of August**.
[2:20] - Rajasthan **does** get some SW monsoon rain (little), so "Rajasthan gets no SW monsoon rain" is false.
[2:21] ~~ Sources: NextGen Indian Geography 06 p.12 (book p.82); NextGen Indian Geography 07 p.1-2 (book p.85-86); PMF Indian Physical Geography 03 p.5, p.29-32; app notes 4:38 (sub 4, 5)
## SUB 3 | Hot Weather Season, Local Storms and the Six Traditional Seasons
[3:0] # Hot weather (pre-monsoon) season – March to May
[3:1] - Northward march of the Sun; over **42.5 °C** in the north and NW; **Ganganagar** recorded about **50 °C** (1935).
[3:2] - **Heat waves** in north India: **March to June** (peak May–June); IMD plains threshold **40 °C**.
[3:3] - **Dust storms (Andhi)** – commonest in **May**, in Rajasthan, Haryana, Punjab, Delhi, western UP.
[3:4] - Pre-monsoon rain (~**10%** of annual) is **convectional**: strong heating → rising air **cools adiabatically** → thunderstorms (CDS 2009 A–R: both true, R explains A).
[3:5] TABLE head: Local storm / wind | Region | Note
  [3:5.0] Mango showers | Kerala and coastal Karnataka (PMF adds Tamil Nadu, AP) | Help mangoes ripen
  [3:5.1] Blossom / cherry blossom showers | Karnataka, Kerala coffee areas | Coffee flowering
  [3:5.2] Kaal Baisakhi / Nor'westers | West Bengal, Assam, Odisha, NE | Violent evening storms; useful for tea, jute, rice
  [3:5.3] Bardoli Chheerha | Assam | Assam's name for the Nor'westers
  [3:5.4] Loo | N and NW plains (Punjab to Bihar) | Hot, dry wind – not rain
  [3:5.5] Karaburan | Central Asia | Hot dusty wind – not Indian
[3:6] - Tornadoes and hailstorms peak in **March–May**; hailstorms are rare in the monsoon; most hail-prone: **Vidarbha and Marathwada**. Deadliest tornado: **Belda, 24 March 1998**.
[3:7] # Seasons
[3:8] TABLE head: IMD season | Months
  [3:8.0] Winter | January–February
  [3:8.1] Pre-monsoon / hot weather | March–May
  [3:8.2] South-west monsoon | June–September
  [3:8.3] Post-monsoon (NE monsoon) | October–December
[3:9] # Six traditional seasons
[3:10] TABLE head: Traditional season | Months
  [3:10.0] Vasanta (Spring) | Chaitra–Vaishakha (March–April)
  [3:10.1] Grishma (Summer) | Jyeshtha–Ashadha (May–June)
  [3:10.2] Varsha (Rainy) | Shravana–Bhadra (July–August)
  [3:10.3] Sharad (Autumn) | Ashvina–Kartika (September–October)
  [3:10.4] Hemanta (pre-winter) | Margashirsha–Pushya (November–December)
  [3:10.5] Shishira (winter) | Magha–Phalguna (January–February)
[3:11] - Order by the Hindu calendar: **Spring → Summer → Rainy → Autumn**. "Pravaha" is not a season. **Nautapa** = nine-day peak of summer heat before the monsoon.

## SUB 4 | Theories of the Monsoon Mechanism, ENSO and Monsoon Research
[4:0] TABLE head: Theory / driver | Core idea
  [4:0.0] Thermal (differential heating) – Edmund Halley, 1686 | Land heats faster than sea → summer low over land draws moist sea winds; reversed in winter
  [4:0.1] Dynamic / ITCZ shift (1950s) | ITCZ moves north over the Ganga plain as the monsoon trough; SE trades cross the equator and turn south-westerly
  [4:0.2] Jet streams | STJ shifts north of the Himalayas → onset; returns south → withdrawal. TEJ (South China Sea → East Africa) coincides with the SW monsoon; Somali jet + Somalia Current strengthen it
  [4:0.3] Tibetan Plateau (~5,000 m) | Summer heat island → upper-air anticyclone, stronger TEJ; heavy snow cover weakens it
  [4:0.4] ENSO / IOD / MJO | El Nino weakens (delayed, weak monsoon, drought); La Nina strengthens; positive IOD can offset El Nino; MJO repeats every 30–60 days
[4:1] - **Mascarene High** (near **Madagascar**) drives the cross-equatorial flow; **dust aerosols** over the Indo-Gangetic Plain help pull the monsoon inland.
[4:2] - **Gilbert Walker** (1920s) described the **Southern Oscillation**: low pressure over the Indian Ocean (Darwin) + high over the Pacific (Tahiti) = good monsoon.
[4:3] - **MONEX** (Monsoon Experiment) **1979** under the **WMO** confirmed the role of the shifting ITCZ (an earlier Indo-Soviet experiment was held in 1973).
[4:4] - **El Nino**: a warm current off **Peru** every **3–7 years** replacing the cold Peruvian current; it **causes drought-like conditions in parts of India**.
[4:5] # Research and forecasting
[4:6] - **IMD** works under the **Ministry of Earth Sciences (MoES)**.
[4:7] TABLE head: Mission | Facts
  [4:7.0] National Monsoon Mission | 2012, MoES, through IITM Pune + IMD; dynamic coupled model (MMCFS)
  [4:7.1] Mission Mausam | 14 Jan 2025 with IMD Vision 2047; Rs 2,000 crore; Bharat Forecast System (6 km, 10-day rain)
[4:8] - **LPA** of SW-monsoon rain (1971–2020) = **868.6 mm**. **Ocean Mean Temperature (OMT)** – upper ocean down to the **26 °C isotherm** – predicts better than **SST**.
[4:9] - **2026 (as of Sep 2026):** April forecast 92% of LPA, revised to 90%; June deficit **43%** → contingency plans; El Nino building.
