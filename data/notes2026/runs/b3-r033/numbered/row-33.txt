## SUB 0 | Disguised Unemployment – Meaning, Test and Where Found
[0:0] - **Meaning:** **more persons are employed on a job than are actually needed** ("a job which a few can accomplish"); the extra workers appear employed but add nothing to output.
[0:1] - **Test:** the **marginal productivity of labour is zero** (or negative) – **withdrawing some workers leaves total output unchanged**, so a shift of people out of agriculture **need not reduce production**.
[0:2] - **Where:** mainly in **agriculture / the primary sector**, on family farms in **rural areas** (also the unorganised sector) – not in industry, factories, services, trade, banking or urban areas.
[0:3] - **NCERT example:** five family members work a two-hectare plot that needs fewer hands; NCERT calls it hidden **underemployment** – people appear to be working but are not fully employed.
[0:4] - **Hidden, not open:** the persons appear employed, so it is **not captured in PLFS headline unemployment rates**.
[0:5] - **Nurkse:** disguised unemployment is a **potential source of saving** – surplus farm labour can be withdrawn for capital formation (roads, irrigation, railways, housing) in under-developed countries.
[0:6] - Distractors: "persons with no jobs" (open), "unemployment of women / of people above 60", "fall in productivity", "change in technology", "data concealment".
[0:7] > Most repeated item of the whole topic (about 25 PYQs, APPSC 1998-2025, UPSC 2013, UPPSC, SSC): meaning = more workers than needed; MPL = zero; found in agriculture (I and II = agriculture + rural). When disguised is not offered, the agriculture answer is **seasonal**.

## SUB 1 | Types of Unemployment – Cause and Where Predominant
[1:0] TABLE head: Type | Cause / feature | Example | Where predominant
  [1:0.0] Seasonal | Work only at certain times of the year | Farm labour idle in off-season months | Agriculture, tourism, agro-industries
  [1:0.1] Disguised | Surplus labour, MPL = 0 | Five family members on a plot needing two | Agriculture, family farms, rural
  [1:0.2] Structural | Demand for labour short of supply; skill / technology mismatch; long-run | Labour-intensive economy turning capital-intensive | India's basic problem
  [1:0.3] Frictional (search) | Time between jobs; workers moving from one job to another | Graduate seeking a first job | Normal; exists even at full employment
  [1:0.4] Cyclical (Keynesian, demand-deficient) | Fall in aggregate demand – recession / depression | Lay-offs in a recession | Developed / capitalist economies
  [1:0.5] Technological | Change in technology, automation; a subtype of structural | Robots replace car workers | Manufacturing and routine services
  [1:0.6] Educated | Qualified persons cannot find matching jobs | – | Urban
  [1:0.7] Institutional | Labour laws, high minimum wages, union rules | Hiring halted due to rigid layoff rules | –
  [1:0.8] Vulnerable | No formal contract or social security | Construction labour, street vending | Informal sector
  [1:0.9] Casual / Classical | Day-to-day contract ends / wages too high (real-wage unemployment) | – | –
[1:1] - **Rural = seasonal + disguised; urban = educated** (NCERT rule). Under PLFS, open unemployment is higher in **urban** areas (AP 2023-24: rural 3.4%, urban 5.9%).
[1:2] - India has **all** types – structural, seasonal, disguised and underemployment ("All the above").
[1:3] - **At full employment** frictional (and structural) unemployment still exist; "functional" in an old APPSC paper is a misprint for **frictional**.
[1:4] - **Capital-intensive industries cause unemployment**; a labour-intensive industry is one that **employs more hands**.
[1:5] - Automation and AI reduce demand mainly for **low-skilled, routine jobs**.
[1:6] - **Structural unemployment** in a developing economy arises from **inadequate productive capacity** (too little capital to employ the growing labour force), not deflation.
[1:7] > Depression → cyclical (APPSC 2019); job-to-job → frictional (SSC); agriculture → disguised, or seasonal when disguised is absent (APPSC 2025).

## SUB 2 | Nature and Causes of Unemployment in India
[2:0] - **India's problem = chronic unemployment and under-employment** of a **structural** kind (demand for labour falls short of supply), with seasonal and disguised forms; **cyclical** unemployment is negligible in India and typical of developed capitalist economies.
[2:1] - **Cause in a developing country:** **lack of complementary factors of production – deficiency of capital**; the rate of capital formation limits jobs for a fast-growing population. Keynes's "lack of effective demand" explains developed economies.
[2:2] - India is a **labour-surplus economy**; Indian agriculture is **land-scarce and labour-surplus**; projected skilled-labour surplus about **245.3 million by 2030**.
[2:3] - **Causes:** rapid **population growth**, **labour immobility**, shift to **capital-intensive** production, **stagnation of the industrial sector**, neglected secondary sector and **MSMEs**, stringent labour laws, **delay in official paperwork**, **slow infrastructure**, import-oriented economy, **jobless growth** led by IT and finance.
[2:4] - About **90%** of the workforce is informal.
[2:5] - Odd ones out: "high growth of labour-intensive manufacturing" and "government aid to the unemployed" do NOT cause unemployment; disguised unemployment, poverty and low productivity hold villages back – the **Panchayat system** does not.
[2:6] - **Latest:** monthly PLFS (CWS) rate **5.5% in May 2026** (5.2% April); **30-35 million** seasonal labourers migrate every year (7 Sep 2026).
[2:7] - **Debate:** a falling rate may reflect **distress entry** into low-productivity work, not real job creation.
[2:8] > APPSC asks the "best description" (chronic unemployment and under-employment) and the cause (deficiency of capital / complementary factors); Keynes's effective demand is the developed-country answer.
[2:9] ~~ Sources: NextGen Indian Economy - APPSC 18 p.12; NextGen Indian Economy - APPSC 19 p.1, p.7-8; NextGen Indian Economy Part 1 - APPSC 02 p.19; app notes 3:33 sub 8
## SUB 3 | Theories – Keynes, Classical, Phillips, Friedman, Okun
[3:0] TABLE head: Economist / school | Idea
  [3:0.0] J.M. Keynes | Unemployment in developed economies = deficiency of effective demand; macro equilibrium is an under-employment, short-run equilibrium
  [3:0.1] Classical | Assumes full employment; wage cuts reduce unemployment; full employment is consistent with seasonal and frictional unemployment
  [3:0.2] A.W. Phillips (1958) | Inverse relation between inflation and unemployment (Phillips curve; Economica 1958, UK data 1861-1957)
  [3:0.3] Milton Friedman | Natural rate of unemployment hypothesis
  [3:0.4] Friedman-Phelps / monetarists | Long-run Phillips curve is vertical at the natural rate (NAIRU)
  [3:0.5] Arthur Okun | 1 point of unemployment above the natural rate ≈ 2% fall in output below potential GDP
[3:1] - **Natural rate = frictional + structural** unemployment, not cyclical.
[3:2] - **Stagflation** (high inflation + high unemployment, 1970s USA) broke the Phillips trade-off.
[3:3] > Phillips = inflation; Okun = output; Friedman = natural rate; Keynes = effective demand.
[3:4] ~~ Sources: NextGen Indian Economy - APPSC 18 p.12; NextGen Indian Economy - APPSC 19 p.8; Ramesh Singh Indian Economy 10th ed. ch.7 p.118-119; app notes 3:33 sub 2, 6
## SUB 4 | Educated Unemployment and Youth
[4:0] - **Urban** phenomenon; cause: **curriculum mismatch** with industry needs.
[4:1] - **Educated unemployment can rise with high economic growth** when professional / skilled education is lacking (A and R both true, R explains A).
[4:2] - **Kerala** has the highest percentage of educated unemployed (**K.P. Kannan**); Kerala also topped the HDI (IHDR 2011) and had the highest unemployment rate (Census 2011), but the jobless rate does not explain the HDI.

## SUB 5 | Underemployment and Other Classifications
[5:0] TABLE head: Term | Meaning
  [5:0.0] Open | Able and willing to work, no job at all
  [5:0.1] Voluntary | Unwilling to work at the prevailing wage (generous benefits, high marginal tax, waiting for a better job)
  [5:0.2] Involuntary | Willing at the going wage but no job – the labour force counts only involuntary unemployment
  [5:0.3] Visible underemployment | Fewer than normal hours of work
  [5:0.4] Invisible underemployment | Full-time but very low income or below ability (MBA working as a driver)
  [5:0.5] Long-term | Unemployed for more than 12 months
  [5:0.6] Unemployment trap | Benefits discourage taking up work
  [5:0.7] Chronic | Long-term unemployment persisting in the economy
[5:1] - **Disguised ≠ underemployment** in strict use: underemployment = not using full skills or hours; disguised = surplus labour with zero marginal output (NCERT uses "underemployment" loosely for both).
