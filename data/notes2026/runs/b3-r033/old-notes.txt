## SUB 1 | Disguised Unemployment
[33:1:0] - More workers than needed; **marginal productivity of labour is zero** (or negative).
[33:1:1] - **Identification:** withdrawing some workers leaves **total output unchanged**.
[33:1:2] - Appear employed but add nothing; common among family members in agriculture.
[33:1:3] - A **hidden** (not open) type; **not captured in PLFS headline rates**.
[33:1:4] Extra labour with zero output = **disguised unemployment**, not underemployment.
## SUB 2 | Cyclical Unemployment
[33:2:0] - Cause: fall in aggregate demand during **recession/depression**; firms lay off workers to cut costs.
[33:2:1] - "Unemployment caused by depression in an economy" = **Cyclical** (APPSC 2019).
[33:2:2] - Keynes: unemployment in developed economies = **deficiency in effective demand**.
## SUB 3 | Frictional vs Structural
[33:3:0] TABLE head: Feature | Frictional (search) | Structural
  [33:3:0.0] Duration | Short-term | Long-term
  [33:3:0.1] Cause | Time lag while searching/changing jobs | Skill-job mismatch; technology or demand change
  [33:3:0.2] Nature | Normal; exists even at full employment | Deep-rooted; needs reskilling/reform
  [33:3:0.3] Example | Worker quits to find a better job | Labour-intensive economy turning capital-intensive
[33:3:1] - At **full employment**, frictional and structural unemployment may still exist.
## SUB 4 | Technological Unemployment
[33:4:0] - Job loss due to change in technology; a **subtype of structural** unemployment.
[33:4:1] - **Automation** in manufacturing and **AI** in services may reduce demand for low-skilled, routine jobs.

## SUB 8 | Nature of India's Unemployment
[33:8:0] - India's basic problem: **structural** (labour demand < supply), with **seasonal, disguised and underemployment**; developed countries: mainly **cyclical** (demand-deficient).
[33:8:1] - India = **labour-surplus** economy; projected skilled-labour surplus about **245.3 million by 2030**.
[33:8:2] - Labour-intensive industry employs more hands.
[33:8:3] # Causes
[33:8:4] - Rapid **population growth**, **labour immobility**, shift to **capital-intensive** production.
[33:8:5] - **Jobless growth**: growth driven by IT and finance, not labour-intensive manufacturing.
[33:8:6] - Neglected secondary sector, less focus on **MSMEs**, stringent labour laws, slow infrastructure, import-oriented economy.
[33:8:7] - About **90%** workforce informal; low female LFPR.
[33:8:8] Odd-one-out: "high growth of labour-intensive manufacturing" is NOT a cause; it would reduce unemployment.

=== NOTES 3:34 | Measurement of Unemployment - Ups, Upss, Cws, Cds; Plfs Methodology and Recent Findings
## SUB 0 | Status Approaches
[34:0:0] # The Four Approaches
[34:0:1] TABLE head: Approach | Reference period / unit | Counted employed if | Captures
  [34:0:1.0] UPS (Usual Principal Status) | 365 days | Worked major time (generally 183+ days) | Chronic (long-term)
  [34:0:1.1] UPSS (Principal + Subsidiary) | 365 days | Also subsidiary work for at least 30 days | Broadest worker count
  [34:0:1.2] CWS (Current Weekly Status) | 7 days | Worked at least 1 hour on any day | Chronic + seasonal
  [34:0:1.3] CDS (Current Daily Status) | Each day of the week; unit = half day | 4+ hours = employed for the day | Daily/intermittent; highest rate
[34:0:2] - **CDS gives the highest rate**: counts days of unemployment even of persons employed under CWS.

## SUB 1 | Jobless Growth After 1991
[35:1:0] - Growth led by **capital-intensive** IT and finance; job creation not concentrated in **labour-intensive manufacturing**.
[35:1:1] - Manufacturing stage skipped ("missing middle").
[35:1:2] - Employment growth lagged labour-force growth (creeping unemployment, mostly unorganised sector).
[35:1:3] - GDP grew 6-7% but formal jobs lagged; about **12 million** enter the job market yearly (7-10 million young Indians a year, 15 May 2026).
[35:1:4] - Direct factory employment fell **61% → 47%** (2011-2023); contract workers rose to **42%** (13 Mar 2026).
