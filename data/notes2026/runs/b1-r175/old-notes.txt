## SUB 2 | Permanent Settlement – Features and Rationale
[175:2:0] - Also called the **Zamindari system**; zamindars recognised as **proprietors**; rights **hereditary, transferable, saleable**.
[175:2:1] - Began as a **decennial (10-year) settlement**, made **permanent in 1793**.
[175:2:2] - **Rent share:** State **10/11**, zamindar **1/11** of rent collected.
[175:2:3] - **Sunset Law:** revenue due by **sunset of the due date**, or the estate was auctioned.
[175:2:4] - **Reasons for fixed, high demand:** sure income, low collection cost, a **loyal landlord class**, hope of land improvement. It did **not** let the Company gain from later price rises.
[175:2:5] - Many zamindars **did not issue pattas**; no official check on them – they only paid 10/11 of collections.
[175:2:6] - **Gains from agriculture went to the zamindars**, not the Company or the cultivators (APPSC 2022).
[175:2:7] - **Litigation:** more litigation after 1793 was traced to Cornwallis's **judicial reforms**, especially **abolition of court fees**; zamindars sued tenants.
[175:2:8] - **Haftam (1799)** and **Panjam (1812)** regulations let zamindars **seize tenants' property**.
[175:2:9] - **Bengal Tenancy Act, 1885:** rights of landlords and tenants in **Bengal and Bihar**.
## SUB 3 | Ryotwari – Features
[175:3:0] - Ryot = **proprietor**; paid revenue **directly to Government**; pattas given; lands **surveyed and assessed** before taxing.
[175:3:1] - Could **sell, mortgage, gift or lease** land while assessment was paid; not evicted while revenue paid.
[175:3:2] - First demand: **50% of estimated produce** (not 30%).
[175:3:3] - Assessment fixed on **land, not crop**; revised about every **30 years** (two separate features).
[175:3:4] - **No automatic exemption** after a bad harvest; occasional remission or reduction possible.
[175:3:5] - Munro: "The Ryotwari is a system which has **always prevailed in India**."
[175:3:6] - Criticism: State became the **"ultimate landlord"**; over-assessment, poor records, unclear boundaries; revenue often heavy, not fixed low.

## SUB 6 | Mahalwari and Taluqdari
[175:6:0] - **Unit:** the **mahal** (village or estate) – the whole village as one unit; designed by Mackenzie for the **Bengal Presidency** (NWP).
[175:6:1] - Revenue fixed for the village, collected through the **headman / village community**; each cultivator liable for his share; periodically revised.
[175:6:2] - Common ownership: **"Bhai Chara"** or **"Mahali"**.
[175:6:3] TABLE head: Official | Role
  [175:6:3.0] Holt Mackenzie | Designed the system, 1822
  [175:6:3.1] R.M. Bird | Revised it (Regulation IX of 1833); survey-based; "father of land settlement in north India"
  [175:6:3.2] James Thomason | Carried on Bird's settlement in the NWP (Lt.-Governor)
[175:6:4] **Taluqdari = Awadh (Oudh)**; "Taluqdari – Bombay" is the wrong pair.

## SUB 8 | Effects and Economic Impact
[175:8:0] # Zamindari Areas
[175:8:1] - Old estates **sold for arrears** → new landed class of **urban merchants and moneylenders**.
[175:8:2] - **Absentee landlordism** (zamindars moved to Calcutta, left agents), **sub-infeudation, rack-renting**; peasants became **tenants-at-will**.
[175:8:3] - No investment in land; cultivation rose after 1800 but fixed demand gave the Company nothing extra.
[175:8:4] # Ryotwari and Mahalwari Areas
[175:8:5] - **Rigid cash revenue** even when crops failed → ryots borrowed from **Mahajans**, mortgaged or lost land; **land alienation**.
[175:8:6] - No famine remission, unlike Mughal **zabt** (one-third share, taccavi loans); Mahalwari's collective liability broke village solidarity.
[175:8:7] - American Civil War cotton slump → defaults → land passed to moneylenders.
[175:8:8] TABLE head: Factor | Effect
  [175:8:8.0] Commercialisation | Cash revenue forced indigo, cotton, opium, jute
  [175:8:8.1] Land | Became a saleable/transferable commodity
  [175:8:8.2] New classes | Merchant-landlords (Zamindari areas); moneylender-landlords (Ryotwari and Mahalwari areas)
  [175:8:8.3] Result | Loyal landlord class + rural indebtedness → peasant revolts
[175:8:9] - **Alice Thorner:** Ryotwari **destroyed the village system** and separated farmers from the government.

=== NOTES 1:176 | Economic Impact of Colonial Rule – Drain of Wealth, Deindustrialisation, Commercialisation of Agriculture
## SUB 0 | Stages of Colonialism
[176:0:0] TABLE head: Stage | Period | Feature
  [176:0:0.0] Mercantilist | 1757–1813 | Company trade monopoly; direct plunder; Bengal revenue buys export goods
  [176:0:0.1] Free trade / industrial capital | 1813–1858 | India a market for British goods and source of raw materials
  [176:0:0.2] Finance capital | 1858 onwards | British capital in railways, plantations, banks
[176:0:1] - Proposed by **R.P. Dutt** in ***India Today***, drawing on **Karl Marx**. R.P. Dutt is a later Marxist writer, not an original drain theorist.
[176:0:2] - **1813** opened the free-trade stage; finance-capital stage began **1858**.
[176:0:3] - India changed from a **leading exporter of manufactures** to a **supplier of raw materials**.
## SUB 1 | Economic Nationalists and the Drain Theory
[176:1:0] # Thinkers and Books
[176:1:1] TABLE head: Thinker | Work / idea
  [176:1:1.0] Dadabhai Naoroji ("Grand Old Man", "father of Indian Nationalism") | *Poverty and Un-British Rule in India* (1901); drain first stated in *England's Debt to India* (1867); coined "moral drain"; first estimated per capita income: Rs 20 (1867–68)
  [176:1:1.1] R.C. Dutt | *Economic History of India* (2 vols, 1901–03)
  [176:1:1.2] M.G. Ranade | *Essays on Indian Economics*; Indian school of economics, industrialisation
  [176:1:1.3] William Digby | *'Prosperous' British India* (1901)
  [176:1:1.4] G. Subramania Iyer | *Some Economic Aspects of British Rule in India* (1903); India as a supplier of cheap raw materials
  [176:1:1.5] Gajula Lakshmi Narasu Setti | Paper *The Crescent* (reported Andhra famines); founded Chennapatnam Swadeshi Sangam (1852)
[176:1:2] - Drain theory **popularised by Naoroji** (APPSC 2025); also argued by R.C. Dutt and Dinshaw Wacha.
[176:1:3] - **Poverty of India: Papers and Statistics:** also by **Dadabhai Naoroji** (from his 1876 paper 'The Poverty of India'), not R.C. Dutt
[176:1:4] - Drain theory = the **Moderates' greatest contribution**; base of **Swadeshi** and early nationalism.
## SUB 2 | Drain of Wealth – Meaning and Components
[176:2:0] - **Meaning:** transfer of India's wealth to England **without adequate economic or material return**.
[176:2:1] - **Began** with the **Diwani of Bengal (1765)**.
[176:2:2] - India generally had a **favourable balance of trade**; the drain took the form of **unrequited exports** – the export surplus funded British **office and war expenses**, with no matching gold or silver.
[176:2:3] TABLE head: Component (Home Charges and more) | What it covered
  [176:2:3.0] Interest | On the public debt raised in Britain
  [176:2:3.1] Salaries and pensions | Of British civil and military officials, remitted home
  [176:2:3.2] Guaranteed railway returns | 5% assured to British investors, whether lines earned or not
  [176:2:3.3] Military expenditure | British Indian Army and imperial wars (Afghanistan, Burma) – the largest single budget head
  [176:2:3.4] Profits | Of British capitalists, merchants and banks
  [176:2:3.5] Unequal trade | Also a component
[176:2:4] - **Cause per Naoroji:** **profits taken by British capitalists from India** (APPSC 2024).
[176:2:5] The drain was **NOT** an excess of imports.
[176:2:6] - **Effects:** stifled **capital formation** and industrialisation.
[176:2:7] - **Moderates demanded:** cuts in **military spending**, lower **land revenue**, end of **salt tax**, **protective tariffs**.
## SUB 3 | Forms of Exploitation – Free Trade and Tariffs
[176:3:0] - **Charter Act 1813** ended the Company's monopoly → **one-way free trade**: British machine goods entered India **almost duty-free**; Indian goods faced **heavy duties in Britain**.
[176:3:1] - **Imperial preference:** special privileges (lower duties) for British imports in India.
[176:3:2] - **Cotton excise duty (1894–96):** on Indian mill cloth to **offset the import duty** on British goods – classic tariff-policy example.
[176:3:3] - **Railways** carried raw materials out and British goods inland; built also for **military mobility**, yet integrated the economy.
[176:3:4] - Suez Canal (**1869**) tightened British control of trade.
[176:3:5] - Karl Marx: "British Economic Policy is disgusting in India."
## SUB 4 | Deindustrialisation
[176:4:0] # Chronology
[176:4:1] - Weavers coerced by Company agents (**Bolt, 1772**).
[176:4:2] - Lancashire cloth in Bengal **1786**; calicoes displaced by **1793**.
[176:4:3] - 1813 one-way free trade → by **1850** India took **one-fourth** of all British cotton exports.
[176:4:4] - **Share of world manufactures:** India about **24.5% in 1750** → under **2% by 1900** (Paul Bairoch)
[176:4:5] # Causes (B.D. Basu's list)
[176:4:6] TABLE head: Cause | How it hit Indian industry
  [176:4:6.0] Trade policy | Free trade forced on India; flood of cheap machine goods; tariffs in Britain
  [176:4:6.1] Raw materials | Export of raw cotton
  [176:4:6.2] Duties | Internal transit and customs duties
  [176:4:6.3] British privileges | Special privileges for British traders; Company monopolies
  [176:4:6.4] Railways | Opened markets to British goods
  [176:4:6.5] Industrial Revolution | In the first half of the 19th century Indian handicrafts were ruined
[176:4:7] # Results
[176:4:8] - Town handicrafts suffered a **"sudden and complete collapse"**.
[176:4:9] - Marx: the British **"inundated the very mother country of cotton with cottons"**.
[176:4:10] - Artisans pushed onto land → **overcrowding of agriculture**, disguised unemployment.
[176:4:11] - **No independent industrial development** – absence of heavy industries; India kept as raw-material supplier.
[176:4:12] - **Trade secrets:** B.D. Basu also listed Indian artisans being forced to reveal their trade secrets as a cause of the ruin of Indian industry.

[177:4:0] - **Company period:** government **never adopted a famine code**; minimal relief.
