## SUB 0 | Measurement – Magnitude, Intensity, Scales and Instruments
[0:0] TABLE head: Feature | Magnitude | Intensity
  [0:0.0] Measures | Energy released at the focus (from the amplitude of seismic waves) | Degree of shaking and damage observed at a place
  [0:0.1] Nature | Quantitative; one value per earthquake | Qualitative; varies from place to place, falls with distance from the epicentre
  [0:0.2] Scale | Richter (Charles F. Richter, 1935); now Moment Magnitude (Mw) | Modified Mercalli (MMI), MSK-64, old Rossi-Forel – all I to XII
[0:1] - **Richter scale:** logarithmic, based on the **amplitude of seismic waves**; each whole number = **10× amplitude** and about **31–32× energy** (two steps ≈ 1,000×) – **not** a hundredfold rise in energy (UPSC 2001).
[0:2] - **Moment magnitude (Mw):** based on the **rupture area of the fault** and the size of the waves; preferred over Richter for large quakes (Richter works best for regional quakes up to ~M5).
[0:3] - **"Richter measures intensity"** is strictly wrong (Richter = magnitude, Mercalli = intensity), but when the options offer only Richter as a seismic scale, the keyed answer is Richter (APPSC 2017, UKPSC 2002, WBCS 2020). When both are offered, intensity = **Mercalli** (APPSC 2018).
[0:4] - **Mercalli scale** → measures **earthquakes** (intensity, from observed effects) – not cyclones, floods or tornadoes. **Mercator** is a map projection, not a seismic scale.
[0:5] - **MSK-64** (Medvedev–Sponheuer–Karnik) – the intensity scale recommended in **1964** at the UNESCO intergovernmental meeting; used in India (BIS zones are defined in MSK).
[0:6] TABLE head: Magnitude | Effect (PMF / USGS)
  [0:6.0] below 3.0 | Micro/minor – recorded, rarely felt, no damage
  [0:6.1] 3.0–3.9 | Often felt, very rarely damage
  [0:6.2] 4.0–4.9 | Light – felt by most, none to minimal damage
  [0:6.3] 5.0–5.9 | Moderate – damages poorly built houses
  [0:6.4] 6.0–6.9 | Strong (Latur 1993 6.2–6.4)
  [0:6.5] 7.0–7.9 | Major (Bhuj 2001 Mw 7.7)
  [0:6.6] 8.0 + | Great (Assam–Tibet 1950 8.6; Chile 1960 9.5)
[0:7] # Instruments
[0:8] TABLE head: Instrument | Use
  [0:8.0] Seismograph | Detects and records seismic waves; the readings give magnitude (and, in the books, "the device for intensity")
  [0:8.1] Seismometer | The sensor (pendulum / spring mass) inside a seismograph
  [0:8.2] Seismogram | The recording itself
  [0:8.3] Seismoscope | Only shows that a tremor occurred and its time
  [0:8.4] Tiltmeter / Creepmeter | Ground tilt / slow slip (creep) along a fault
[0:9] > Most-asked angle: Richter = magnitude, Mercalli/MSK = intensity, seismograph = recording device.

## SUB 1 | Focus, Epicentre and Basic Terms
[1:0] TABLE head: Term | Meaning
  [1:0.0] Focus / hypocentre | Point inside the Earth where the earthquake originates – energy is released here
  [1:0.1] Epicentre | Point on the surface directly above the focus; first surface point to receive the waves; intensity and damage are greatest here
  [1:0.2] Isoseismal line | Joins places of equal intensity
  [1:0.3] Homoseismal (coseismal) line | Joins places where the shock arrives at the same time (CDS 2014)
  [1:0.4] Seismology | Branch of geology that studies earthquakes; the scientist = seismologist
[1:1] - **John Milne** = **Father of Modern Seismology** (horizontal seismograph).
[1:2] - Wrong statement traps: "epicentre is directly **below** the focus"; "the point of origin is the epicentre".
[1:3] - **Depth of focus:** shallow **0–70 km**, intermediate 70–300 km, deep 300–700 km. **Shallow-focus** quakes are the **most damaging** (energy hits a small area); deep quakes spread energy over a wide area.
[1:4] - Foreshock (before), aftershocks (after), swarm (many small quakes); **seismic doublet** = two independent quakes of similar magnitude close in time and place.
[1:5] - Rare continental-mantle quakes start 80+ km below the Moho; **Stanford** made the first global map of them. **26 Jun 2026**: USGS-described doublet at **Caracas, Venezuela** (M 7.1 and 7.5) (app CA).
[1:6] ~~ Sources: PMF Physical Geography 02 p.90, 92-93 (book pp.177-180, focus, epicentre, isoseismic line, depth classes); NextGen Rocket Disaster Management p.12, 25; NCERT Class 11 Fundamentals of Physical Geography ch.3; app notes 4:10 sub 0
## SUB 2 | Earthquake as a Disaster – Classification and Prediction
[2:0] - An earthquake is a **natural**, **geological** disaster – **not** an atmospheric one (blizzards, lightning, hailstorms are atmospheric). War, industrial and train accidents are man-made.
[2:1] - **High Powered Committee (J.C. Pant, 1999)** – "Geologically related disasters": **landslides and mudflows, earthquakes, dam failures/dam bursts, mine fires**. Cattle epidemics = biological. **Sea surge / storm surge** = hydro-meteorological, **not** geological.
[2:2] - **Prediction:** earthquakes **cannot yet be predicted accurately** (time, place, magnitude); science only gives **probabilities** of future events from data. Thunderstorms, floods, hailstorms can be forecast to an extent.
[2:3] - Possible precursors studied: foreshocks, changes in well-water and **oil-well flow** (pore pressure rising as tectonic stress builds – NDA 2013), radon emission, animal behaviour.
[2:4] - **Tremors** are not caused only by earthquakes – a **meteor impact**, volcanic eruption, explosion or collapse can also shake the ground.
[2:5] ~~ Sources: NextGen Rocket Disaster Management p.25, 40, 47-48 (geological disaster, hazard classes); PMF Internal Security & DM 02 p.44 (natural vs anthropogenic); HPC Report 2001 (PreventionWeb); NCERT Class 11 Fundamentals of Physical Geography ch.5 (prediction); app notes 4:10 sub 0
## SUB 3 | Causes and Types of Earthquakes
[3:0] TABLE head: Type | Cause | Example
  [3:0.0] Tectonic | Sudden release of stress along a fault at plate boundaries – the main cause (tectonism) | Himalaya; San Andreas (transform)
  [3:0.1] Volcanic | Magma movement, eruptions; usually milder | Ring of Fire volcanoes
  [3:0.2] Reservoir-induced | Water load and seepage reactivate an old fault | Koyna 1967 (M 6.3), Maharashtra
  [3:0.3] Intraplate | Hidden old faults in the stable shield | Latur (Killari) 1993, Jabalpur 1997, Bhuj (Kutch)
  [3:0.4] Human-induced (other) | Mining, fracking, nuclear tests; low magnitude | –
[3:1] - Earthquakes are most likely **near faults**. UPSC 2004: **all four** – development of a fault, movement along a fault, volcanic eruption, folding of rocks – can cause earthquakes.
[3:2] - **Koyna**: dam on an **old fault plane**, activated by changes in reservoir water level → region likely to become more quake-prone (R explains A).
[3:3] - **Latur 1993**: far from any plate boundary → quakes can occur in **stable areas along hidden faults**.
[3:4] - Denudation, rotation and revolution do **not** cause earthquakes. San Andreas movement is tectonic, not induced.
[3:5] - **AP**: reservoir-triggered tremors possible near **Nagarjuna Sagar, Srisailam, Polavaram** and old Eastern Ghats faults.
[3:6] ~~ Sources: PMF Physical Geography 02 p.91-92 (book pp.178-179, fault zones, reservoir-induced Koyna); NCERT Class 11 Fundamentals of Physical Geography ch.3; NextGen Rocket Disaster Management p.26; app notes 4:10 sub 1

## SUB 6 | Effects – Tsunami, Landslides, Liquefaction
[6:0] - **Tsunami** (Japanese word) = large waves from sudden **displacement of the sea floor**; most commonly caused by **undersea (sea) earthquakes**; also submarine landslides, volcanic eruptions, explosions, meteor impacts – **not** heavy rain, wind storms or deserts.
[6:1] - Tsunamigenic conditions: magnitude above ~6.5, **shallow focus** (< ~70 km), **vertical** sea-floor movement; over 80% from megathrust subduction quakes. For a tsunami to hit the **Indian coast** the quake must be **above M 7**. **Not all** major earthquakes are tsunamigenic; slow-rupture ("tsunami earthquakes") make **larger** tsunamis than their magnitude suggests.
[6:2] - India's tsunami warning: **ITEWC at INCOIS, Hyderabad** (set up after the **2004** tsunami).
[6:3] TABLE head: Effect | Where / why
  [6:3.0] Ground shaking and rupture | Collapse of buildings, dams, bridges; ~95% of quake deaths in poorly built 1–3 storey houses
  [6:3.1] Landslides | Fragile Himalaya and North-East
  [6:3.2] Liquefaction | Water-saturated soft soils – Indo-Gangetic Plain
  [6:3.3] Fires | Broken gas and power lines (San Francisco 1906, Tokyo 1923)
  [6:3.4] Floods, seiches | Dam damage; standing waves in lakes and reservoirs
[6:4] ~~ Sources: NextGen Rocket Disaster Management p.10-12 (tsunami, ITEWC); PMF Physical Geography 02 p.102 (book p.189, effects); INCOIS / NDMA tsunami FAQ (M > 7 for the Indian coast); app notes 4:10 sub 6

## SUB 8 | Seismic Waves
[8:0] TABLE head: Wave | Arrival | Motion | Medium
  [8:0.0] P (primary) | First – fastest (~1.7× S) | Longitudinal, push-pull | Solid, liquid, gas
  [8:0.1] S (secondary) | Second | Transverse | Solids only
  [8:0.2] Surface (L): Love + Rayleigh | Last – slowest | Love side to side; Rayleigh rolling | Surface only; most damage
[8:1] - Most surface damage = **R & L (Rayleigh and Love) waves**; P-waves are the least destructive.
[8:2] - **S-wave shadow zone** beyond ~**103–105°** → **outer core is liquid**; P-wave shadow 103°–142°.
