=== NOTES 4:85 | Industrial Location – Factors and Theories (weber); World Industrial Regions
## SUB 0 | Factors of Industrial Location
[85:0:0] TABLE head: Factor | Pull | Example
  [85:0:0.0] Raw material (weight-losing) | Near ore or coalfield | Iron and steel, sugar
  [85:0:0.1] Market | Near consumers or ports | Japan's market-based steel at the coast
  [85:0:0.2] Power, labour, transport, capital, policy | Hydropower, skilled labour, ports, government support | Northern Italy, Pearl River Delta
[85:0:1] - **Steel**: about **4 tonnes** of inputs (iron ore, coking coal, limestone, dolomite) → **1 tonne** of steel, so plants sit on coalfields or ore fields.
[85:0:2] - **Japan**: **market-based** iron and steel; imports ore and coal in big ships, so plants are at **ports/coast** (mostly the southern coastal region; some at Kamaishi in NE Honshu and Muroran).
## SUB 1 | Weber's Least-cost Theory (1909)
[85:1:0] - **Alfred Weber**: firm locates where total **transport + labour + agglomeration** costs are least.
[85:1:1] TABLE head: Term | Meaning
  [85:1:1.0] Locational triangle | 2 raw-material sources + 1 market; least-transport-cost point lies inside it
  [85:1:1.1] Material Index (MI) | Weight of localised raw materials / weight of finished product
  [85:1:1.2] Isodapanes | Lines of equal extra transport cost; labour or agglomeration savings pull a firm off the least-transport point
[85:1:2] - **MI > 1** → locate at raw material; **MI < 1** → at market.
[85:1:3] TABLE head: Material type | Meaning | Example
  [85:1:3.0] Ubiquities | Available everywhere | Water, air
  [85:1:3.1] Pure localised | Lose no weight | Cotton
  [85:1:3.2] Gross (weight-losing) | Lose weight in processing | Iron ore, coal, sugarcane
[85:1:4] Cotton is non-weight-losing, so textile mills are NOT tied to raw-cotton sites.
## SUB 2 | Other Location Theorists
[85:2:0] TABLE head: Theorist | Idea
  [85:2:0.0] Alfred Weber | Least cost
  [85:2:0.1] Edgar Hoover | Transport-cost structure, terminal costs; favours ports and break-of-bulk points
  [85:2:0.2] Walter Isard | Substitution principle (trade-off between transport and other costs)
  [85:2:0.3] August Losch | Maximum-profit / market-area (demand) approach
  [85:2:0.4] P. Sargant Florence | Location Quotient (a region's share of an industry vs its national share)
## SUB 3 | Footloose, Sunrise, Umland and Agglomeration
[85:3:0] - **Footloose**: not tied to raw material or a fixed place; light, high-value, low transport cost, low pollution – **diamond cutting** (APPSC 2019 answer), electronics, software, watch-making.
[85:3:1] - **Sunrise industry**: information technology, electronics.
[85:3:2] - **Umland**: area surrounding a town, linked by common economic and cultural activities; term first used in **1883**.
[85:3:3] - **Agglomeration economies**: cost savings when firms cluster – shared infrastructure, specialised suppliers, skilled labour pool. They do NOT tie footloose industries to raw materials.

## SUB 7 | Leading Producers and the Shift to Asia
[85:7:0] - **Steel**: **China 1st** (over 50% of world steel); **India 2nd** – 168.4 MT crude steel; capacity about 220 MT; target **300 MT by 2030**.
[85:7:1] - Leading cotton textile producer: **China** (2025).
[85:7:2] - **Shipbuilding (2025 new orders)**: **China** first (about 60–66%), **South Korea** second (about 20%), **Japan** third.
[85:7:3] - India: Rourkela = first public-sector plant (German help); **VISL Bhadravati** = oldest public-sector steel plant in the south.
[85:7:4] - **Shift to Asia**: China and India are the top two steel producers; old Western regions (Ruhr, Lorraine, US Rust Belt) declined.
[85:7:5] - **Asian Tigers** (newly industrialising): South Korea, Taiwan, Singapore, Hong Kong.
[85:7:6] - Revised Index of Core Industries, base **2022-23**; iron ore added → **nine** industries (July 2026).

## SUB 2 | Locational Factors of Steel Plants
[87:2:0] - Raw materials are **weight-losing**, so plants sit on the **Chota Nagpur** coal-ore belt (Jharkhand, Odisha, Chhattisgarh, West Bengal).
[87:2:1] - **Coastal sites** (Vizag) use imported coking coal; domestic coking coal is **high-ash**.
[87:2:2] - Plants put away from raw materials for regional balance: **Salem, Visakhapatnam, Vijayanagar**.
## SUB 3 | Raw Materials, Process Mix and Mini Steel
[87:3:0] - Process mix (8 Sep 2026): **43% BF-BOF, 22% EAF, 35% induction furnace**; BF-BOF share projected at 56% by 2030.
[87:3:1] - Mini/secondary units melt scrap or **sponge iron** in EAF and induction furnaces.
[87:3:2] - India = **leading producer of sponge iron (DRI)**.
[87:3:3] - **Leading sponge iron states**: **Odisha** (1st) and **Chhattisgarh** (2nd), then West Bengal and Jharkhand. Units working in 2020-21: 285 in all; Odisha 76, Chhattisgarh 69, West Bengal 36, Jharkhand 24.
[87:3:4] - 2024-25: iron ore output 289.398 MT (imports 6.4 MT, adequate); coking coal output 66.49 MT but imports **57.07 MT**.
[87:3:5] - Old framing (IAS 1993): India self-sufficient in coking coal for three decades. Planning Commission Energy Policy report: petroleum reserves to end in 22 years, gas 30, coal 80.
