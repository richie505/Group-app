# Japan – market-based steel
- **Japan** = **market-based** iron and steel: lacks ore and coal, imports them in big ships, so plants stand at **ports/coast** (Tokyo–Yokohama, Osaka–Kobe–Himeji, Kitakyushu).
- Most centres lie on the southern coast; a few at **Kamaishi** (NE Honshu) and **Muroran** (Hokkaido).
- **Northern Kyushu** = iron and steel (Yawata), **not cotton** – Japan's cotton is in Kinki (Osaka) and Kanto.
- **Japan leads** through **high technology**, not abundant minerals; a leading shipbuilder of East Asia.

=== Factors of Location, Weber and Other Theorists
# Factors
| Factor | Pull | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Raw material (weight-losing) | Near ore or coalfield | Iron and steel, sugar, cement |
| Power | Near cheap power | Aluminium |
| Market | Near consumers or ports | Japan's market-based steel at the coast |
| Labour, transport, capital, policy | Skilled labour, ports, government support | Northern Italy, Pearl River Delta |
- **Steel**: about **4 tonnes** of inputs (iron ore, coking coal, limestone, dolomite) → **1 tonne** of steel, so plants sit on coalfields or ore fields.
# Weber's Least-cost Theory (1909)
- **Alfred Weber**: least-cost approach – firm locates where total **transport + labour + agglomeration** costs are least (OPSC 2018); Christaller = central places, not industry.
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Locational triangle | 2 raw-material sources + 1 market; least-transport-cost point lies inside it |
| Material Index (MI) | Weight of localised raw materials / weight of finished product |
| Isodapanes | Lines of equal extra transport cost |
- **MI > 1** → locate at raw material; **MI < 1** → at market.
| Material type | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Ubiquities | Available everywhere | Water, air |
| Pure localised | Lose no weight | Cotton |
| Gross (weight-losing) | Lose weight in processing | Iron ore, coal, sugarcane |
- Cotton is non-weight-losing, so textile mills are NOT tied to raw-cotton sites.

=== Footloose, Sunrise, Umland and Agglomeration
- **Footloose**: not tied to raw material or a fixed place; light, high-value, low transport cost, low pollution – **diamond cutting**, electronics, software, watch-making.
- **Sunrise industries**: new, fast-growing sectors – **information technology**, electronics, **hospitality**/tourism; iron and steel and textiles are old ("sunset") industries.
- **Umland**: the **area surrounding a town**, linked to it by economic and cultural activities; term first used in **1883**.
- **Agglomeration economies**: cost savings when firms cluster – shared infrastructure, specialised suppliers, skilled labour pool. They do NOT tie footloose industries to raw materials.
- **Industrial inertia**: a firm stays put after the original advantage is gone (Ruhr, Pittsburgh, Lancashire).

=== Industrial Revolution and the Shift to Asia
- **Industrial Revolution** began in **England (Britain)** c. **1750** – coal and iron, canals and rail, indented coast and rivers.
- **NCERT trap (NDA 2011):** from the 1780s it was the **cotton** industry, not iron, that symbolised British industrialisation.
- **Steel**: **China 1st** (over 50% of world steel); **India 2nd**.
- **Cotton textiles**: **China** is the leading producer.
- **Shipbuilding (2025 new orders)**: China first (about 60–66%), South Korea second (about 20%), Japan third.
- India: Rourkela = first public-sector plant (German help); VISL Bhadravati = oldest public-sector steel plant in the south.
- **Shift to Asia**: old Western regions (Ruhr, Lorraine, US Rust Belt, English Midlands) declined; China and India lead.
- **Asian Tigers** (newly industrialising): South Korea, Taiwan, Singapore, Hong Kong.
- Revised Index of Core Industries, base **2022-23**; iron ore added → **nine** industries (July 2026).
- India steel: 168.4 MT crude steel (2025-26); capacity about 220 MT; target **300 MT by 2030**.
