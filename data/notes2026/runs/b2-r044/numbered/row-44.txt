## SUB 0 | High Court Judges – Appointment, Tenure, Transfer, Removal
[0:0] # Appointment and qualifications (Art. 217)
[0:1] - Every HC judge is appointed by the **President** by warrant (not the Governor, not the CJI). The **Governor cannot appoint or remove** HC judges – but he does appoint the **CM, SPSC members and the Advocate General**.
[0:2] - **Chief Justice**: President after consulting the **CJI and the Governor**. **Other judges**: also the **Chief Justice of that HC** → consultees = **HC CJ, CJI, Governor** (not the Attorney General). Common HC: **Governors of all the States** concerned.
[0:3] - **Collegium**: HC CJ + **2** senior-most HC judges propose → **CJI + 2** senior-most SC judges (Third Judges case, 1998); NJAC (99th Amendment) struck down in **2015**.
[0:4] - **Qualifications**: citizen; **10 years judicial office** or **10 years HC advocate**; no minimum age; **no "distinguished jurist" route** (only SC, Art. 124(3)(c)).
[0:5] - **Strength** of each HC: fixed by the **President** (Art. 216) – not Parliament, Cabinet, PM or Governor; strength differs from HC to HC. (SC strength: Parliament by law.)
[0:6] - Sanctioned strength (18 Sep 2025): **1,122** for all HCs, **804** filled. **8 Aug 2026**: 30 HC judges appointed (Madras 15, Karnataka 6).
[0:7] - **Justice H.R. Khanna** (8th Law Commission, **80th Report**, 1979): at least **one-third** of HC judges should be from another State.
[0:8] # Oath, tenure, resignation
[0:9] - **Oath** before the **Governor** or a person appointed by him (Art. 219) – not the President or CJI.
[0:10] - Holds office till **62** – raised from **60** by the **15th Amendment, 1963**; the **114th Amendment Bill (2010)** sought **65**. Not "during pleasure", not "as long as they desire". (SC judges: **65**.)
[0:11] - **Resignation** in writing to the **President** (Art. 217(1)(a)) – not the CJI, HC CJ or Governor.
[0:12] # Transfer (Art. 222)
[0:13] - HC judges **can be transferred**; the **President** transfers after consulting the **CJI** (Art. 222(1)) – the CJI/collegium only advises; the PM or Law Minister has no power. Only **HC judges**: **no provision to transfer SC judges**.
[0:14] # Removal
[0:15] - Same manner and grounds as an SC judge (Arts. **217(1)(b), 218**, 124(4)): **President's order** after an address by **both Houses** with special majority. Grounds only **proved misbehaviour or incapacity** – **difference of opinion with the Chief Justice** is never a ground; neither the Governor nor the State Legislature can remove.
[0:16] - Procedure: **Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968** – motion by 100 LS / 50 RS members; 3-member committee (SC judge, HC CJ, jurist). No HC judge impeached so far.

## SUB 1 | High Courts in India – Number, History, Jurisdiction
[1:0] # Constitutional basis
[1:1] - HCs: **Part VI, Chapter V, Articles 214–231**. **Art. 214**: a HC for each State. **Art. 231** (7th Amendment, 1956): **Parliament by law** may set up a **common HC** for two or more States (or States + a UT) – not the President's order, an SC order or an amendment. **Art. 230**: Parliament extends/excludes HC jurisdiction over a UT (7th Amendment, 1956). **Art. 241**: Parliament may constitute a HC for a UT.
[1:2] - A **new HC** is created by a **law of Parliament** and brought into being by a **Presidential notification** (e.g. AP HC from 1 Jan 2019 under the AP Reorganisation Act).
[1:3] # History
[1:4] - **Indian High Courts Act, 1861** → first three in **1862**: **Calcutta (2 July 1862, oldest), Bombay, Madras**. Fourth: **Agra 1866**, shifted to **Allahabad 1869** – **150th anniversary in 2016**.

## SUB 2 | Judicial Review – Meaning, Origin, Basis
[2:0] - **Meaning**: power of the **SC and HCs** to pronounce upon the **constitutionality of laws and executive orders** (Centre and States) and declare them **void** – not questioning the **wisdom** of laws, not pre-assent review, not reviewing its own judgments (that is Art. 137 review), not hearing appeals from lower courts. Hence the SC "can declare illegal any law of the State".
[2:1] - Examines the **constitutional validity of laws** – not a "periodic review of the Constitution" or "review of the functioning of the judiciary".
[2:2] - Enjoyed by the **Supreme Court as well as the High Courts** (Arts **32** and **226**; NextGen: Arts **13 and 226** confer it on HCs) – not all courts.
[2:3] - The term is **not used in the Constitution**; part of the **basic structure** (**Kesavananda 1973**; **L. Chandra Kumar 1997**).
[2:4] - **Origin USA**: **Marbury v. Madison (1803)**, CJ **John Marshall**; India borrowed it from the **USA**. Prevalent in **both India and the USA**; **UK** – parliamentary sovereignty, courts cannot void Acts.
[2:5] - **Indian vs British parliamentary system**: the difference is **judicial review** (India) – both have real and nominal executives, collective responsibility and bicameral legislatures.
[2:6] - **Checks legislative excesses** of Parliament and State Assemblies (not President/Governor, no-confidence or elections); **restricts the sovereignty of Parliament** – laws cannot break the Basic Structure.
[2:7] - India = **balance of parliamentary sovereignty (British) and judicial supremacy (American)** – neither pure parliamentary sovereignty, judicial supremacy nor cabinet supremacy.
[2:8] - **Custodian / guardian of Fundamental Rights**: the **Supreme Court** (Art. 32) – with the **High Courts** (Art. 226); not the President, PM, Parliament or Rajya Sabha.
[2:9] - Basis: written Constitution + **federalism** + FRs. **Symbol of federal government** (APPSC key) – courts as umpire of the Centre-State division. **Theory**: **supremacy of the Constitution** / limited government.
[2:10] - **Grounds**: violation of FRs; lack of **legislative competence**; repugnance to the Constitution / Basic Structure – not disagreement with majority policy.
[2:11] - FRs held amendable first in **Shankari Prasad (1951)**.

## SUB 3 | Judicial Activism and PIL
[3:0] # Terms
[3:1] TABLE head: Term | Meaning
  [3:1.0] Judicial activism (= judicial dynamism) | Proactive role of courts protecting rights and filling gaps; opposite of restraint
  [3:1.1] PIL | "A legal action initiated in a court of law for the enforcement of public interest or general interest in which the public or a class of the community have pecuniary or some other interest" (SC definition)
  [3:1.2] Social Action Litigation | Other name of PIL (also SIL, Class Action Litigation)
  [3:1.3] Locus standi relaxed | Any member of the public with "sufficient interest" can move court for others' rights
  [3:1.4] Epistolary jurisdiction | Letters, postcards, newspaper reports treated as petitions
[3:2] - **Why activism arises**: mainly the **failure of the legislature and executive** to discharge their duties – not judges' wish for supremacy or populism.
[3:3] - **PIL = outcome of judicial activism** (its most popular form) – not of judicial restraint; "intertwined with" activism. PIL evolved by **judicial initiative / activism** – **not** by a constitutional amendment, Act of Parliament or Presidential order.
[3:4] - PIL under **Art. 32 (SC)** or **Art. 226 (HC)**; in line with **Art. 39A**.
[3:5] - **Example of activism**: SC decision on the **rights of the third gender** (**NALSA, 2014**) – not routine river-water, tax-appeal or land-ownership decisions.
[3:6] # Origin and pioneers
[3:7] - **Judicial activism**: originated in the **USA**; term coined **1947** by **Arthur Schlesinger Jr.**; in India from the **mid-1970s** (post-Emergency). PIL: originated in the **USA in the 1960s**; in India early **1980s** (first cases 1979).
[3:8] - **Pioneers of PIL**: **Justice V.R. Krishna Iyer and Justice P.N. Bhagwati** – the first SC judges to admit PILs; with O. Chinnappa Reddy and D.A. Desai they laid the foundation of activism. Judge "who introduced PIL" = **P.N. Bhagwati**.
[3:9] - **V.R. Krishna Iyer** was **never Chief Justice of India** (UPSC 2008: only "progenitor of PIL" is correct). Bhagwati was CJI 1985-86.
[3:10] - **First PIL**: **Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar (1979)** – **undertrial prisoners spending long years in Bihar jails** (speedy trial); filed by **Kapila Hingorani ("Mother of PILs")**.

[3:15] - Genuine SC evolutions: **Basic Structure**, **PIL**, **removal of handcuffs**, continuing mandamus, expansion of Art. 21. **Not** an SC evolution: "**exemptions to FRs**" (Arts **31A–31C, 33**) – constitutional text.

## SUB 5 | Court of Record and Contempt of Court
[5:0] - **Arts 129 (SC) and 215 (HC)**: the **SC and the HCs** are Courts of Record (not district courts). Two powers: records of **perpetual memory and testimony**, of **evidentiary value**, not questioned in lower courts, recognised as **legal precedents**; and power to **punish for contempt of itself**.
[5:1] - So only the **SC or a HC** can punish for its own contempt (not any court, tribunal or quasi-judicial body); under the 1971 Act a HC also punishes contempt of **subordinate courts**.
[5:2] - "Contempt of court" is **not defined in the Constitution** – defined by the **Contempt of Courts Act, 1971**. **Parliament** makes laws on contempt (Union List entry 77; Concurrent List entry 14).
[5:3] - **Contempt of Court Act, 1926** – first major law; **H.N. Sanyal Committee (1961)** → **Contempt of Courts Act, 1971** (UPSC 2022: statements 1, 2 and 4 correct; 3 wrong).
[5:4] TABLE head: Type | Meaning
  [5:4.0] Civil contempt | Wilful disobedience of a judgment/order or wilful breach of an undertaking given to a court
  [5:4.1] Criminal contempt | Scandalising or lowering the court's authority; prejudicing or interfering with judicial proceedings; obstructing the administration of justice
[5:5] - **Not contempt**: a **complaint against a court to a higher court**, fair criticism, fair reporting.
[5:6] - Purpose: to secure **public respect and confidence** in the judicial process – A-R: both true and R explains A.
[5:7] - **Justice C.S. Karnan** (Calcutta HC): first sitting HC judge to **appear personally before the SC** in a contempt case (March 2017); sentenced to **six months** for **contempt of court** (May 2017).
[5:8] - Jul 2026: SC (CJI Surya Kant bench) shifted "bulldozer justice" contempt pleas to HCs. Sep 2026: SC refused to stay Bombay HC contempt proceedings over a judicial officer's outburst.

## SUB 8 | High Court Powers – Jurisdiction and Writs
[8:0] - Jurisdictions of a HC: **original, writ, appellate, supervisory**, control over subordinate courts, court of record, judicial review – **no advisory jurisdiction** (that is the SC's, Art. 143).
[8:1] - **Art. 226**: writs for **FRs "and for any other purpose"** (ordinary legal rights) – **wider than Art. 32**; to **any person, authority or government** within its territory, and **even outside** if the **cause of action** arises within. So "a HC cannot issue orders to any government except its own" is **wrong**. Art. 226 is discretionary; Art. 32 itself is an FR (R does not explain A).
[8:2] - **Art. 227**: **administrative and judicial superintendence** over all courts and tribunals in its territory; may act **suo motu**; **227(4)** – not over **Armed Forces** courts/tribunals. The SC has no Art. 227-type power.
[8:3] - **Appellate**: a HC is primarily a court of appeal; second civil appeals only on a **substantial question of law**; Sessions Court death sentences need HC confirmation (**BNSS 2023**).
[8:4] - Four HCs (Calcutta, Bombay, Madras, Delhi) have **original civil jurisdiction** in high-value cases.

## SUB 10 | Limits of Judicial Review and Overcoming a Judgment
[10:0] - **Narrower than the USA**: Art. 21 "**procedure established by law**" vs US "**due process of law**" (US SC = "third chamber" shaping policy). Presidential system first formed in the **USA**.

## SUB 11 | Judicial Overreach Debate
[11:0] TABLE head: Basis | Review / restraint | Activism
  [11:0.0] Nature | Interpretative, legalistic | Creative, proactive, reform-oriented
  [11:0.1] Question | "Is the law valid?" | "Is the law just?"
[11:1] - **Overreach**: courts entering the executive / legislative domain → strains (blurs) **separation of powers**.
[11:2] - Concerns: courts doing executive work, weak enforcement, flood of letter petitions, **delays in other cases** (not a reduction), PIL misuse; Art. 142 "**complete justice**" undefined and may bypass democratic scrutiny.
[11:3] - **Justice A.S. Anand**: activism must not become **judicial adventurism**. **Upendra Baxi**: courts should **fill a vacuum**, not replace democratic institutions.
[11:4] - PIL rethink debate (May 2026) – curbing misuse, **not abolishing access to justice**.
[11:5] - **Mar 2026**: SC ban on an NCERT chapter on judicial delay and corruption criticised as **censorship under Art. 19**.
