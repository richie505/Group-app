## SUB 0 | Features – Nominal Executive, Collective Responsibility, Double Membership
[0:0] TABLE head: Feature | Fact
  [0:0.0] Nominal and real executive | President = de jure head (Head of State), like the British Monarch; PM + Council of Ministers = real (de facto) executive
  [0:0.1] Aid and advice | Art. 74(1): advice binding on the President
  [0:0.2] Majority party rule | President appoints (Art. 75(1)) the leader of the majority party in the Lok Sabha, or of the alliance with a majority there, as PM – a convention
  [0:0.3] Collective responsibility | Art. 75(3): to the Lok Sabha only (not both Houses, not the President, not the ruling party); ministers hold office together while they command a majority – "swim and sink together"
  [0:0.4] Individual responsibility | Art. 75(2): ministers hold office during the pleasure of the President – individually responsible to the President (dismissed on the PM's advice)
  [0:0.5] Double membership | Ministers are MPs of either House; a non-member must enter Parliament within six months (Art. 75(5)), else ceases to be a minister
  [0:0.6] Leadership of the PM | Heads the Council of Ministers; Leader of the Lok Sabha (if a member) and chief spokesperson
  [0:0.7] Dissolution | Lok Sabha can be dissolved early on the PM's advice
[0:1] - **Not in the Constitution:** "PM is leader of the majority party" (convention); the **ranks** of ministers (Cabinet, State, Deputy).
[0:2] - **⚠ Traps:** collective responsibility to "Parliament" is accepted only when the Lok Sabha is not offered; "Council of Ministers responsible to both Houses" ✗ (UPSC 2007 A false, R true – members of both Houses can be ministers); "Cabinet headed by the Head of State" ✗ (UPSC 2013: 1 and 2 only); "President is the real head of the executive" ✗ (APPSC G2).
[0:3] - **Cabinet membership** is **not restricted** to Lok Sabha members.

## SUB 1 | Parliamentary System – Meaning and Why India Is Parliamentary
[1:0] - **Names:** **cabinet government**, **responsible government**, Westminster model – the executive stays in power only while it has a **majority (confidence) in the popular House**.
[1:1] - **Defining test:** India is parliamentary because the **Council of Ministers is responsible to the Lok Sabha** (Art. 75(3); States Art. 164(2)) – not because the Lok Sabha is directly elected, Parliament can amend the Constitution or the Rajya Sabha cannot be dissolved (UPSC 2015).
[1:2] - **Parliamentary executive** = an executive **dependent on the support of a majority in Parliament** – not "elected by Parliament", not "Parliament acting as executive".
[1:3] - **Main principle:** **responsibility of the executive to the legislature**; the legislature controls the executive. "Legislature and Executive are independent" is the **presidential** feature.
[1:4] - **Removal:** the government is **responsible to Parliament and can be removed by it** (no-confidence) – not "all parties represented", not "elected by the people", not "fixed term" (UPSC 2020).
[1:5] - **Basis:** **fusion of powers** – co-operation of legislature and executive; presidential = separation of powers.
[1:6] - **Source of authority:** the Constitution sets up the parliamentary system at the **Centre and in the States**; both governments draw authority from the **Constitution**.
[1:7] - India = **secular, federal, parliamentary** – "presidential system" ✗; "government by experts" ✗ (ministers are amateurs). India is parliamentary, cabinet and responsible government.
[1:8] - **Statements:** "President **represents the nation but does not rule** it" ✓; "executive independent of and not accountable to the legislature" ✗.
[1:9] > Exam angle: "There is parliamentary government in India because…" = **CoM responsible to the Lok Sabha** (UPSC 2015 ×2); A-R UPPSC 2025: both true, R explains A.
[1:10] ~~ Sources: Laxmikanth, Indian Polity 8th ed. ch.13 p.206-209 (src/polity/laxmikanth-indian-polity-01.txt); app notes 2:38 (sub 0)
## SUB 2 | Presidential System – Features and Contrast
[2:0] - President = **Head of State and Head of Government** – **single executive**; all executive power vested in him.
[2:1] - **Elected** for a **fixed term** (US: electoral college, 4 years); removable only by **impeachment**; **not accountable** to the legislature; **cannot dissolve** the legislature.
[2:2] - **Appoints his own Council of Ministers** (secretaries), who are not members of the legislature and are responsible only to him.
[2:3] - **Principle:** **separation of powers** with **checks and balances** – President and legislature check each other (veto, Senate confirmation, impeachment). "Fusion of powers" = parliamentary; "division of powers" = federalism.
[2:4] - **Origin:** first formed in the **USA** (oldest and purest form); USA has a presidential form – India, Canada and Britain are parliamentary.
[2:5] - APPSC G2 key: head of State ✓, head of Government ✓, directly elected ✓, "**accountable to the legislature**" ✗ → "i, ii and iii".
[2:6] TABLE head: Parliamentary | Presidential
  [2:6.0] Nominal head + real head (PM) – dual executive | One person is both heads – single executive
  [2:6.1] Collective executive (cabinet); ministers are legislators | Secretaries outside the legislature
  [2:6.2] Fusion of powers | Separation of powers
  [2:6.3] Removable by no-confidence | Fixed tenure; only impeachment; fixed term
  [2:6.4] Lower House can be dissolved early | No dissolution
[2:7] - **Semi-presidential:** directly elected President with real powers + **PM responsible to the legislature** – **France (Fifth Republic, 1958), Russia, Sri Lanka (1978 Constitution)**; **not India** (President indirectly elected, nominal).
[2:8] - **Parliamentary examples:** UK, Japan, Canada, India.

## SUB 3 | India Compared with Britain and the USA
[3:0] TABLE head: Feature | India | Britain
  [3:0.0] Model | Westminster model, from Britain – the British Parliament is the "Mother of Parliaments" | Parliamentary form first introduced here
  [3:0.1] Head of State | Indirectly elected President, 5 years | Hereditary monarch
  [3:0.2] Supremacy | Constitutional supremacy (written Constitution) – not sovereignty of Parliament | Sovereignty of Parliament
  [3:0.3] Judicial review | Courts can strike down laws; Parliament subject to the basic structure | Courts cannot question Acts of Parliament
  [3:0.4] PM from | Either House | House of Commons
  [3:0.5] Ministers | Non-member allowed for six months | Must be MPs
  [3:0.6] Shadow cabinet; legal responsibility of ministers | No | Yes
[3:1] - **Key difference (UPSC 1998):** India has **judicial review**; a real and nominal executive, collective responsibility and bicameralism exist in both.
[3:2] - UK examples: ruling party can replace the PM (e.g. **Boris Johnson**, UK PM 2019-22, resigned as Conservative leader July 2022); in India removal needs loss of **Lok Sabha confidence**.
[3:3] TABLE head: Feature | India | USA
  [3:3.0] Executive | Parliamentary | Presidential
  [3:3.1] Residuary powers | Union (Parliament) | States
  [3:3.2] Pocket veto | President has it | President has it – common
  [3:3.3] Upper house | Nominated members (Rajya Sabha) | No nominated members in the Senate
  [3:3.4] Judicial review | Narrower – "procedure established by law" | Wider – "due process of law"; SC called a "third chamber"
  [3:3.5] Term limit | None for the PM | 22nd Amendment (1951)
[3:4] - **US facts:** Constitution in force **1789** (not 1810); **revenue bills originate in the House of Representatives**; **John Quincy Adams** and **George W. Bush** were both sons of Presidents.
[3:5] - **Common** to India and USA: written Constitution, federal structure, independent judiciary with judicial review, Fundamental Rights / Bill of Rights, pocket veto.
[3:6] - **Records (as of 2026):** longest-serving Chief Minister = **Pawan Kumar Chamling** (Sikkim, 1994-2019); on **22 March 2026** **Narendra Modi** (8,931 days as head of an elected government, CM + PM) passed Chamling's **8,930** (app notes).

## SUB 4 | Merits, Demerits and the Constituent Assembly's Choice
[4:0] TABLE head: System | Merits | Demerits
  [4:0.0] Parliamentary | Responsible government – executive continuously accountable; harmony of legislature and executive; prevents despotism; ready alternative government | Unstable government; no continuity of policy; cabinet dictatorship with a large majority; "government by amateurs"; against separation of powers
  [4:0.1] Presidential | Stable, fixed-term executive; continuity of policy; experts as secretaries | Executive-legislature deadlock; personality cult; irresponsible executive
[4:1] - **Main advantage** of the parliamentary form: the **executive remains responsible to the legislature** (UPSC 2017) – "continuity of policy" is the presidential merit.
[4:2] - **Why the Constituent Assembly chose it:** **familiarity** from the **1919 and 1935 Acts**; vast size and diversity; avoid US-style deadlock.
[4:3] - **Dr B.R. Ambedkar:** a democratic executive must satisfy **stability and responsibility**, which cannot both be maximised; the Draft preferred **more responsibility** to more stability. The parliamentary system gives a "**daily as well as a periodic assessment**" of the Government (CAD, 4 Nov 1948).
[4:4] TABLE head: Person / body | Contribution
  [4:4.0] Vasant Sathe | Proposed a presidential form, 1970s-80s
  [4:4.1] NCRWC (Venkatachaliah, 2000-02) | Advised keeping the parliamentary system
[4:5] - **One Nation, One Election:** amendments to Arts **83, 172, 327**; critics say fixed tenures graft presidential mechanics onto the system.
[4:6] - **Shamsher Singh (1974):** a Governor publicly criticising Cabinet policy runs "**counter to the parliamentary system**".
