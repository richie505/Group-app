=== Parliament on the State List – Articles 249, 250, 252, 253 and 356
# The five situations
| Article | Trigger | Area / duration |
|---|---|---|
| **249** | **Rajya Sabha** resolution, **2/3 of members present and voting**, national interest | Resolution in force **up to 1 year**, renewable 1 year at a time; law ceases **6 months** after the resolution lapses |
| **250** | **National Emergency** (Proclamation under **Art. 352** in operation) | Whole or any part of India; law ceases **6 months after the Emergency ends** |
| **252** | Resolutions of the legislatures of **two or more States** | Only the consenting States (others may adopt later); only **Parliament** can amend or repeal |
| **253** | To implement **international treaties, agreements, conventions** | Whole or any part of India; **no State's consent** needed |
| **356/357** | **President's Rule** in a State | Parliament (or its delegate) makes the State's laws; they continue after PR ends until the **State Legislature** alters, repeals or re-enacts them |
- **Normal rule:** Parliament cannot legislate on the State List except under **Arts 249, 250, 252, 253 and 356** – "on its own in the national interest" is **wrong** (needs the Rajya Sabha resolution).
- **Articles are the grant, emergencies the occasion:** the power during a National Emergency is given by **Art. 250** (Art. 352 only proclaims the Emergency).
# Article 249 – Rajya Sabha resolution
- **Majority:** **two-thirds of members present and voting** – not total membership, not the Lok Sabha (the Lok Sabha has **no role**).
- **Duration:** resolution in force for a period **not exceeding one year** (Art. 249(2)); can be renewed any number of times.
- A **special power of the Rajya Sabha** (with Art. 312 – new All India Services).
- The State **may still legislate**, but the **Parliamentary law prevails** in a conflict (**Art. 251**).
# Article 250 – Emergency
- During a Proclamation of Emergency **Parliament** (not the President or a minister) legislates on State List matters; State power is **not suspended**, Union law prevails on conflict.
- Laws cease **6 months after the Proclamation ceases** – they do **not** continue indefinitely.
# Article 252 – consent of States
| Act passed under Article 252 | Year |
|---|---|
| Prize Competition Act | **1955** |
| Wild Life (Protection) Act | 1972 |
| Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act | 1974 |
| Urban Land (Ceiling and Regulation) Act | 1976 |
| Transplantation of Human Organs Act | 1994 |
- A request by **one State** is not enough; the requesting States **lose the power to amend or repeal** the law.
- **Environment (Protection) Act, 1986** is **not** an Art. 252 law (made under Art. 253 after the Stockholm Conference).
# Article 253 – international agreements
- Parliament can make **any law for the whole or any part of India** to implement treaties, agreements, conventions or decisions of international bodies – **even on State List subjects** and **without the consent of any State** (UPSC 2013; UPPCS 2006, 2016).
- So "the Union can legislate on State List subjects to implement an international agreement" is the statement that reflects India's federal design.
> Statement angle: State List law possible on – request of 2+ States ✓, Rajya Sabha 2/3 resolution ✓, international agreements ✓, Emergency ✓, President's Rule ✓; a **Lok Sabha** resolution ✗.
> Wrong-answer angle: "Both Union and States have absolute freedom on the Concurrent List" ✗ – Union law prevails in conflict.

=== Residuary Powers – Article 248
# The rule
- **Article 248** (Part XI) + **Entry 97 of the Union List**: matters in **no list** belong **exclusively to Parliament** – Parliament **only**, not the States, the President, the Supreme Court or "both jointly".
- Art. 248 itself says Parliament has **exclusive power** to make any law on any matter **not enumerated in the Concurrent or State List** – so both "residuary powers are in Art. 248" and "Parliament has exclusive power over items not in the State or Concurrent List" are correct.
- Includes **residuary taxation** (e.g. gift tax, wealth tax, expenditure tax); examples: **space research**, **cyber law**.
| Country | Residuary power with |
|---|---|
| **India, Canada** | **Centre** (Union / Parliament) |
| **USA, Australia** | **States** |
- **Sarkaria Commission (1988):** residuary powers **except taxation** to the **Concurrent List**; residuary taxation to stay with Parliament.
- **Rajamannar Committee (1971):** residuary powers (legislation and taxation) to the **States**.
# Jammu and Kashmir (till 5 August 2019)
- Under Art. 370 and the 1954 Presidential Order, residuary power for J&K lay with the **State Legislature** (except a few matters such as terrorism) – not Parliament (APPSC 2001, DAO 2011).
- Other J&K facts asked: J&K was part of the territory of India (Art. 1, First Schedule), but not all provisions on States applied to it; by accession India got **Defence, External Affairs and Communications**.
- **⚠ Now:** Art. 370 provisions ceased in Aug 2019; the **J&K Reorganisation Act, 2019** made J&K and Ladakh **Union Territories** (31 Oct 2019), so residuary power now rests with **Parliament** (upheld by the SC, Dec 2023).
> Trap: "Like other States, J&K's residuary powers vest in Parliament" was the WRONG statement (pre-2019).

=== Part XI and the Article Map 245-255
# Where it sits
- **Part XI** (Relations between the Union and the States) = **Articles 245-263**: Chapter I **legislative relations = Arts 245-255**; Chapter II administrative relations = Arts 256-263.
- Distribution of legislative powers is regulated by **Articles 245 and 246** (with the **Seventh Schedule**); scheme borrowed from **Canada**, plus a third **Concurrent List** (from the GoI Act, 1935).

|---|---|

- Arts **265, 266** (taxes by authority of law; Consolidated Fund) are in **Part XII**, not Part XI Chapter I.

| Article | Content |

# Article 255
- An Act is **not invalid** merely because a required **recommendation or previous sanction** was not given, **if assent was later given** by the Governor or the President – so courts cannot strike down a law on that ground alone.

=== Seventh Schedule – Lists and Subjects
# Article 246 scheme
| Clause | Who | List |
|---|---|---|
| 246(1) | Parliament – exclusive | List I (**Union**) |
| 246(2) | Both – concurrent | List III (**Concurrent**) |
| 246(3) | State – exclusive, subject to (1) and (2) | List II (**State**) |
| 246(4) | Parliament for areas not in a State (UTs) | All three Lists |
- **Art. 246 / Seventh Schedule** divide all subject areas (incl. environment-related subjects) into the three Lists, and is also the source of the power to impose **taxes and fees** (tax entries are in the Lists).
- **Order of predominance:** **Union List > Concurrent List > State List** – in a Concurrent-State conflict the **Concurrent List prevails**; no reference to the President or the Speaker.
- **Limits on Parliament:** it legislates subject to the **distribution of powers** (and the Constitution) – not the UN Charter, conventions or public opinion.
- **Art. 246A (GST)** breaks the strict division: **simultaneous** power; **only Parliament** for **inter-State supply (IGST)**.

=== Territorial Extent – Article 245 and Territorial Nexus
| Legislature | Extent |
|---|---|
| **Parliament** | Whole or any part of India; **alone can make extra-territorial laws** |
| **State Legislature** | Own State only; outside reach only with a real **territorial nexus** |
- **Art. 245(2):** no law of Parliament is invalid because it has **extra-territorial operation** – so the power of extra-territorial legislation = **Parliament only** (not a House alone, the President, the State or any UN approval).
- Parliament's laws apply to persons and property in India and to **Indian citizens and their property anywhere** in the world.
- **Doctrine of territorial nexus:** decides the **scope of a State Legislature's extra-territorial power** – a State law with extra-territorial operation is valid if there is a **sufficient, real (not illusory) nexus** between the State and the object (Wallace 1948; R.M.D. Chamarbaugwalla 1957; TISCO 1958).
- **⚠ Do not confuse:** "what cannot be done directly cannot be done indirectly" = **colourable legislation**, not territorial nexus.
# Exceptions to Parliament's reach
- **Andaman & Nicobar, Lakshadweep, Dadra & Nagar Haveli and Daman & Diu (Art. 240):** **President's regulations** have the force of Acts of Parliament.
- **Scheduled Areas:** the **Governor** may **bar or modify** Acts of Parliament; Governor of **Assam** likewise for tribal areas.

=== Repugnancy, Paramountcy and Central Control
# Article 254
- Applies to a Central vs State law on a **Concurrent** subject.
- **254(1):** **Union law prevails**; the State law is **void to the extent of repugnancy** – the **doctrine of paramountcy** (reconciles conflicting laws where both levels can legislate on the same matter).
- **254(2):** a State law **reserved for and assented to by the President** prevails **only in that State**.
- **Proviso:** Parliament can later **override** it by a fresh law.
- **Art. 251** (not 254) covers conflict with laws made under **Arts 249/250**; Art. 250 is **not** the repugnancy article.
# Central control over State legislation
- **Governor may reserve** State Bills for the President (Art. 200/201); Art. 254(2) reservation is an exception to aid and advice.
- **State Bill** with **reasonable restrictions on inter-State trade** in the public interest needs the **President's previous sanction** (Part XIII, Art. 304(b)).
- **Arts 301-303:** Parliament cannot **prefer or discriminate** between States in trade laws, except in **scarcity**.
- **Nov 2025 SC advisory opinion (Presidential Reference):** **rejected "deemed assent"** and **judicially fixed timelines** for Governors and the President.

=== Doctrines of Interpretation
- Most doctrines are **judicially evolved**, not written in the Constitution.
| Doctrine | Meaning | Case |
|---|---|---|
| **Pith and substance** | True nature of a law decides; incidental encroachment on another List is fine (Art. 246) | Prafulla Kumar Mukherjee v Bank of Commerce (1947, Privy Council); State of Bombay v F.N. Balsara (1951) – prohibition law valid |
| **Colourable legislation** | "What cannot be done directly cannot be done indirectly" | Kameshwar Singh (1952); K.C. Gajapati Narayan Deo v State of Orissa (1953) |
| **Harmonious construction** | Overlapping entries read together so both operate | — |
| **Severability** | Only the unconstitutional part void, if separable (Art. 13) | A.K. Gopalan (1950); F.N. Balsara (1951) |
| **Eclipse** | Pre-Constitution law conflicting with an FR is dormant; revives if the right is amended | Bhikaji Narain Dhakras v State of M.P. (1955) |
| **Territorial nexus** | Scope of State extra-territorial laws (Art. 245) | Chamarbaugwalla (1957) |
| **Basic structure** | Parliament cannot destroy basic features | Kesavananda Bharati (1973) |
| **Judicial review** | Courts test **legislative acts and executive actions** (Arts 13, 32, 226) | Marbury v Madison (1803, USA) |
