=== NOTES 4:21 | Climate Change – Evidence, Causes, Global Warming, Ipcc Assessments, Impacts
## SUB 0 | Evidence of Climate Change – Past Climates and Recent Warming
[21:0:0] # Past climates (asked 7 times)
[21:0:1] - **Tree rings (dendroclimatology)**: ring width is a fairly accurate index of a year's **precipitation** (water available in the growing season); old tree rings are the best record of a place's **past local climate over centuries** (weather logs cover only recent decades).
[21:0:2] - Other evidence: glacial landforms (advances and retreats), sediments of glacial lakes, archaeological finds (Rajasthan wet and cool about 8,000 BC).
[21:0:3] - **Great Ice Age = Pleistocene** (cooling began in the **Pliocene**) – APPSC G1 2002 key: "Pleistocene and Pliocene". Last glacial peak about 18,000 years ago; present interglacial began about 10,000 years ago.
[21:0:4] - **Glaciation of peninsular India** = the **Permo-Carboniferous (Carboniferous) ice age** of Gondwana, recorded in the **Talchir tillite** at the base of the Gondwana rocks; Pleistocene glaciers reached only the Himalaya.
[21:0:5] - **Little Ice Age**: about **1550–1850** (NCERT; others 1300–1850, glaciers greatest **1500–1850**); much colder winters in Europe and N America; among exam options **1650–1870** is the period given. 950–1250 AD = Medieval Warm Period.
[21:0:6] # Recent warming
[21:0:7] TABLE head: Indicator | Record
  [21:0:7.0] 2024 | Hottest year on record, about 1.55°C above pre-industrial
  [21:0:7.1] 2025 | 2nd/3rd warmest (~1.43°C); warmest La Nina year
  [21:0:7.2] 2015–2025 | The 11 warmest years (WMO State of the Global Climate 2025)
  [21:0:7.3] India | Warmed about 0.7°C since 1900 (less than global land, ~1.59°C); IMD: 2024 warmest year since 1901
  [21:0:7.4] Gangotri Glacier | Retreated about 1,700 m since 1935
  [21:0:7.5] Sea level | Up about 11 cm since 1993
[21:0:8] - **Global warming** = rise in global surface temperature, caused by rising greenhouse-gas concentrations (CDS 2017 A-R: both true, II explains I).
[21:0:9] - **Sea-level rise** = thermal expansion + melting ice; higher snow albedo is not a cause.
[21:0:10] ~~ Sources: NCERT Fundamentals ch.11 (OnlyIAS Physical Geography 08 p.7–8); PMF Physical Geography 01 p.68 (Carboniferous glaciation of Gondwana); Talchir Formation studies (J. Palaeogeography 2015); WMO; IMD; app notes 4:21 sub 0–1
[21:0:11] - **20th century:** global temperature **rose** by about **0.6°C (1.1°F)** and sea level rose 10–20 cm (IPCC TAR 2001) – it did not "rise and fall alternately".
[21:0:12] - **Proxy records of past climate:** **tree rings**, **ice cores** from the cryosphere (trapped air bubbles, isotopes), **coral reef** growth bands, ocean sediments and pollen.
## SUB 1 | Causes – Natural and Anthropogenic
[21:1:0] # Natural causes (asked 5 times)
[21:1:1] TABLE head: Cause | Meaning / effect
  [21:1:1.0] Eccentricity | Shape of Earth's orbit
  [21:1:1.1] Obliquity | Axial tilt
  [21:1:1.2] Precession (of the equinoxes) | Wobble of the rotational axis
  [21:1:1.3] Solar output (sunspots) | Satellites since 1978 show no net increase: the Sun does not explain recent warming
  [21:1:1.4] Volcanoes | SO2 + ash form aerosols that reflect sunlight: short-term cooling
[21:1:2] - **Milankovitch** (Serbian, **1920s**): astronomical theory – orbital forcing by eccentricity, tilt (shifting of the axis) and precession. **Solar irradiance is NOT a Milankovitch factor** (NCERT lists sunspots separately as a solar cause).
[21:1:3] # Anthropogenic causes
[21:1:4] - **Chief contributor** to warming in the last and present century = **anthropogenic impact** (IPCC AR6), not plate tectonics, volcanoes or Earth's rotation.
[21:1:5] - Fossil fuels (CO2), **land-use change** (deforestation removes sinks), **agriculture** (methane, N2O). CO2 causes about **three-quarters** of warming.
[21:1:6] - Rising GHGs → global warming, **CO2 fertilisation** of plants, stratospheric cooling linked to **ozone depletion**; there is **no "oxygen fertilisation effect"** (CDS 2007).
[21:1:7] - **Water vapour** = most abundant greenhouse gas (feedback).
[21:1:8] - **China** = largest CO2 emitter, and largest in the developing world; India 3rd (after China and USA).
[21:1:9] TABLE head: Gas | GWP (100 yr, AR6) | Main sources
  [21:1:9.0] CO2 | 1 | Fossil fuels, deforestation, cement
  [21:1:9.1] Methane | ~27–30 | Rice paddies, livestock (enteric fermentation), landfills
  [21:1:9.2] Nitrous oxide | ~273 | Fertilisers (largest farm source), manure
  [21:1:9.3] CFCs / HFCs | Very high | Refrigeration, air conditioning (synthetic)
  [21:1:9.4] SF6 | ~25,200 (highest) | Electrical switchgear (entirely synthetic)
[21:1:10] # Short-lived climate pollutants (SLCPs)
[21:1:11] - Stay in the air **days to a few decades** – far **shorter** than CO2.
[21:1:12] - **Black carbon** = **soot**; **tropospheric ozone** is not emitted directly (sunlight on methane, CO, VOCs, NOx); methane; HFCs.
[21:1:13] - **Climate and Clean Air Coalition**: UNEP + **six countries**, 16 Feb 2012.

## SUB 2 | Impacts of Global Warming
[21:2:0] - IPCC: warming raises the frequency and severity of **cyclones, storms and hurricanes – all of these**, plus floods, droughts, glacier melt (UPPCS 2018).
[21:2:1] - **Melting polar ice and sea-level rise** = the most cited effect; Uttarakhand 2002 PYQ keyed statements 1, 3 and 4 (coral islands submerged, Fiji by 2044) – these were alarmist forecasts, not facts (as of 2026 Fiji is not submerged).
[21:2:2] TABLE head: Area | Effect
  [21:2:2.0] Sea level | Uninhabited Kiribati islets Tebua Tarawa, Abanuea submerged 1999; Tuvalu at risk
  [21:2:2.1] Coral reefs | 1.2°C warm-water reef tipping point already crossed (UN brief, Jun 2026)
  [21:2:2.2] Amazon | Beyond ~2°C parts may turn savanna and a carbon source
  [21:2:2.3] Oceans, species | Acidity up 30% since the Industrial Revolution; up to 25% of mountain species face extinction (AR6)
  [21:2:2.4] Disease | Warmer, wetter conditions spread vector-borne malaria, dengue
[21:2:3] - **AP**: heatwaves in Palnadu, Kurnool, Anantapur; **coastal erosion at Uppada (Kakinada)**.
[21:2:4] - **COP31**: **Türkiye (Antalya), November 2026**.
[21:2:5] ~~ Sources: IPCC AR6; Shankar Environment ch.17, 20; WMO; app notes 4:21 sub 6
[21:2:6] - In India warming **shrinks glacier area** (Himalayan glaciers retreating) while extreme precipitation, sea-level rise (thermal expansion) and floods **increase**.

=== NOTES 4:23 | Physical and Chemical Characteristics of Ocean Water – Temperature and Salinity Distribution
## SUB 0 | Salinity – Meaning, Factors and Distribution
[23:0:0] # Basics
[23:0:1] - **Salinity** = total dissolved salts in sea water: grams of salt per **1,000 g (1 kg)** of water, in **‰ / ppt** or PSU. **Average 35‰** (35 g per kg); open ocean 33–37‰; **Indian Ocean average 35‰**, Atlantic about 36‰.
[23:0:2] - **Isohaline** = line joining places of equal salinity. Brackish water: about 5–35 ppt (NCERT upper limit 24.7‰).
[23:0:3] - **Main source of salts = land** (weathered rocks), **carried by rivers**; volcanoes add a little. Salinity **reduces evaporation**.
[23:0:4] TABLE head: Factor | Effect
  [23:0:4.0] Evaporation | Raises
  [23:0:4.1] Freezing (salt left behind) | Raises
  [23:0:4.2] Precipitation | Lowers
  [23:0:4.3] River inflow | Lowers (Black Sea: Danube, Dnieper, Dniester)
  [23:0:4.4] Ice melting | Lowers
  [23:0:4.5] Currents, winds | Redistribute
[23:0:5] # Horizontal distribution
[23:0:6] TABLE head: Region | Salinity | Reason

## SUB 2 | Density, Clines and Water's Anomalous Behaviour
[23:2:0] - Water is **densest at 4°C**: a lake freezes at the **top** while the bottom stays liquid at about 4°C (UPSC 2011: density maximum at 4°C).
[23:2:1] - **Density of sea water rises** as **temperature falls**, **salinity rises**, and **pressure (depth)** increases; colder and saltier water sinks and drives deep currents.
[23:2:2] - **Sea water is denser than fresh water**: swimming is easier in sea water (CDS 2008: A and R true, R explains A); an egg sinks in tap water but **floats in strong salt solution** because the solution is denser than the egg; people float in the Dead Sea.

## SUB 3 | Temperature of Ocean Water
[23:3:0] - Oceans are **store-houses** of solar energy: they heat and cool slowly and absorb **over 90%** of the excess heat of global warming, and about a quarter of human CO2 (ocean **pH ~8.1** and falling).
[23:3:1] - **Horizontal**: falls from equator to poles (about 0.5°C per degree of latitude); highest just north of the equator; **NH oceans warmer** (more land; averages 19°C vs 16°C).
[23:3:2] - **Tropics: western side warmer** (trade winds pile warm water west); **temperate zone: eastern side warmer** (westerlies, North Atlantic Drift) – UPSC 2021: **both statements correct**.
[23:3:3] - **Annual range smaller in the Pacific** than the Atlantic (vast) and **greater in the Northern Hemisphere** than the Southern (UPSC 2007: only II).
[23:3:4] - **Vertical, three layers**: warm surface layer (about **500 m**, 20–25°C) → **thermocline** (**500–1,000 m**, rapid fall) → cold deep layer (**0–4°C**); about 90% of ocean water lies below the thermocline.

[24:1:0] TABLE head: Term | Meaning
  [24:1:0.0] Wavelength | Distance between two successive crests – two consecutive points of the same phase
  [24:1:0.1] Wave height / amplitude | Trough to crest / half the height
  [24:1:0.2] Period / frequency | Time between crests / waves per second
  [24:1:0.3] Swells | Slow, steady, long-period waves from distant storms
  [24:1:0.4] Breakers | Waves break when depth < half the wavelength
  [24:1:0.5] Rip current | Narrow, fast channel flowing away from the shore
  [24:1:0.6] Kallakkadal | Kerala swell surge from far southern storms
[24:1:1] - Waves carry **energy, not water**; wind gives them energy.
[24:1:2] # Tsunami
[24:1:3] - Japanese "**harbour wave**". Caused by sudden **displacement of the sea floor**: **tectonic activity**, chiefly **fault movement in undersea earthquakes** (most important cause), also submarine landslides and volcanoes – not wind, the Moon or sunspots.
[24:1:4] - Speed depends on depth (about √(g × depth)): **faster in deep water** (over 700 km/h), so in the open sea it is a **gentle rise and fall** and a **ship in the deep sea is not affected**.
[24:1:5] - Near the shore it **slows down** and grows to **enormous height** (NDA 2013: I and II) – so its impact is greater at the coast; "speed is more in shallow water" is **false** (APPSC FRO 2022: A true, R false).
[24:1:6] - **Warning sign**: the sea **recedes far, exposing the sea floor**. Mitigation: build houses on **elevated sites**.
[24:1:7] - **26 Dec 2004** (Sumatra quake, M 9.1): in India the **Coromandel coast** (Tamil Nadu) was hit hardest; Indira Point was submerged.
[24:1:8] - **ITEWS** (INCOIS, **Hyderabad**, since 2007) confirms a tsunami with **bottom pressure recorders** in the deep ocean (seismic stations detect only the quake; tide gauges are coastal). Tide gauges, BPRs and seismic networks are the working tools; satellite observation is not an effective real-time tsunami warning system (APPSC key).

  [24:1:9.0] INCOIS, Hyderabad | Runs ITEWS; PFZ advisories; SAMUDRA app
  [24:1:9.1] IMD | Cyclone and multi-hazard warnings
  [24:1:9.2] NIOT, Chennai | OMNI buoys (temperature, salinity, currents); station at Nellore (AP)
  [24:1:9.3] RTGS Vijayawada (AP) | Cyclone, high-wave and fish-shoal alerts to fishermen

## SUB 2 | Tides
[24:2:0] # Causes
[24:2:1] - Tides = periodic rise and fall of sea level, once or twice a day, caused by the gravitational pull of the **Sun and Moon on the Earth**; the **Moon's pull is greater** (nearer). **Centrifugal force** balances gravity.
[24:2:2] - **Direct tide** = bulge facing the Moon (gravity); **indirect tide** = opposite side (centrifugal force).
[24:2:3] - Earth rotating west to east is true but does not explain tides (CDS 2018: both true, II not the explanation).
[24:2:4] - **Surges** = irregular, weather-driven (winds, pressure) – not tides; sea water spreading over nearby low land = coastal **inundation** ("oceanic logging").
[24:2:5] # Spring and neap tides
[24:2:6] TABLE head: Basis | Spring tide | Neap tide
  [24:2:6.0] Sun–Earth–Moon | In a straight line | At right angles
  [24:2:6.1] Moon phase | New and full moon | First and third quarter (not new moon)
  [24:2:6.2] Height | About 20% higher than normal | About 20% lower
  [24:2:6.3] Tidal range | Maximum | Minimum: high tide lower, low tide higher
[24:2:7] - **Tidal range** = difference between high and low tide. **Perigean** tides: unusually high when the Moon is closest.
[24:2:8] TABLE head: Type | Feature
  [24:2:8.0] Semi-diurnal | Two highs + two lows a day (most common)
  [24:2:8.1] Diurnal | One high + one low
  [24:2:8.2] Mixed | Unequal heights
[24:2:9] - High tides recur about every **12 h 25 min**, so about **50 minutes** later each day (not exactly every 12 h).
[24:2:10] # Tidal places and energy
[24:2:11] TABLE head: Place | Feature
  [24:2:11.0] Bay of Fundy (Canada) | World's greatest tidal range
  [24:2:11.1] Gulf of Khambhat (Bhavnagar), Gujarat | 5–10 m; about 7,000 MW; India's most potential tidal-energy area
  [24:2:11.2] Gulf of Kutch | 5–8 m
  [24:2:11.3] Sundarbans | About 5 m
  [24:2:11.4] Chandipur (Odisha) | Sea recedes a few km at ebb tide twice a day; you can walk on the sea floor (UPSC 2017)
[24:2:12] - Gulfs with **wide fronts and narrow rears** get the highest tides (so "narrow fronts and wider rears" is wrong, UPSC 2004); a tidal **bore** forms in narrow, shallow estuaries; the Hooghly's tides matter to **Kolkata port**.
[24:2:13] - Tidal energy: **barrage** (tidal range) or **underwater turbines** (tidal stream).

[24:2:15] - **Tidal currents**: tidal water channelled between islands or into bays and estuaries forms tidal currents.
## SUB 3 | Effects of Currents – Fishing Grounds, Climate and Upwelling
[24:3:0] - **Best fishing grounds** form **where warm and cold ocean currents meet** (mixing brings nutrients and plankton), mainly between **40° and 60°** – asked 4 times (APPSC G1 2002 twice, UPSC 2013 twice).

  [24:3:1.3] Peru–Chile | Humboldt | Upwelling of cold water; fisheries; Atacama desert
  [24:3:1.4] Namibia | Benguela | Namib, Kalahari deserts

[24:3:3] - **Cold currents** on west coasts help form **arid** climates; warm currents warm the coasts beside them; currents keep Earth's **heat balance**.

## SUB 4 | Causes of Ocean Currents
[24:4:0] TABLE head: Factor | Effect
  [24:4:0.0] Planetary winds | Main driver; trades drive equatorial currents west, westerlies drive drifts east
  [24:4:0.1] Coriolis force | Deflects right in the NH, left in the SH → gyres
  [24:4:0.2] Solar heating | Water expands near the equator → slight slope
  [24:4:0.3] Temperature, salinity (density) | Saltier and colder water is denser; cold polar water sinks and creeps slowly to the equator (thermohaline)
  [24:4:0.4] Coastline shape, floor relief | Deflect / steer currents (a landmass does not always obstruct them)
[24:4:1] - **Revolution of the Earth is NOT a cause** (CDS 2013).
[24:4:2] - Ocean currents = large masses of surface water circulating in regular patterns; the slow **surface movement** of water, set in motion **mainly by prevailing winds**, help keep the **heat balance**, are affected by the **configuration of the ocean** – UPSC 2002: **all four** statements correct.
[24:4:3] - **Equatorial Counter Current** flows **west to east** between the North and South Equatorial Currents (through the doldrums) because their **convergence piles up water in the west** (UPSC 2015, asked twice).
[24:4:4] - Surface currents = upper 400 m, about 10% of ocean water; deep currents = 90%.
[24:4:5] - Waves = oscillations moving energy; tides = periodic vertical rise and fall; **currents = continuous horizontal flow** with major effects on **climate, fisheries and navigation**; currents **redistribute heat**, so they influence climate.

## SUB 5 | Gyres and the Sargasso Sea
[24:5:0] TABLE head: Gyre | Components
  [24:5:0.0] North Atlantic | N Equatorial → Gulf Stream → N Atlantic Drift → Canary
  [24:5:0.1] South Atlantic | S Equatorial → Brazil → West Wind Drift → Benguela (no Canary)
  [24:5:0.2] North Pacific | N Equatorial → Kuroshio → N Pacific Drift → California
  [24:5:0.3] South Pacific | S Equatorial → East Australian → West Wind Drift → Humboldt
  [24:5:0.4] South Indian | S Equatorial → Agulhas → West Wind Drift → West Australian
[24:5:1] - Gyres turn **clockwise in the NH**, anticlockwise in the SH.
[24:5:2] - **Sargasso Sea** (**North Atlantic**): the **only sea with no coastline** – bounded by currents: **Gulf Stream (W), North Atlantic Drift (N), Canary (E), North Equatorial (S)**; calm, warm, salty; named after its floating **Sargassum** weed (**typical marine vegetation**).

## SUB 6 | Indian Ocean Currents
[24:6:0] - **North Indian Ocean** currents **reverse with the monsoon** (**monsoon drift**) – the reason the Indian Ocean's currents change direction (UPSC 1997, asked twice); the South Indian Ocean keeps a permanent anticlockwise gyre.
[24:6:1] TABLE head: Season | Bay of Bengal / Arabian Sea | WICC | EICC
  [24:6:1.0] SW monsoon (summer) | Clockwise | Southward | Northward
  [24:6:1.1] NE monsoon (winter) | Anticlockwise | Northward (poleward), November–February | Southward
[24:6:2] - **Findlater Current / Somali Jet**: western **Arabian Sea**; upwelling off the **Horn of Africa**.

=== NOTES 4:25 | Ocean-atmosphere Interaction – El Nino, La Nina, Enso, Iod, Walker Circulation
## SUB 0 | El Nino – Meaning, Mechanism and Effects
[25:0:0] # What El Nino is (asked 9 times)
[25:0:1] - **El Nino** = an **abnormally warm ocean current** that appears off the coast of **Peru and Ecuador** in the **eastern (and central) tropical Pacific** and flows south along the west coast of South America, **replacing the cold Peruvian (Humboldt) current**. It is an **ocean** event, not an air mass, a wind or a low-pressure centre (CDS 2011).
[25:0:2] - **Ocean:** El Nino develops only in the **Pacific Ocean** – not in the Indian Ocean, Bay of Bengal, Atlantic or Mediterranean.
[25:0:3] - **Name:** Spanish for **"the little boy / Christ child"**, because it appears around **Christmas (December)**.
[25:0:4] - **Recurrence:** **irregular**, about every **3–7 years** (2–7 in some books); it does **not occur every year** (MPPSC 2024 wrong statement).
[25:0:5] - The El Nino current carries **warmer, less saline** water (the warm, rain-freshened pool of the western Pacific spreads east) – NDA 2010 "all four" statements.
[25:0:6] - **Atmospheric counterpart = the Southern Oscillation**; together they are **ENSO**.
[25:0:7] # Mechanism
[25:0:8] TABLE head: Step | Normal year | El Nino year
  [25:0:8.0] Pressure | Low over Indonesia / N Australia, high off Peru | Pressure falls over the central/eastern Pacific; a weak high forms in the west
  [25:0:8.1] Trade winds | Strong easterlies push warm water west | Weaken or even reverse
  [25:0:8.2] Peru coast | Cold, nutrient-rich upwelling – rich fishing | Equatorial counter current piles warm water off Peru; thermocline drops; upwelling stops
  [25:0:8.3] Peru sea temperature | Cold | Warms by up to about 10°C
  [25:0:8.4] Plankton and fish | Abundant (anchoveta) | Plankton falls; fish catch collapses – the warm water does not increase plankton (UPPSC 2023: statement 2 false)
[25:0:9] # World effects
[25:0:10] TABLE head: Region | El Nino effect
  [25:0:10.0] Peru, Ecuador, coastal Colombia, N Chile | Heavy rain and flooding of the normally arid coast (APPSC FSO "planetary climatic event")
  [25:0:10.1] Indonesia, northern Australia | Drought, drier
  [25:0:10.2] Central Pacific | Convective storms, hurricanes
  [25:0:10.3] India | Delayed, weak monsoon; drought (e.g. 2014 deficit, about 12% below normal; 2014–16 El Nino droughts)
[25:0:11] - APPSC Group 1: Peru warm current + "Christ child" + 2014 Indian rainfall deficit = **all three correct**.

## SUB 1 | ENSO, La Nina and the Indian Monsoon
[25:1:0] # Southern Oscillation and ENSO
[25:1:1] - **Southern Oscillation** = a **pressure seesaw between the Pacific and Indian Oceans** – **Tahiti (French Polynesia) vs Port Darwin (N Australia)**; first described by **Sir Gilbert Walker (1920s)**.
[25:1:2] - **SOI** = Tahiti minus Darwin pressure. **Positive SOI** (high at Tahiti, low at Darwin) = good Indian monsoon, **La Nina**-like; sustained **negative SOI = El Nino**, bad monsoon.
[25:1:3] - **ENSO** = El Nino + Southern Oscillation; detected and used for **short-term (seasonal) climate prediction** of **weak Indian monsoon** years (UPSC 2002, asked twice).
[25:1:4] - **Monsoon and ENSO are inversely related** (APPSC Fisheries ADF).
[25:1:5] # El Nino vs La Nina
[25:1:6] TABLE head: Feature | El Nino | La Nina
  [25:1:6.0] Central/east Pacific sea temperature | Warmer | Cooler (strong upwelling)
  [25:1:6.1] Trade winds | Weak / reversed | Stronger than usual
  [25:1:6.2] Walker circulation | Weakens, shifts east | Strengthens
  [25:1:6.3] Indian monsoon | Weak, below normal – drought | Normal to above normal – good rain
  [25:1:6.4] North India winter | – | Colder, frequent cold waves (e.g. January 2012)
  [25:1:6.5] Bay of Bengal | Fewer post-monsoon cyclones (INCOIS study) | More floods, severe cyclones
  [25:1:6.6] Australia / Indonesia | Drier | Heavy rain, floods (2010–11 Australian floods)
[25:1:7] - **La Nina** is a cooling of the **central/eastern equatorial Pacific** – **not the Indian Ocean** – and it **does affect** the monsoon (favourably). So both statements of UPSC 2011 are **wrong**.
[25:1:8] - Over India, El Nino = harbinger of drought; **La Nina brings rain** (APPSC Group 1). A **positive IOD** can also offset El Nino, but the Pacific opposite of El Nino is La Nina.
[25:1:9] - Factor affecting India's climate among El Nino, Gulf Stream, western intensification, North Atlantic Oscillation = **El Nino**.
[25:1:10] - Not every El Nino year is a drought year: about **6 of 10** El Nino years brought depressed Indian rainfall; 1997–98 strong El Nino, no drought (positive IOD); moderate 2002 El Nino, severe drought.
[25:1:11] - El Nino and La Nina are **climate patterns, not storms**. Last global El Nino: **2023–24**; **2025** = warmest La Nina year on record.
[25:1:12] ~~ Sources: NextGen Indian Geography - APPSC Exams 06 p.14 (book p.84); PMF Physical Geography 06 p.49, 51 (book p.413, 415); Rocket General Geography p.54; app notes 4:25 sub 1, 4
## SUB 2 | Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD) and Madden-Julian Oscillation (MJO)
[25:2:0] # Indian Ocean Dipole ("Indian Nino")
[25:2:1] - **IOD** = difference in **sea-surface temperature** between **two poles of the tropical Indian Ocean**: a **western pole (Arabian Sea / off Africa)** and an **eastern pole (south of Indonesia)** – **not the eastern Pacific** (UPSC 2017 statement 1 false).
[25:2:2] - Identified in **1999** (Saji et al., IISc); develops from about April, peaks in October; atmospheric part = **EQUINOO**.
[25:2:3] TABLE head: IOD phase | West (Arabian Sea) | East (south of Indonesia) | India
  [25:2:3.0] Positive | Warmer | Cooler, dry | More monsoon rain; can offset El Nino (1983, 1994, 1997); more Arabian Sea cyclones
  [25:2:3.1] Negative | Cooler | Warmer, rainy | Reduced rainfall; more Bay of Bengal cyclones
[25:2:4] - **An IOD can influence an El Nino's impact on the monsoon** (UPSC 2017 statement 2 true → answer **2 only**). In **1997** a strong **positive IOD** saved India from drought despite a **Super El Nino**.
[25:2:5] - The IOD is a separate Indian Ocean mode that **runs independently of ENSO** but interacts with it; positive IOD events often develop in El Nino years. (NextGen's notes pair the positive phase with La Nina – PMF and Rocket give the effect on India as above.)
[25:2:6] # Madden-Julian Oscillation
[25:2:7] - Identified by **Roland Madden and Paul Julian (1971–72)**: an **eastward**-moving pulse of clouds and rain that starts over the warm **tropical Indian Ocean** and fades in the eastern Pacific.
[25:2:8] - Recurs every **30–60 days**; convection up to about 10 km; causes **active and break spells** of the monsoon.
[25:2:9] ~~ Sources: PMF Physical Geography 06 p.51-52 (book p.415-416); NextGen Indian Geography - APPSC Exams 06 p.11, 07 p.1 (book p.81, 85); Rocket General Geography p.54; en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Ocean_Dipole (Saji et al. 1999, Nature); app notes 4:25 sub 6, 7
## SUB 3 | Walker Circulation, El Nino Types and ENSO Monitoring
[25:3:0] # Walker circulation (normal year)
[25:3:1] - East–west (zonal) loop over the tropical Pacific: air **rises over the warm western Pacific / Indonesia**, flows east aloft, **sinks over the cool eastern Pacific**; surface **easterly trade winds** return west. Driven by **high pressure over the eastern Pacific and low over Indonesia**; linked to the Peru upwelling.
[25:3:2] - El Nino **weakens** (sometimes reverses) the Walker cell and shifts it east; La Nina strengthens it.
[25:3:3] - **Bjerknes feedback:** weaker trades → warm water shifts east → winds weaken further → air sinks over the Indian Ocean (high pressure) → **late or weak Indian monsoon**.
[25:3:4] # Types of El Nino
[25:3:5] TABLE head: Type | Maximum warming
  [25:3:5.0] Canonical (conventional) | Eastern Pacific, near Peru
  [25:3:5.1] El Nino Modoki (Japanese: "similar but different") | Central Pacific, with cooler water on both the eastern and western sides (two-cell Walker circulation)
[25:3:6] # Monitoring and 2026 outlook
[25:3:7] - **Nino 3.4 region:** **5°N–5°S, 120°W–170°W** (central equatorial Pacific).
[25:3:8] - **NOAA:** El Nino when the **Oceanic Nino Index** (3-month SST anomaly in Nino 3.4) is **≥ +0.5°C** for **5 consecutive overlapping seasons**. IOD is measured by the **Dipole Mode Index (DMI)**.
[25:3:9] - **IMD, 13 April 2026:** southwest monsoon forecast **92% of LPA (±5%)** – below normal – as **El Nino** was expected to develop during the season (LPA 87 cm).
[25:3:10] ~~ Sources: PMF Physical Geography 06 p.48-50 (book p.412-414); newsonair.gov.in, "IMD forecasts below normal southwest monsoon rainfall this year" (13 Apr 2026); app notes 4:25 sub 2, 3, 5
[25:3:11] - **Early signal of El Nino:** the sea-level satellite **Sentinel-6 Michael Freilich** tracked an **eastward Kelvin wave** carrying warm water along the equatorial Pacific.
[25:3:12] - **IMD, May 2026:** warned that a developing **super El Nino** in the equatorial Pacific could **weaken** India's 2026 southwest monsoon (PMF Lens May 2026, 8.7).
[25:3:13] - **Super El Nino:** an unusually strong El Nino – Nino 3.4 sea-surface temperature anomaly **above +2.0°C** (1982–83, 1997–98, 2015–16).

## SUB 2 | Coral Reefs – Nature, Conditions and Types
[26:2:0] # Nature
[26:2:1] - Built from the **calcium carbonate** skeletons of tiny colonial **coral polyps** (phylum **Cnidaria**); the CaCO3 is **aragonite**. **Oyster shells** too are **calcium carbonate**.
[26:2:2] - **Mutualism with zooxanthellae** (single-celled dinoflagellate algae): the algae photosynthesise and give the coral **food and colour**; the coral gives **shelter and CO2/nutrients**.
[26:2:3] - **"Rainforests of the Sea"** – the marine counterpart of **tropical rain forests** – because of exceptional **species richness**: under **1%** of the ocean floor, over **25%** of marine species.
[26:2:4] - Reefs **break storm waves** and prevent coastal erosion; also nurseries, fisheries, tourism. APPSC Group 1: both statements true, but wave-breaking does **not explain** the epithet.
[26:2:5] - **Biorock (mineral accretion)**: a small electric current on a steel frame precipitates CaCO3 – used to **restore damaged reefs** (ZSI trial, Gulf of Kachchh) (UPSC 2022).
[26:2:6] # Growth conditions
[26:2:7] TABLE head: Condition | Requirement
  [26:2:7.0] Temperature | Not below about 20°C (20–21°C minimum); optimum 23–25°C; tropical seas
  [26:2:7.1] Light | Photic, shallow water – under about 50 m (Leong: not over 180 ft; ideal under 100 ft)
  [26:2:7.2] Water | Clean, sediment-free, normal sea salinity (about 3.5%, not 6%); freshwater and very saline water harm growth
  [26:2:7.3] Avoid | Muddy river mouths, cold currents
[26:2:8] - Astt. Comm. 2017: photic + clean water + 20–21°C = **I, II and IV**; "salinity of 6%" is wrong.

  [26:2:10.0] Fringing | Close to shore; shallow lagoon or none; commonest in India | Andamans, Gulf of Mannar
  [26:2:10.1] Barrier | Parallel to the coast, separated by a wide, deep lagoon | Great Barrier Reef
  [26:2:10.2] Atoll | Ring / horseshoe reef enclosing a lagoon, no central land | Lakshadweep

[26:2:12] - **Subsidence theory** of reef formation: **Charles Darwin (1842)** – fringing reef → barrier reef → atoll as the island slowly sinks (Daly: glacial-control theory).
## SUB 3 | Coral Bleaching and Ocean Acidification
[26:3:0] # Coral bleaching
[26:3:1] - **Meaning:** stressed polyps **expel (eject) their zooxanthellae** and lose pigment, turning **white**; a bleached coral is **stressed, not necessarily dead** (it can recover if conditions improve).
[26:3:2] - **Most significant cause:** **global warming** – rising sea-surface temperature, **marine heatwaves** (1°C above normal for weeks can trigger it); also UV, turbidity, pollution, salinity change, **extreme low tides**, **ocean acidification**, El Nino – **not** more carbonate, not mining or disease as the main cause.
[26:3:3] TABLE head: Global bleaching event | Period | Note
  [26:3:3.0] 1st | 1998 | Strong El Nino
  [26:3:3.1] 2nd | 2010 | –
  [26:3:3.2] 3rd | 2014–2017 | 68.2% of reef areas
  [26:3:3.3] 4th | 2023 – about mid-2025 | About 84% of reefs, 83 countries (NOAA–ICRI)
[26:3:4] - Every strong El Nino since 1998 came with a global bleaching event; recovery takes **more than 15 years**. GCRMN 2026: major disturbances now every **4–6 years**; **87%** of reefs faced heat stress in 2024. **Andaman** mass bleaching linked to about a **2°C** rise in Indian Ocean temperature.
[26:3:5] # Ocean acidification
[26:3:6] - CO2 + water → **carbonic acid** → bicarbonate + hydrogen (hydronium) ions → **pH falls** (sea water about **8.1**, alkaline) → **carbonate ions fall** → corals, calcareous plankton and shellfish **form shells less easily** (it does not strengthen reefs).
[26:3:7] - Oceans absorb about **30–40%** of human CO2; worst in **cold seas** (higher CO2 solubility). Nicknames: **"evil twin of global warming"**, "the other CO2 problem".
