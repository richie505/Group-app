=== Landmark Cases – Champakam to Minerva Mills
| Case | Year | Held |
|---|---|---|
| **A.K. Gopalan v. State of Madras** | **1950** | Preventive detention; narrow reading of **Art. 21** (personal liberty) – **not a property case** |
| **State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan** | **1951** | **First FR–DPSP conflict case**: in a conflict **FRs prevail**; DPSPs "run as subsidiary" to Part III; communal G.O. struck down under **Art. 29(2)** |
| In re Kerala Education Bill | 1958 | **Harmonious construction** – give effect to both as far as possible (FRs still prevail in a direct clash) |
| **Golaknath v. State of Punjab** | **1967** | **11 judges**; Parliament **cannot amend/abridge FRs** (FRs "transcendental and immutable"); prospective overruling – the **first time** the SC limited Parliament's amending power |
| **Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala** | **1973** | **13 judges (largest bench)**; FRs can be amended but **not the basic structure** |
| Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India | 1978 | Widened Art. 21 – procedure must be fair, just and reasonable |
| **Minerva Mills v. Union of India** | **1980** | Struck down **Art. 368(4) and 368(5)** and the 42nd Amendment's extension of Art. 31C; **harmony and balance between FRs and DPSPs is part of the basic structure** |
| Sanjeev Coke | 1983 | Art. 39(b) includes privately owned resources (decided after Minerva Mills) |
- **Chronology**: **Gopalan (1950) → Champakam (1951) → Golaknath (1967) → Kesavananda (1973) → Minerva Mills (1980)** → Sanjeev Coke (1983).
- **Golaknath** (Subba Rao CJ): Fundamental Rights "**enable a man to chalk out his life in the manner he likes best**".
- **Bench size**: Kesavananda **13** is the largest; Golaknath **11** is the usual "second largest" answer – but R.C. Cooper (Bank Nationalisation, 1970) and T.M.A. Pai (2002) also sat **11** judges; Minerva Mills had **5**.
- **Property and amending power**: Golaknath, Kesavananda and Minerva Mills; **Gopalan** is about personal liberty.
- **Champakam → 1st Amendment, 1951** inserted **Art. 15(4)** – the right changed was the **right against discrimination (Art. 15)**, overcoming the **Art. 29(2)** bar; 15(4) enables special provisions for **socially and educationally backward classes, SCs and STs**.

=== FRs vs DPSPs – Differences and Relationship
| Point | Fundamental Rights | DPSPs |
|---|---|---|
| Courts | **Justiciable** – law violating an FR is **void under Art. 13** (to the extent of inconsistency) | **Non-justiciable (Art. 37)** – a law cannot be struck down merely for breaking a DPSP |
| Nature | **Negative** – restrain the State | **Positive** – directions to the State |
| Aim | **Political democracy** | **Social and economic democracy** (welfare State) |
| Sanction | Legal | Moral and political |
| Role | **Means** ("lay down the means") | **Ends / goals** (Justice Grover: "prescribe the goal") |
- **Art. 37**: DPSPs "shall not be enforced by any court", but are "**fundamental in the governance of the country**" and it is "**the duty of the State to apply these principles in making laws**".
- By and large FRs and DPSPs are **complementary** (supplementary) to each other, not contradictory; DPSPs **supplement FRs** in achieving a welfare State. SC: DPSPs are the "**conscience of the Constitution**".
- **Parliament can amend FRs to implement DPSPs**, provided the **basic structure** is not damaged – so "Parliament cannot amend FRs for implementing DPSPs" is the **wrong** statement.
- **Who amends FRs**: **Parliament** under **Art. 368** (special majority).
- The **Preamble** may be used to determine the ambit of both FRs and DPSPs.

=== First Amendment, Article 31B and the Ninth Schedule
- **1st Amendment, 1951** (PM **Jawaharlal Nehru**): added **Arts. 31A, 31B** and the **Ninth Schedule** for the **protection of agrarian (land) reforms in certain States**; also Art. 15(4).
- **Art. 31B**: Acts in the Ninth Schedule are not void for inconsistency with Part III – **retrospective**; it is **still operative** (I.R. Coelho did not end it, only opened such laws to review).
- Ninth Schedule protects **Central and State laws** from challenge on the ground of FRs; entries are **not limited to land reform** (grown to **over 280**); entries are added only by **Parliament through a constitutional amendment**, not by the Central Government.
| Case | Year | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| **Waman Rao** | 1981 | Laws added after **24 April 1973** (Kesavananda date) open to challenge |
| **I.R. Coelho** | **2007** | No blanket immunity – such laws tested on basic structure and Arts. **14, 19, 21** ("golden triangle") |
> APPSC G1 Prelims 2024: Ninth Schedule – only statements I (protects Central and State laws) and II (not only land reform) are correct.

=== Article 31C and the Amendments of 1971–1976
| Stage | What happened |
|---|---|
| **24th Amendment, 1971** | Answer to **Golaknath**: Parliament can amend **any part including FRs**; added **Art. 13(4)** and **368(3)**; President's assent to amendment bills made **mandatory** |
| **25th Amendment, 1971** | Answer to **R.C. Cooper (Bank Nationalisation, 1970)**: "compensation" → "**amount**"; **inserted Art. 31C** – (1) laws for **Art. 39(b) and (c)** not void for violating Arts. **14, 19 (and 31)**; (2) a declaration that a law gives effect to that policy cannot be questioned in court |
| 26th Amendment, 1971 | **Abolished privy purses** and privileges of former rulers (answer to the Privy Purses case, 1970) |
| **Kesavananda, 1973** | First provision of 31C **upheld**; **second provision** (bar on judicial review) **struck down** |
| **42nd Amendment, 1976** | Extended the **first provision** of 31C to laws implementing **any DPSP** – gave DPSPs **primacy over Arts. 14 and 19** |
| **Minerva Mills, 1980** | Struck down that **extension**; 31C back to **39(b) and (c)** only |
- **Primacy of DPSPs over FRs** = the **42nd Amendment** (the usual answer; the 25th gave only Art. 39(b)-(c) laws limited primacy; the 44th did not do this).
- Earlier: **4th Amendment, 1955** – adequacy of compensation made non-justiciable; **17th, 1964** – widened "estate" in Art. 31A.
- Minerva Mills also held that a **proclamation of Emergency is subject to judicial review**.
- **Present position**: 31C protects only **39(b)-(c)** laws; **Property Owners Association (2024)** reaffirmed that the original 31C (as upheld in Kesavananda) continues.
> APPSC G1 Prelims 2024: I (first provision struck in Kesavananda) – **wrong**, it was the second; II ("extension of the second provision") – **wrong**, the 42nd extended the first; III (Minerva = harmony) – correct; IV (present position = Champakam) – **wrong**. Incorrect = **I, II and IV**.

=== Harmonious Construction and Article 39(b)
- **Harmonious construction**: read Part III and Part IV together so both operate – **In re Kerala Education Bill (1958)**, **Minerva Mills (1980)**.
- **Minerva Mills**: the balance between Parts III and IV is part of the **basic structure**; absolute primacy to either destroys it; "FRs and DPSPs are **complementary**".
- DPSPs strengthen FRs: **48A + Art. 21** = healthy environment; **45 + Art. 21** → **Art. 21A** (**86th Amendment, 2002**). Non-justiciability does **not** exclude DPSPs from interpretation.
| Case | Year | View on Art. 39(b) |
|---|---|---|
| Ranganatha Reddy | 1977 | **Justice V.R. Krishna Iyer** (minority): includes all privately owned resources |
| Sanjeev Coke | 1983 | Followed the Krishna Iyer view |
| **Property Owners Association v. State of Maharashtra** | 5 Nov 2024 | **9 judges, 7:2**, CJI **D.Y. Chandrachud**: not every private resource is a "material resource of the community" |
- Test (2024): the resource's **nature, scarcity, effect on community well-being** and the consequences of its **concentration in private hands**.
