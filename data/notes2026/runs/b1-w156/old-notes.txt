## SUB 0 | The English in Andhra – Globe, Machilipatnam and Armagaon
[157:0:0] - **1611**: the ship **Globe** under **Captain (Anthony) Hippon** – not Hawkins – reached **Machilipatnam** (January 1611), with **Peter Floris** (a Dutchman in English service) as merchant.
[157:0:1] - Permission from the Golconda Sultan **Muhammad Quli Qutb Shah** → **first English factory in Andhra / South India / on the east coast = Machilipatnam (1611)** – not Kakinada, Visakhapatnam, Bheemunipatnam or Golconda.
[157:0:2] - A-R (APPSC G2 2025): "Globe under Hawkins" is **false**; "Quli Qutb Shah permitted the factory" is **true** → (A) false, (R) true.

  [157:0:3.5] 1682 | Visakhapatnam |

## SUB 1 | First Europeans in Andhra – Order of the Factories
[157:1:0] - **First Europeans to trade with India = Portuguese (1498)**; order of arrival: **Portuguese → Dutch → English → French**.
[157:1:1] - **First trade contacts with the Andhra region = Portuguese** (16th century, from San Thome and Pulicat) – but they opened a factory at Machilipatnam only in **1670**.
[157:1:2] - **First European factory in Andhra = Dutch, Machilipatnam, 1605** (NextGen; Spectrum) – "first factory / trade centre at Machilipatnam" = **Dutch**, not Portuguese.
[157:1:3] TABLE head: Order | Power | Machilipatnam factory | Permission
  [157:1:3.0] 1st | Dutch | 1605 | Muhammad Quli Qutb Shah
  [157:1:3.1] 2nd | English | 1611 | Muhammad Quli Qutb Shah
  [157:1:3.2] 3rd | French | 1669 | Abdullah Qutb Shah
  [157:1:3.3] 4th | Portuguese | 1670 | Abdullah Qutb Shah
[157:1:4] - Why the Europeans came: coastal Andhra's position on the **Bay of Bengal** for sea trade, its **chintz** and **diamonds**; Hyderabad founded **1591** by Muhammad Quli Qutb Shah.
[157:1:5] - **Tavernier** (17th-century French jeweller) visited the Andhra (Golconda) mines for diamonds; **Marco Polo** visited **Motupalli**.
[157:1:6] - **Portuguese** contributions: crops such as **tobacco**, chilli, cashew (to check soil erosion in Goa); Arabian Sea monopoly until the **17th century**; **Blue Water Policy** of **Almeida**.

[157:1:8] - **Pre-colonial economy:** Andhra's dominant activity was **textile production** and export – Masulipatnam chintz and calico.

=== NOTES 1:158 | Trade Centres of Coastal Andhra – Masulipatnam, Nizampatnam, Bheemunipatnam, Yanam
## SUB 0 | Machilipatnam – the Great Port of the Qutb Shahis
[158:0:0] - **Machilipatnam (Masulipatnam)** = the **most famous port town for (handloom) textile trade in the 16th-17th centuries** and the **chief trading centre of Andhra with the world before the 19th century** – not Motupalli, Visakhapatnam, Narsapur, Korangi or Bheemunipatnam.
[158:0:1] - Under **Golconda** in the 1600s it was famous for **textiles and diamonds** (also rubies) – not silver, gold or platinum.
[158:0:2] - Principal maritime gateway of the **Qutb Shahis**; passed to the Mughals when **Aurangzeb annexed Golconda (1687)**; **Komatis** were the key merchant community.
[158:0:3] - Ptolemy's **Maisolia (Mysolia)**, the "great market" = Machilipatnam (Kantakossyla = Ghantasala).
[158:0:4] - **First English factory in South India = Masulipatam, 1611** (Vizagapatam came in 1682).
[158:0:5] TABLE head: Export | Centre
  [158:0:5.0] Chintz (glazed, brighter after washing), palampores, kalamkari | Machilipatnam
  [158:0:5.1] Saltpetre | Machilipatnam, Narsapur, Pulicat
  [158:0:5.2] Diamonds, rubies | Golconda mines; Koh-i-Noor from Kollur (Guntur)
  [158:0:5.3] Silk and cotton piece-goods | Bengal
  [158:0:5.4] Indigo | Nagulavancha, Nalgonda
  [158:0:5.5] Salt | Pedaganjam, Chinaganjam
  [158:0:5.6] Cheap calico | Narasapuram
  [158:0:5.7] Taanis | Injaram
  [158:0:5.8] Rumaals (kerchiefs) | Nellore
[158:0:6] - Textiles = dominant industry, second only to diamonds in the Qutb Shahi economy; **pintado** = printed textile; English purchases were financed with **bullion**.
[158:0:7] - Imports: **horses** from Ormuz/Persia, **elephants** from Ceylon, **camphor** from Punjab – credited to the Reddi-period merchant **Avachi Tippaya Setti**.

[158:0:9] - **First Golconda farman for a Masulipatnam factory:** the **Dutch** (1605–06), not the Portuguese; Masulipatnam rose as a maritime centre in the **last quarter of the 16th century**.
## SUB 1 | Ports Before the Europeans – Motupalli and Ghantasala
[158:1:0] - **Motupalli (Desiyakondapattanam)**, now in Bapatla district = the **most important seaport of the Kakatiya kingdom** – not Machilipatnam (Qutb Shahi port), Kakinada, Nellore or Dharanikota.
[158:1:1] - **Marco Polo** visited it in **1287** under **Rudrama Devi**; **Ganapatideva's Abhaya Sasanam** protected foreign traders; Reddi minister **Somaya** issued another; **Anavota Reddi** lifted customs duties.
[158:1:2] - **Haravilasam** of **Srinatha**, dedicated to **Avachi Tippaya Setti**, describes the **sea trade of the Reddi period** – not Kashikhandam, Harivamsa or Bhimeswara Purana.
[158:1:3] - **Roman coins** in Andhra: **Bhattiprolu** (NextGen previous-paper key), Nagarjunakonda, Vinukonda, Vijayawada, Attirala, Salihundam; **Ghantasala** (Ptolemy's **Kantakossyla**) and **Koddura** were the Satavahana ports of Indo-Roman trade.
[158:1:4] - **Penugonda** = central trade centre of medieval Andhra.

## SUB 2 | Shipbuilding – Narasapuram
[158:2:0] - **Narasapuram / Narsapur** (West Godavari, Godavari delta) = the **chief shipbuilding centre of coastal Andhra in the 16th-17th centuries** – not Machilipatnam, Kakinada (Cocanada), Nagapattinam, Quilon or Madras.
[158:2:1] - **Methold** described large, strongly built ships there under the Qutb Shahis; good timber and a sheltered river mouth.
[158:2:2] - **Corangi** = a Company-era shipbuilding site; **odaberam** = sea-going ships (Shuka Saptati).
[158:2:3] ~~ Sources: NextGen AP History - APPSC Part 15 p.22 (ships and boats: Narasapuram, Machilipatnam); old app notes row 158

=== NOTES 1:159 | First Carnatic War – Causes, Battle of St. Thome, Treaty of Aix-la-chapelle
## SUB 0 | First Carnatic War (1746-48) – Causes and Course
[159:0:0] - Carnatic Wars were fought between the **English and the French** (not English–Dutch, English–Marathas or Haider Ali–Marathas).
[159:0:1] - Background: an **extension of the War of Austrian Succession** in Europe and Anglo-French rivalry.
[159:0:2] - **Immediate cause**: the **English navy under Barnet seized French ships** → France retaliated (the Austrian war and the Carnatic throne were background, not the immediate cause).
[159:0:3] - **Sept 1746**: **La Bourdonnais** with the fleet from **Mauritius** captured **Madras**; **Dupleix** refused to ratify his ransom deal.
[159:0:4] - **Anwaruddin** (guardian of the minor Nawab sent by Nizam-ul-Mulk; Nawab of Arcot from 1744) took the English side – later killed at **Ambur (1749)**.
[159:0:5] - The **European** traders got their chance to interfere in Indian politics when Indian rulers such as the Nizams of Hyderabad enlisted their help against rivals.
[159:0:6] ~~ Sources: Spectrum Modern India p.76-77 (book p.44-45); NextGen Indian History - APPSC Exams Part 5 p.34; old app notes row 159
## SUB 1 | Battle of St. Thome and the Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle
[159:1:0] - **Battle of St. Thome (Adyar), 1746**: a small French force (about **930**) under **Captain Paradis** defeated about **10,000** troops of Nawab Anwaruddin under **Mahfuz Khan**.
[159:1:1] - Lesson: a small disciplined European army could beat a large Indian army; **naval power** would decide the contest.
[159:1:2] - **Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle (1748)** ended the War of Austrian Succession **and** the First Carnatic War; **Madras returned to the English** in exchange for **Louisbourg** (North America) → both statements correct (UPPCS 2025).
[159:1:3] - Political result **nil** – pre-war position restored.

  [159:1:4.1] Second | 1749-54 | Pondicherry (1754-55)
  [159:1:4.2] Third | 1756/58-63 | Paris (1763) – ended French political power

=== NOTES 1:161 | Third Carnatic War – Battle of Wandiwash, Treaty of Paris (1763); Reasons for English Success
## SUB 0 | Battle of Wandiwash (1760) and Lally's Campaign
[161:0:0] - **Third Carnatic War (1758-63)** = Indian theatre of the **Seven Years' War (1756-63)**; NextGen dates it 1757-63, some books 1756-63.
[161:0:1] - **1758:** **Count de Lally** took **Fort St. David** (Cuddalore) and Vizianagaram; failed before **Madras** (no money, no naval support) and in a raid on **Thanjavur** for funds.
[161:0:2] - Lally **recalled Bussy from Hyderabad** – a blunder that cost the French the Deccan; he quarrelled with his officers, did not understand Indian conditions and left troops unpaid.
[161:0:3] - **Battle of Wandiwash (Vandavasi, Tamil Nadu), 22 January 1760:** **General Eyre Coote** (English) routed **Count de Lally** (French); **Bussy taken prisoner**.
[161:0:4] - Result: **British defeated the French** – not the Portuguese, Dutch, Danes, Mysore or the Carnatic Nawab; French political power in India ended.
[161:0:5] - Wandiwash **removed the last European rival** to British power (the Dutch had lost at **Bidara, 1759**); some historians call it the true turning point, though Plassey is usually called decisive.
[161:0:6] - **Siege of Pondicherry:** Lally held it **eight months**, surrendered **16 January 1761**; **Jinji** and **Mahe** also fell; Lally was later executed in France (1766).
[161:0:7] TABLE head: Person | Side | Role
  [161:0:7.0] Eyre Coote | English | Won Wandiwash (not Clive)
  [161:0:7.1] Pocock | English | Admiral; beat d'Aché
  [161:0:7.2] Count de Lally | French | Fort St. David 1758; lost Wandiwash; surrendered Pondicherry 1761
  [161:0:7.3] Bussy | French | Taken prisoner at Wandiwash
  [161:0:7.4] d'Aché | French | Admiral; fleet beaten off Pondicherry, retired to Mauritius
[161:0:8] > Lally surrendered at **Pondicherry (1761)**; Bussy was captured at **Wandiwash (1760)**.
[161:0:9] ~~ Sources: Spectrum Modern India p.81-82; NextGen Indian History APPSC Part 5 p.18; PMF Modern History Part 2 p.18-20; old app notes row 161
## SUB 1 | Why the English Won and the French Failed
[161:1:0] TABLE head: Factor | English | French
  [161:1:0.0] Company | Private enterprise; board of directors elected yearly; quick decisions | State concern – controlled by the French government (king held over 60% of shares; dividend guaranteed)
  [161:1:0.1] Navy | Superior navy cut the sea link to France | Fleet beaten; no sea support
  [161:1:0.2] Money | Commercial interests never neglected; Bengal's revenue and men after Plassey (1757) | Commerce subordinated to territorial ambition → short of funds
  [161:1:0.3] Bases | Calcutta, Bombay, Madras | Only Pondicherry
  [161:1:0.4] Leaders | Eyre Coote, Stringer Lawrence, Clive | Dupleix (recalled 1754), Lally
[161:1:1] - Strongest single answer to "why the French failed in the Deccan": **the English were the stronger military power** (navy, army, commanders) – not "Pondicherry was not strategic".
[161:1:2] - **CDS 2011:** the French company was **heavily dependent on the French government**.
[161:1:3] - The Company's wars were paid for from **money raised in India** – local taxes in its fortified towns and, after 1757-65, **Bengal's revenue** – not by the British government.
[161:1:4] - **Plassey → Carnatic:** Clive's victory made Bengal's revenue available for the southern campaign; by 1758 Clive had also taken French **Chandernagore**.
[161:1:5] > Private + navy + Bengal money + three presidencies = English; state-run + no navy + only Pondicherry = French.
[161:1:6] ~~ Sources: Spectrum Modern India p.83, 85-86, 117-118; PMF Modern History Part 2 p.19-20; old app notes row 161
## SUB 2 | Treaty of Paris (1763)
[161:2:0] - Ended the **Seven Years' War** and the **Third Carnatic War** – not London, Madras or Mahe; Allahabad (1765) is the treaty after Buxar.
[161:2:1] - French factories **restored only as trading posts**; **could not be fortified**; French lived under British protection.
[161:2:2] - Enclaves kept (five): **Pondicherry, Karaikal, Mahe, Yanam, Chandernagore** – **Machilipatnam was not** (it had gone to the English through Salabat Jang's 1759 grant).
[161:2:3] - After 1763 the English were the **supreme European power** in India.
[161:2:4] ~~ Sources: PMF Modern History Part 2 p.19-20; Spectrum Modern India p.82, 90; old app notes row 161

=== NOTES 1:162 | Anglo-french Rivalry – the Three Carnatic Wars and the English Ascendancy
## SUB 0 | The Three Carnatic Wars at a Glance
[162:0:0] TABLE head: War | Years | European link | Battle | Treaty | Result
  [162:0:0.0] First | 1746-48 (Spectrum 1740-48) | Austrian War of Succession | St. Thome (Adyar), 1746 | Aix-la-Chapelle (1748) | Madras returned to the English for Louisburg (N. America); no lasting supremacy
  [162:0:0.1] Second | 1749-54 (not 1750-55) | None – Hyderabad and Carnatic successions | Ambur 1749; Arcot 1751 | Pondicherry (1754) | Inconclusive ("undecided"); Dupleix recalled
  [162:0:0.2] Third | 1758-63 (also given 1756-63) | Seven Years' War | Wandiwash 1760 | Paris (1763) | Decisive English victory
[162:0:1] - **Yandabo (1826)** ended the First Anglo-Burmese War – never a Carnatic treaty.
[162:0:2] - **Outcome:** the English emerged supreme – by 1761 their influence was firm at **Arcot** (Muhammad Ali) and at the **Nizam's court**; they became the **kingmakers of South India**; the French were left with trading enclaves.
[162:0:3] - Small disciplined European armies beat far larger Indian armies (lesson of St. Thome).
[162:0:4] - Chronology: capture of Madras (1746) → Aix-la-Chapelle (1748) → Pondicherry (1754) → Paris (1763).
[162:0:5] > First = Aix-la-Chapelle; Second = Pondicherry (inconclusive); Third = Paris (Wandiwash).

## SUB 1 | First Carnatic War (1746-48)
[162:1:0] - Fought between the **British and the French** companies; an **extension of the Austrian War of Succession**.
[162:1:1] - **Immediate cause:** the English navy under **Barnett** **seized French ships**; France retaliated by seizing **Madras (Fort St. George), 1746** with **La Bourdonnais**'s fleet from Mauritius.
[162:1:2] - La Bourdonnais agreed to **ransom Madras** back to the English, but **Dupleix overruled him** and kept it.
[162:1:3] - **Battle of St. Thome (Adyar), 1746:** **Captain Paradis** with about **930** French beat about **10,000** men of **Nawab Anwaruddin** (under Mahfuz Khan); the Nawab had sided with the English.
[162:1:4] - Ended by **Aix-la-Chapelle (1748)**; did **not** give permanent British supremacy.
[162:1:5] ~~ Sources: Spectrum Modern India p.76-77; NextGen Indian History APPSC Part 5 p.18; old app notes row 162
## SUB 2 | Second Carnatic War (1749-54) – Dupleix, Ambur and Arcot
[162:2:0] TABLE head: Dispute | English candidate | French candidate
  [162:2:0.0] Hyderabad (Nizam-ul-Mulk died 1748) | Nasir Jang (son) | Muzaffar Jang (grandson)
  [162:2:0.1] Carnatic | Anwaruddin, then his son Muhammad Ali | Chanda Sahib
[162:2:1] - **Dupleix** – Governor of Pondicherry (from **1742**; Director-General 1741), first European to interfere in Indian succession politics; originator of the subsidiary-force idea (French army at Hyderabad).
[162:2:2] - **Ambur (near Vellore), 1749:** Muzaffar Jang, Chanda Sahib and the French killed **Anwaruddin**; Muzaffar Jang became Subahdar of the Deccan and **rewarded the French** with grants (Masulipatnam, lands near Pondicherry).
[162:2:3] - **Arcot, 1751:** **Clive** seized it with about **210** men and held it **53 days**; then won **Arni and Kaveripak** over **Raja Sahib and Chanda Sahib**.
[162:2:4] - **Trichinopoly:** **Stringer Lawrence** and Clive forced the French commander **Law** to surrender (1752); Chanda Sahib executed.
[162:2:5] - **Bussy** kept **Salabat Jang** in power at Hyderabad and won the **Northern Circars (1753)** for the French.
[162:2:6] - **Godeheu** replaced Dupleix and signed the **Treaty of Pondicherry (1754)**: no interference in princes' quarrels, each kept its possessions; the **Seven Years' War broke out before it was ratified** → Third war.

## SUB 0 | Battle of Plassey (23 June 1757)
[163:0:0] - **Date/place:** **23 June 1757**, **Palashi (Plassey)** on the **Bhagirathi**, **Nadia district, West Bengal** (south of the Nawab's capital Murshidabad) – not Bihar or Orissa.
[163:0:1] - **Sides:** **Robert Clive** (Company, about 3,000) defeated **Siraj-ud-daula** (about 50,000); **Mir Jafar** was a conspirator on Clive's side, not the opponent.
[163:0:2] - **Conspirators:** **Mir Jafar** (Mir Bakshi, commander), **Rai Durlabh** (Diwan), **Jagat Seth** ("world banker"), **Omichund** (deceived with a decoy treaty), Yar Lutuf Khan.
[163:0:3] - **Loyal to Siraj:** **Mir Madan** and **Mohan Lal**.
[163:0:4] - Siraj was captured and murdered on the order of Mir Jafar's son **Miran**.
[163:0:5] - **Significance:** the **Company's first decisive military success**; regarded as the **foundation / starting point of British rule** in India; paved the way for **British mastery of Bengal**; Bengal's wealth began to flow to Britain (start of the drain).
[163:0:6] - Won by **treachery** – "a transaction", not a prolonged contest between equal armies; Buxar (1764) **confirmed** it.
[163:0:7] - **Gains:** Mir Jafar Nawab; **zamindari of the 24 Parganas**; duty-free trade; Bengal's money used to defeat the French.
[163:0:8] - **Siyar-ul-Mutakherin** (contemporary Persian history covering Plassey) – **Ghulam Husain** Khan Tabatabai.
[163:0:9] - **Robert Clive** = "**Founder of the British Empire in India**"; "**heaven-born general**" (**William Pitt the Elder**).

## SUB 0 | Treaty of Allahabad (August 1765) and the Diwani
[164:0:0] - **Clive** returned to Calcutta in **May 1765** as Governor of Bengal for the **second** time and made two treaties at Allahabad in **August 1765**.
[164:0:1] TABLE head: Party | Terms
  [164:0:1.0] Shah Alam II | Farman granting the Diwani of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa (dated 12 August 1765) for Rs 26 lakh a year; got Kora and Allahabad; lived at Allahabad under Company protection → first Mughal pensioner of the Company
  [164:0:1.1] Shuja-ud-daula (Awadh) | Dominions restored except Kora and Allahabad; war indemnity Rs 50 lakh; duty-free trade for the Company in Awadh; Banaras left to Balwant Singh; Awadh = buffer state
  [164:0:1.2] Nawab of Bengal | Company paid Rs 53 lakh for nizamat; real power with the Company
[164:0:2] - **Grantor of the Diwani:** the **Mughal emperor Shah Alam II** – not the Nawab, not Farrukhsiyar or Shuja; year **1765** (not 1757/1760/1763).
[164:0:3] - Diwani = the Company's **legal foothold**: right to collect revenue and administer **civil justice**.
[164:0:4] - **After Bengal**, duty-free trade was next secured in **Awadh** (Nawab of Awadh).
[164:0:5] - **Varanasi was not given to Shah Alam** – he got only Kora and Allahabad.
[164:0:6] - **Deputy Diwan of Murshidabad:** **Mohammad Reza Khan**, appointed by Clive (also Naib Subahdar under Najm-ud-daula).
[164:0:7] - **Khasis** – the first hill tribe the British came into contact with after the 1765 Diwani (UPSC 2002).
[164:0:8] - **Chronology:** Mir Qasim made Nawab (1760) → Buxar (Oct 1764) → **Clive returns (May 1765)** → **Allahabad (Aug 1765)** → **Warren Hastings Governor (1772)**.
[164:0:9] - **1772:** Hastings **stopped the Rs 26 lakh** to Shah Alam, took back Kora and Allahabad and sold them to Awadh; ruled "by right of conquest".
[164:0:10] TABLE head: Treaty | Year | Context
  [164:0:10.0] Allahabad | 1765 | After Buxar
  [164:0:10.1] Madras | 1769 | With Haidar Ali
  [164:0:10.2] Salbai | 1782 | First Anglo-Maratha War
[164:0:11] > Diwani 1765: Shah Alam II → Company (Clive), Rs 26 lakh; Awadh Rs 50 lakh.
[164:0:12] ~~ Sources: Spectrum Modern India p.124-125; NextGen Indian History APPSC Part 6 p.16, 18; old app notes row 164
## SUB 1 | Battle of Buxar (22 October 1764) – the Most Decisive Battle
[164:1:0] TABLE head: Item | Fact
  [164:1:0.0] Date | 22 October 1764 (some books 22-23 Oct)
  [164:1:0.1] Place | Buxar, Bihar
  [164:1:0.2] British commander | Major Hector Munro (not Clive)
  [164:1:0.3] Allies defeated | Mir Qasim (deposed Nawab of Bengal), Shuja-ud-daula (Nawab of Awadh), Shah Alam II (Mughal emperor = ruler of Delhi)
  [164:1:0.4] Nawab of Bengal at the time | Mir Jafar (restored 1763)
[164:1:1] - Shuja fled, sought help from the **Rohillas and Marathas**, then surrendered; Shah Alam had already joined the English.
[164:1:2] - **Buxar = the most decisive battle** for British supremacy: an **open military victory** over the Nawab of Bengal, the Nawab of Awadh and the Mughal emperor together; it **confirmed Plassey** (Malleson: "amongst the most decisive battles ever fought").
[164:1:3] - Consequence specific to Buxar: **Diwani of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa** and Awadh made a dependent buffer.
[164:1:4] TABLE head: Point | Plassey (1757) | Buxar (1764)
  [164:1:4.0] Nature | Treachery | Open fighting
  [164:1:4.1] Enemy | One Nawab | Bengal + Awadh + Mughal emperor
  [164:1:4.2] Called | Foundation of British rule | Most decisive battle
[164:1:5] - **Pairs:** Buxar – Mir Qasim and allies vs Munro (not Mir Jafar vs Clive); Wandiwash – French vs Company; Chilianwala (1849) – Sikhs; Kharda (1795) – Marathas vs Nizam.
[164:1:6] - **Order:** Ambur (1749) → Plassey (1757) → Wandiwash (1760) → Buxar (1764).
[164:1:7] > Buxar: 1764, Munro, three allies, Mir Jafar was Nawab.
[164:1:8] ~~ Sources: Spectrum Modern India p.123, 126; NextGen Indian History APPSC Part 5 p.18, Part 6 p.15-16; old app notes row 164
## SUB 2 | Mir Qasim (1760-63)
[164:2:0] - **Son-in-law of Mir Jafar**; placed on the throne by Governor **Vansittart (1760)**; earlier **Faujdar of Purnia and Rangpur**; the ablest successor of Alivardi.
[164:2:1] - **Treaty of 1760:** ceded **Burdwan, Midnapur and Chittagong** to the Company.
[164:2:2] - **Capital moved Murshidabad → Munger (Monghyr), Bihar** – to keep away from Calcutta.
[164:2:3] - **Army on European lines** – the Armenian **Gurgin Khan**; **gun factory at Monghyr**.
[164:2:4] - **Abolished all duties on internal trade** (1763) → Indian merchants competed on equal terms; the Company's dastak advantage vanished → war of 1763.
[164:2:5] - Defeated at Katwah, Giria, Munger (1763) → **fled to Awadh** → alliance with Shuja and Shah Alam → Buxar.
[164:2:6] ~~ Sources: Spectrum Modern India p.121-123; NextGen Indian History APPSC Part 6 p.15; old app notes row 164
## SUB 3 | Mir Jafar, Najm-ud-daula and the Plassey Settlement
[164:3:0] - **Mir Jafar – two tenures: 1757-1760 and 1763-1765**.
[164:3:1] - After Plassey he paid about **Rs 1.77 crore** and gave the **zamindari of the 24 Parganas**; the Company got **duty-free trade in Bengal, Bihar and Orissa** and resources to fight the French.
[164:3:2] - Intrigued with the **Dutch** → Company victory at **Chinsura/Bidara (1759)**.
[164:3:3] - **Removed 1760** – he could not meet the Company's demands.
[164:3:4] - His son **Najm-ud-daula** became Nawab (1765); defence and administration went to Naib Subahdar **Muhammad Reza Khan**.
[164:3:5] - **Order of Nawabs:** Alivardi Khan → Siraj-ud-daula (last independent Nawab) → Mir Jafar → Mir Qasim.
[164:3:6] - **Robert Clive:** Governor of Bengal **1757/58-60 and 1765-67**; banned **double batta** (1766); called "heaven-born general" by **William Pitt**.
[164:3:7] ~~ Sources: Spectrum Modern India p.121-124; NextGen Indian History APPSC Part 6 p.15, 17; old app notes row 164
## SUB 4 | Key Terms – Farman, Dastak, Diwani, Nizamat
[164:4:0] TABLE head: Term | Meaning
  [164:4:0.0] Farman of 1717 | Farrukhsiyar let the Company trade in Bengal duty-free for Rs 3,000 a year – the "Magna Carta" of the Company
  [164:4:0.1] Dastak | Free pass for duty-free trade, misused for private trade
  [164:4:0.2] Diwani | Revenue collection + civil justice
  [164:4:0.3] Nizamat | Police, criminal justice, law and order – formally kept by the Nawab under Dual Government
[164:4:1] - **Dual Government (1765-72):** Company held the **Diwani** (through naib diwans **Reza Khan** for Bengal, **Raja Shitab Rai** for Bihar) – "power without responsibility"; it led to the **Bengal famine of 1770** (Governor **John Cartier**).
[164:4:2] ~~ Sources: NextGen Indian History APPSC Part 6 p.12, 16-17; Spectrum Modern India p.89, 125; old app notes row 164

=== NOTES 1:165 | Dual Government in Bengal and Its Consequences; the Bengal Famine of 1770
## SUB 0 | Sanyasi-Fakir Revolt and Bankim Chandra's Anandamath
[165:0:0] - **Sanyasi (Fakir) Revolt, 1763-1800** (Bengal): causes – **famine of 1770**, Company insensitivity, **restrictions on visits to holy places**; sanyasis raided Company factories and treasuries; subdued by **Warren Hastings**.
[165:0:1] - Leaders: **Majnum Shah**, Chirag Ali, Musa Shah, **Bhawani Pathak**, **Debi Chaudhurani** – not Shah Alam II.
[165:0:2] - **Anandamath (1882)** by **Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay** (not Sarat Chandra) – based on the **Sanyasi revolt**, set in the 1770 famine; contains **Vande Mataram**.
[165:0:3] TABLE head: Work / fact | Detail
  [165:0:3.0] Durgeshnandini (1865) | First Bengali historical novel – Pathan–Mughal conflict in Akbar's reign
  [165:0:3.1] Sitaram (1887) | His last novel (Spectrum calls Rajasimha his finale – its enlarged edition was 1893)
  [165:0:3.2] First graduates of Calcutta University (1858) | Bankim and Jodu Nath Bose
  [165:0:3.3] Birthplace | Naihati (Kanthalpara) – Rail Coach Factory and Museum, Rail Budget 2012-13

## SUB 1 | Dual Government in Bengal (1765-1772)
[165:1:0] - **Introduced by Robert Clive** (second governorship 1765-67) after Buxar and the **First Treaty of Allahabad** (Diwani from **Shah Alam II**, tribute **Rs 26 lakh**).
[165:1:1] - **Period: 1765-1772** – not 1764-72 or 1765-70.
[165:1:2] TABLE head: Diwani (Company) | Nizamat (Nawab)
  [165:1:2.0] Revenue collection and civil justice – real power, no responsibility | Police, criminal justice, law and order – responsibility without power or funds
[165:1:3] - Company got diwani from the emperor and controlled nizamat through its right to nominate the **deputy subahdar**; it collected revenue through **Indian deputies**:
[165:1:4] TABLE head: Naib Diwan | Region
  [165:1:4.0] Mohammad Reza Khan (Deputy Diwan of Murshidabad, appointed by Clive; also deputy nazim) | Bengal
  [165:1:4.1] Raja Shitab Rai | Bihar
[165:1:5] - **Why "dual":** hide the Company's imperial role; avoid British Parliament's scrutiny; other Europeans kept paying duties to the "Nawab" – **not** because the Company had enough British manpower (it lacked it).
[165:1:6] - Criticised as "**power divorced from responsibility**"; conflict of the two sidelined the local nobility and bred resentment.
[165:1:7] - **Abolished 1772 by Warren Hastings** (Court of Directors' decision) – he "tore the mask of Mughal sovereignty"; the Calcutta council acted as Diwan; **treasury Murshidabad → Calcutta**; Reza Khan and Shitab Rai removed.
[165:1:8] - Hastings = **last Governor of Bengal (1772-73)** and **first Governor-General of Bengal (1773)**; **first Governor of Bengal = Clive**.
[165:1:9] - Company debt → government loan (1772) → **Regulating Act, 1773**.
[165:1:10] > Clive introduced (1765), Hastings abolished (1772).
[165:1:11] ~~ Sources: Spectrum Modern India p.125; NextGen Indian History APPSC Part 6 p.16-17; old app notes row 165
## SUB 2 | Consequences and the Bengal Famine of 1770
[165:2:0] - In under two decades **Bengal, the richest province**, was reduced to **acute poverty, famines and epidemics**.
[165:2:1] - **Weavers** forced to sell to Company agents at low prices; Company servants misused **dastaks** for private trade (Mir Qasim had abolished internal duties for all in protest).
[165:2:2] - **R.P. Dutt:** Bengal's surplus revenue drained in the **mercantilist phase, 1757-1813**.
[165:2:3] - **Famine of 1770:** first major famine under Company rule; struck **Bengal and Bihar**; about **ten million** died – about **one-third (≈33%)** of Bengal's people.
[165:2:4] - Causes: monsoon failure; **land revenue demanded in cash** kept rising, peasants sold grain; directly linked to the **Dual Government**.
[165:2:5] - Company gave **little or no relief** and **kept collecting revenue**; Governor at the time: **John Cartier** (1769-72), not Hastings.
[165:2:6] - Andhra's **Guntur (Dokkala) famine, 1832-33** is a separate event.
[165:2:7] ~~ Sources: PMF Modern History Part 3 p.54, 86; NextGen Indian History APPSC Part 6 p.17, 34; old app notes row 165
