## SUB 1 | East-flowing vs West-flowing Rivers – Deltas, Estuaries and Direction
[37:1:0] # Why most Peninsular rivers flow east
[37:1:1] - The **Western Ghats** are the main water divide; the Deccan Plateau is **higher in the west and slopes east** to the Bay of Bengal, so most large rivers flow **east**.
[37:1:2] - The Peninsular block was **tilted north-west → south-east** (Himalayan upheaval), giving the whole drainage a Bay of Bengal orientation.
[37:1:3] - **Narmada and Tapi** are the only major Peninsular rivers flowing **west** – they occupy **trough faults (rift valleys)**.
[37:1:4] # Comparison
[37:1:5] TABLE head: Feature | East-flowing (Bay of Bengal) | West-flowing (Arabian Sea)
  [37:1:5.0] Rivers | Subarnarekha, Brahmani, Baitarani, Mahanadi, Vamsadhara, Nagavali, Godavari, Krishna, Pennar, Palar, Kaveri, Vaigai, Tamraparni | Narmada, Tapi, Mahi, Sabarmati, Vaitarna, Mandovi, Kali, Sharavati, Netravati, Periyar, Bharathapuzha, Pamba
  [37:1:5.1] Size | Long and large, dendritic tributaries | Short and swift (except Narmada, Tapi)
  [37:1:5.2] Mouth | Extensive deltas | No deltas – long, deep estuaries (natural harbours)
  [37:1:5.3] Use | Irrigation | Hydropower
[37:1:6] - **Why no deltas in the west:** steep Western Ghats slope (rapid flow), **short courses**, **hard rocky terrain**; Narmada and Tapi filled their fault troughs, so there is little alluvium – sediment is carried away by waves.
[37:1:7] - **Mahanadi** (delta near **Paradeep**, Odisha), Godavari, Krishna, Kaveri build deltas; **Narmada, Tapi, Mandovi** end in **estuaries** (Narmada estuary ~27 km, south of **Bharuch**).
[37:1:8] - **Subarnarekha** forms an **estuary** between the Ganga and Mahanadi deltas.
[37:1:9] - About **600** short, swift rivers drain the west coast.
[37:1:10] # Classic odd-ones
[37:1:11] - **Kali (Kalinadi)** of Karnataka is **west-flowing** (Diggi, Uttara Kannada → Arabian Sea at Karwar) – not east-flowing.
[37:1:12] - **Periyar** – west-flowing, reaches the Arabian Sea via **Vembanad**; **Pennar and Palar** flow east to the Bay of Bengal.
[37:1:13] - **Krishna** rises ~64 km from the Arabian Sea but flows ~1,400 km east to the **Bay of Bengal**.
[37:1:14] - **Tamraparni** flows east into the Gulf of Mannar – not west-flowing; **Brahmani** is east-flowing.
[37:1:15] - **Betwa** is a Yamuna tributary (flows north-east); **Rapti** is a Ghaghara tributary (NW → SE) – neither is west-flowing.
[37:1:16] - **Tungabhadra, Penganga** flow east (Krishna / Godavari systems).
[37:1:17] - **Kerala:** all major rivers flow west, but the **Kabini** (Wayanad) flows **east** to the Kaveri; **MP:** most rivers flow east but **Narmada and Tapi** flow **west** – so "no east-flowing river in Kerala" and "no west-flowing river in MP" are both **false**.
[37:1:18] > A–R (UPSC 2004): "West-flowing rivers have no deltas" / "they do not carry any alluvial sediments" → key (a), both true and R explains A; PMF calls this debatable because "do not carry any" is too absolute.
[37:1:19] > A–R (CDS 2010): "East-flowing Deccan rivers have deltas" is true, but "because they flow through soft rocks" is false – the Deccan is hard rock.
[37:1:20] > A–R (UPPSC 2014): "Western Ghats rivers do not form deltas" / "short courses, high speed over hard rocks" → both true, R explains A.
[37:1:21] ~~ Sources: NextGen Indian Geography 07 p.13 (book p.97); PMF Indian Physical Geography 02 p.16-20, p.47-48, p.55-56; OnlyIAS NCERT Indian Geography 04 p.11-13; Rocket Indian Geography p.6-7; app notes 4:37 (sub 1)
[37:1:22] - About **77%** of India's drainage area is oriented to the **Bay of Bengal** and about **23%** to the Arabian Sea.

## SUB 8 | Structure and Sequence – Older Drainage, Rift Valleys, Consequent and Superimposed Rivers
[37:8:0] - Peninsular drainage is **older** than the Himalayan – **broad, graded, shallow valleys**, **fixed courses**, no meanders, **non-perennial** flow.
[37:8:1] - **Consequent rivers** follow the general slope – **most Peninsular rivers**, e.g. **Godavari, Krishna, Kaveri** (Western Ghats → Bay of Bengal). **Subsequent:** Chambal, Sind, Ken, Betwa, Son meet the Yamuna/Ganga at right angles.
[37:8:2] - **Superimposed** rivers keep a course developed on a former cover rock – **Chambal**, Damodar, Subarnarekha, Banas.
[37:8:3] # Rift valleys
[37:8:4] TABLE head: River | Rift between / note
  [37:8:4.0] Narmada | Vindhya (north) and Satpura (south) – divides the Deccan Tableland from northern India; west-flowing
  [37:8:4.1] Tapi | Satpura (north) and Ajanta (south)

## SUB 11 | Luni and Inland Drainage
[37:11:0] - **Luni** – rises in the Aravallis south-west of **Ajmer** (near Pushkar; called **Sagarmati**), flows west then south-west after **Telwara**, and is **lost in the marshy Rann of Kutch** – does not reach the sea (not the Gulf of Khambhat or Kutch); **endorheic / land-bound**; largest river of the Thar; ~495–511 km.

=== NOTES 4:38 | Indian Climate – Monsoon Mechanism, Theories and Seasons
## SUB 0 | Winter Season, Retreating and North-East Monsoon, Western Disturbances
[38:0:0] # Retreating monsoon (October–November/December)
[38:0:1] - Caused by the **southward movement of the Sun's vertical rays**; the NW low disappears, the **subtropical westerly jet returns south of the Himalayas**, the TEJ disappears.
[38:0:2] - Withdrawal is gradual (about three months): from the extreme **north-west first** – NextGen/NCERT: from the beginning of **October**; PMF/IMD: from **mid-September** – out of the peninsula by October and from the south-east tip by **December**.
[38:0:3] - **Fine weather and clear skies**; land temperature rises again → hot, humid "**October heat**".
[38:0:4] - **Tropical cyclones** are the hallmark: they form mostly in the **Bay of Bengal** (rarely the Arabian Sea), and their frequency peaks in **October–November**; they bring heavy rain to the east coast → **bimodal** rainfall in the south.
[38:0:5] - The retreating winds blow from land **towards the Bay of Bengal**, pick up moisture there and rain on the Coromandel coast.
[38:0:6] - About **13%** of annual rainfall comes in this season.
[38:0:7] > "Declining monsoon is indicated by clear sky, pressure change over the Bay of Bengal and rising temperature on land" – all three (BPSC).
[38:0:8] # North-east monsoon and Tamil Nadu
[38:0:9] - The winter high over NW India sends winds NW → SE down the plains; over the Bay of Bengal they become the **north-east monsoon** and pick up moisture.
[38:0:10] - **Tamil Nadu / Coromandel coast** gets its **main rain in October–December (Nov–Dec)** from the NE monsoon; South Arcot and Ramanathapuram get **over 50%** of their rain then. Also **Rayalaseema, Nellore, south coastal AP, SE Karnataka, SE Kerala**; **not the Gujarat coast**.
[38:0:11] - **Why Tamil Nadu is dry in the SW monsoon:** it lies in the **rain shadow** of the Western Ghats for the Arabian Sea branch, and the Bay branch blows **parallel to the coast**. So **Chennai** gets the least SW-monsoon rain among Kolkata, Mangalore, Chennai, Delhi.
[38:0:12] - States **not** fed mainly by the June–September monsoon: **Tamil Nadu** (NE monsoon) and **Jammu & Kashmir** (western disturbances).
[38:0:13] # Western disturbances
[38:0:14] - **Temperate (extra-tropical) cyclones from the Mediterranean**, carried east by the westerlies through Iran–Afghanistan–Pakistan into **north-west India**.
[38:0:15] - Give **10–15 cm** of winter rain to the **north-western plains** – **beneficial for rabi crops (wheat)** – and **snowfall** in J&K, Himachal Pradesh, Uttarakhand.
[38:0:16] - Winter rain in the NW plains **decreases from west to east** (and from north to south).
[38:0:17] - **Northern plains' winter rain = western disturbances**, not the NE monsoon (which is active in winter but is not the cause) and not the retreating monsoon.
[38:0:18] - They are **not** part of the monsoon system (CDS: "not associated with monsoon climate" = western disturbances).
[38:0:19] # Winter season (December/January–February)
[38:0:20] - **High pressure over NW India** (NCERT: the **1019 mb** isobar passes through north-west India, **1013 mb** in the far south) → **anticyclonic**, dry, outblowing winds; cold northern plains, cold waves, fog; **no loo or thunderstorms**.
[38:0:21] - **Anticyclonic conditions** form because the land is **cold and the air dense** – not because of winter rain (UPSC 2001: A true, R false).

## SUB 1 | Monsoon – Meaning, Characteristics and Controls of Indian Climate
[38:1:0] # Meaning and characteristics
[38:1:1] - India has a **tropical monsoon** climate; "monsoon" from Arabic **Mausim** = season; **Al Masudi** (Arab geographer) described the seasonal rhythm of winds.
[38:1:2] - Monsoons are **seasonal (secondary/periodic) winds** that **reverse direction** with the season – **not primary (planetary) winds**.
[38:1:3] - They are confined to the tropics, roughly **20°N–20°S** (textbook statement).
[38:1:4] - The distribution of SW-monsoon rain is governed mainly by **relief**.
[38:1:5] - Monsoon rain is **not steady**, **unevenly (not equally) distributed**, has **breaks** and is **uncertain/variable**.
[38:1:6] - India is a "**thirsty land**" despite abundant rain: rain is **concentrated in a few months**, **runs off rapidly** and **evaporates quickly** – all three.
[38:1:7] - Main cause of the monsoon: the **temperature (pressure) contrast between the land and the Indian Ocean**.
[38:1:8] - **Early maritime links** of India with South-East Asia across the Bay of Bengal were helped by the **monsoon winds** (UPSC 2011).
[38:1:9] # Controls (factors) of Indian climate
[38:1:10] - **Latitude** – the **Tropic of Cancer** crosses the middle of India; the south is tropical, the north sub-tropical. India lies about **8°4'–37°6'N**, so it is **not wholly between the tropics** (Jharkhand PCS: A true, R false).
[38:1:11] - **Himalayas** – block cold Central Asian winds, trap the monsoon and give India its **climatic distinctiveness**.
[38:1:12] - **Distance from the sea (continentality)**, **altitude**, **relief**, **pressure and winds**, **upper-air circulation (jet streams)**, **ocean currents**, **cyclones/western disturbances**.
[38:1:13] - CDS 2015: **location, thermal contrast, upper-air circulation and the Himalayas** are all major factors.
[38:1:14] - **Amritsar and Shimla** lie on nearly the same latitude; Shimla is cooler because of **altitude** (~2,200 m vs ~230 m). Likewise **Ooty vs Coimbatore**, **Agra (16 °C) vs Darjiling (4 °C)** in January.
[38:1:15] - At a similar latitude **Mumbai** is moderate while **Nagpur** is extreme – **distance from the sea**. In **Kerala and the Andaman Islands** the day–night range is only about **7–8 °C**.
[38:1:16] > A–R (NDA 2011): "Monsoon winds are driven by the size of the Asian landmass" and "upper-air circulation is associated with the monsoon" – both true, R not the explanation of A.

## SUB 2 | Onset, Advance, Branches and Breaks of the South-West Monsoon
[38:2:0] # Onset and advance
[38:2:1] - Season: **June–September** (IMD); also stated as **mid-June to mid-September**; gives **~75%** of annual rain.
[38:2:2] - Sudden onset = "**burst**" in the first week of June; rain in Kerala called **Varsha**.
[38:2:3] TABLE head: Stage | Normal date
  [38:2:3.0] Andaman & Nicobar Islands (first Indian territory) | ~25 May
  [38:2:3.1] Kerala (first state, Malabar coast) | 1 June (2026: 4 June)
  [38:2:3.2] Bhopal / Delhi | ~15 June / ~25 June
  [38:2:3.3] Whole country | 8 July (IMD revised normal)
[38:2:4] - IMD declares the Kerala onset after **10 May** when **60% of 14 stations** get **2.5 mm** on two consecutive days (5-day **pentads** used).
[38:2:5] - Order of onset: **Kerala → West Bengal → Uttar Pradesh → Rajasthan** (CDS 2010).
[38:2:6] - Duration: **> 120 days** in the SW peninsula, **< 75 days** in west Rajasthan; the monsoon reaches **west Rajasthan last**.
[38:2:7] # Arabian Sea branch
[38:2:8] - Strikes the **Western Ghats** and Malabar first; heaviest rain at **Agumbe, Hulikal** on the crest; the **windward west coast** gets very heavy **orographic** rain because the **Western Ghats** lie across the winds.
[38:2:9] - **Mumbai** (windward) gets far more than **Pune** (leeward); the eastern side of the Ghats is a **rain-shadow** area.
[38:2:10] - Enters central India via the **Narmada–Tapi** valleys; one arm runs **parallel to the Aravallis** → scanty rain in Rajasthan.
[38:2:11] - The **Chhattisgarh Basin** is **not much affected** by the Arabian Sea branch (it gets Bay branch rain).
[38:2:12] - The **Palghat Gap** lets only part of the Arabian Sea branch through to Tamil Nadu – the **majority** of monsoon winds do **not** enter through it.
[38:2:13] # Bay of Bengal branch
[38:2:14] - Strikes Myanmar and the **funnel-shaped Garo–Khasi–Jaintia hills** → orographic rain in **Meghalaya** (Mawsynram, Cherrapunji).
[38:2:15] - Deflected **westward by the Himalayas** along the Ganga plain → rain **decreases east to west**; **Jharkhand** and the east get their rain from the **SW monsoon**.
[38:2:16] > APPSC FRO: "Kerala is the first state to get the SW monsoon in early June" and "the Bay branch causes heavy rain in Meghalaya" – **both correct**.
[38:2:17] # Breaks
[38:2:18] - A **break** occurs when the **monsoon trough (ITCZ) shifts north to the Himalayan foothills**: the plains stay dry, the hills get heavy rain, and temperature rises.
[38:2:19] - One or two breaks a season; most often in the **second week of August**.
[38:2:20] - Rajasthan **does** get some SW monsoon rain (little), so "Rajasthan gets no SW monsoon rain" is false.
[38:2:21] ~~ Sources: NextGen Indian Geography 06 p.12 (book p.82); NextGen Indian Geography 07 p.1-2 (book p.85-86); PMF Indian Physical Geography 03 p.5, p.29-32; app notes 4:38 (sub 4, 5)
[38:2:22] - The **west coast** gets more rain (narrow plain, high Ghats), but the **east coast** produces more **rice** (wide deltas, alluvial soil).

[38:3:0] # Hot weather (pre-monsoon) season – March to May
[38:3:1] - Northward march of the Sun; over **42.5 °C** in the north and NW; **Ganganagar** recorded about **50 °C** (1935).
[38:3:2] - **Heat waves** in north India: **March to June** (peak May–June); IMD plains threshold **40 °C**.
[38:3:3] - **Dust storms (Andhi)** – commonest in **May**, in Rajasthan, Haryana, Punjab, Delhi, western UP.
[38:3:4] - Pre-monsoon rain (~**10%** of annual) is **convectional**: strong heating → rising air **cools adiabatically** → thunderstorms (CDS 2009 A–R: both true, R explains A).

## SUB 4 | Theories of the Monsoon Mechanism, ENSO and Monsoon Research
[38:4:0] TABLE head: Theory / driver | Core idea
  [38:4:0.0] Thermal (differential heating) – Edmund Halley, 1686 | Land heats faster than sea → summer low over land draws moist sea winds; reversed in winter
  [38:4:0.1] Dynamic / ITCZ shift (1950s) | ITCZ moves north over the Ganga plain as the monsoon trough; SE trades cross the equator and turn south-westerly
  [38:4:0.2] Jet streams | STJ shifts north of the Himalayas → onset; returns south → withdrawal. TEJ (South China Sea → East Africa) coincides with the SW monsoon; Somali jet + Somalia Current strengthen it
  [38:4:0.3] Tibetan Plateau (~5,000 m) | Summer heat island → upper-air anticyclone, stronger TEJ; heavy snow cover weakens it
  [38:4:0.4] ENSO / IOD / MJO | El Nino weakens (delayed, weak monsoon, drought); La Nina strengthens; positive IOD can offset El Nino; MJO repeats every 30–60 days
[38:4:1] - **Mascarene High** (near **Madagascar**) drives the cross-equatorial flow; **dust aerosols** over the Indo-Gangetic Plain help pull the monsoon inland.
[38:4:2] - **Gilbert Walker** (1920s) described the **Southern Oscillation**: low pressure over the Indian Ocean (Darwin) + high over the Pacific (Tahiti) = good monsoon.
[38:4:3] - **MONEX** (Monsoon Experiment) **1979** under the **WMO** confirmed the role of the shifting ITCZ (an earlier Indo-Soviet experiment was held in 1973).
[38:4:4] - **El Nino**: a warm current off **Peru** every **3–7 years** replacing the cold Peruvian current; it **causes drought-like conditions in parts of India**.
[38:4:5] # Research and forecasting
[38:4:6] - **IMD** works under the **Ministry of Earth Sciences (MoES)**.
[38:4:7] TABLE head: Mission | Facts
  [38:4:7.0] National Monsoon Mission | 2012, MoES, through IITM Pune + IMD; dynamic coupled model (MMCFS)
  [38:4:7.1] Mission Mausam | 14 Jan 2025 with IMD Vision 2047; Rs 2,000 crore; Bharat Forecast System (6 km, 10-day rain)
[38:4:8] - **LPA** of SW-monsoon rain (1971–2020) = **868.6 mm**. **Ocean Mean Temperature (OMT)** – upper ocean down to the **26 °C isotherm** – predicts better than **SST**.
[38:4:9] - **2026 (as of Sep 2026):** April forecast 92% of LPA, revised to 90%; June deficit **43%** → contingency plans; El Nino building.

## SUB 3 | Tropical Evergreen (Rain) Forests and Biodiversity
[40:3:0] - Tall trees (**> 45 m**, 45–60 m), **multi-layered continuous canopy**, little undergrowth; **lianas, epiphytes**, orchids, canes, bamboo; **leaf litter decomposes fastest** – soil surface almost bare (UPSC 2021).
[40:3:1] - **Maximum plant diversity and biomass**: "greatest variety of animal and plant species" = **tropical moist (rain) forests** – not temperate deciduous, taiga, savanna, grassland, tundra, mangrove or desert.
[40:3:2] - In India, maximum tree diversity in the wet evergreen/semi-evergreen forests of the **Western Ghats**, then the **North-East** (ISFR).
[40:3:3] - Near the **equator** (about 10°N–10°S): no dry season, so the trees do not shed leaves together.
[40:3:4] - **Where in India:** western slopes of the **Western Ghats** (Kerala, Karnataka), **Assam**, Meghalaya, Manipur, Tripura, Nagaland, **Arunachal Pradesh, Mizoram**, **Andaman & Nicobar** – so "rain forest in India" = **Assam and Kerala**; **not Himachal Pradesh**, UP, MP, Chhattisgarh, Odisha.
[40:3:5] - **Species:** **rosewood, ebony**, mahogany, gurjan, ironwood, jamun, aini, champa, mesua, white cedar, canes; dominant species asked = **ebony** (not sandalwood, juniper, acacia).
[40:3:6] - **Cinchona** is an evergreen tropical (hill) plant – grown in WB (Darjeeling), Assam, Kerala/TN hills, **not Chhattisgarh**.
[40:3:7] - **Semi-evergreen:** 200–250 cm; buttressed trunks; laurel, rosewood, mesua, thorny bamboo.
[40:3:8] ~~ Sources: NextGen Indian Geography 08 p.8 (book p.106); PMF Indian Physical Geography 03 p.41-44; OnlyIAS NCERT Indian Geography 06 p.10; app notes 4:40 (sub 1)
[40:3:9] - **Silent Valley** (Palakkad, Kerala) = classic **tropical evergreen (rain) forest**; match: conifer – Himachal Pradesh, mangrove – Sundarbans, thorn/deciduous – Rajasthan.

## SUB 6 | Forestry Practice – Seeds, Logging, Timber and Forest Fires
[40:6:0] # Forest fires
[40:6:1] - **Types:** **ground** fire (burns humus/peat below the litter), **surface** fire (litter and undergrowth – grassland and scrub fires), **crown** fire (runs through the canopy; fastest-spreading and most dangerous in **coniferous** forests, whose resinous needles burn readily).
[40:6:2] - **Counter fire (back-firing)** = deliberately burning a strip of fuel ahead of the advancing fire, from a control line – the **traditional** method of fighting forest fires; fire lines, alert systems and mobile squads are aids.
[40:6:3] # Logging and timber
[40:6:4] - **Salvage logging** = removing trees killed or damaged by fire, wind, insects or disease to recover their timber value.
[40:6:5] - **Traditional logging tools:** axe, hand (cross-cut) saw, wedges, mauls – the **chainsaw** is a modern powered machine.
[40:6:6] - **Wood transport:** **rafting** = logs tied together and floated as one unit; **floating** = loose logs one by one; rope-ways and tramways run overland.
[40:6:7] - **Timber groups (macrostructure):** **hardwood** (broad-leaved angiosperms, e.g. teak, sal) and **softwood** (conifers, e.g. pine, deodar). **Wood anatomy** (cells, grain, density, pores) shows the best way to **process and use** a timber.
[40:6:8] - **Forest inventory** = systematic collection of forest data (area, stock, growth) for assessment and planning.
[40:6:9] # Seeds, planting and site
[40:6:10] - **Recalcitrant seed** (cannot be dried/stored) – **sal (Shorea robusta)**; **orthodox seed** (stores dry) – **babul (Vachellia nilotica)**; **vivipary** (germinates on the tree) – mangrove **Rhizophora mucronata**; **stump planting** – **teak (Tectona grandis)**.
[40:6:11] - **Timber line (tree line)** = upper altitude or high-latitude limit beyond which trees do not grow.
[40:6:12] - **Degraded, heavily eroded zone (NW India):** hardy dry species **Acacia modesta, Capparis aphylla (kair), Salvadora oleoides (pilu)** survive there.
