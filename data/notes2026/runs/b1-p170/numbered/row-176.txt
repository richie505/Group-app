## SUB 0 | Drain of Wealth and the Economic Nationalists
[0:0] # Meaning
[0:1] - **Drain of wealth:** the part of India's wealth carried to **Britain** without **adequate economic or material return** – peculiar to **British** rule (not Portuguese, Dutch or French).
[0:2] - **Began** with the **Diwani of Bengal (1765)** – Bengal's revenue bought the Company's export "investments".
[0:3] - India usually had a **favourable balance of trade**; the drain took the form of **unrequited exports** (export surplus paid for Home Charges, British office and war expenses) – NOT an excess of imports.
[0:4] - In the 18th century the drain was about **2-3% of Britain's national income** (Spectrum) and nearly 9% of India's income (PMF) – it helped finance Britain's industrial revolution.
[0:5] # Components (Home Charges and more)
[0:6] TABLE head: Component | What it covered
  [0:6.0] Salaries, pensions | Of British civil and military officials, remitted home
  [0:6.1] Interest | On the public debt raised in Britain
  [0:6.2] Railway guarantee | 5% assured return to British investors, paid from Indian revenue
  [0:6.3] Military | British Indian Army and wars abroad – the largest budget head
  [0:6.4] Profits | Of British capitalists, merchants, banks, shipping and insurance
[0:7] - **Reason per Naoroji's theory (APPSC FRO):** profits taken by British capitalists from India.
[0:8] - Naoroji: what was drained was a **"potential surplus"** that could have developed India; imperialists claimed linking India to the world capitalist market was itself modernisation (both true, II does not explain I).
[0:9] # Dadabhai Naoroji
[0:10] - **"Grand Old Man of India"**; propounded and popularised the **drain theory**; first stated in **"England's Debt to India" / "England's Duties to India" (1867)**; coined **"moral drain"**.
[0:11] - Book: ***Poverty and Un-British Rule in India* (1901)**; also ***Poverty of India: Papers and Statistics*** (from his 1876 paper "The Poverty of India"); he used the phrase **"Un-British"**.
[0:12] - **First estimate of per capita income: Rs 20 (1867-68)** – not Ranade, Hunter or R.C. Dutt.
[0:13] - Showed (1880) that a **jail convict's food and clothing cost about 50% more** than the average Indian's income (old app key; not checked).
[0:14] - First Indian elected to the **House of Commons (1892, Finsbury Central)**; first to make **Swaraj** the national demand (Calcutta Congress, 1906).

## SUB 1 | Stages of Colonialism; One-way Free Trade and Deindustrialisation
[1:0] # Stages (R.P. Dutt, *India Today*)
[1:1] TABLE head: Stage | Period | Feature
  [1:1.0] Mercantilist (merchant capital) | 1757-1813 | Company monopoly; mostly purchase of Indian goods for export, paid from Bengal revenue
  [1:1.1] Free trade (industrial capital) | 1813-1858 | India a market for British goods and supplier of raw materials
  [1:1.2] Finance capital | 1858 onwards | British capital in railways, plantations, banks
[1:2] - India changed from a **leading exporter of manufactures** to a **supplier of raw materials** – an **agricultural colony of manufacturing Britain**.
[1:3] # One-way Free Trade
[1:4] - **Charter Act of 1813** ended the Company's trade monopoly → **one-way free trade**: British goods entered India **duty-free / nominal duty**; Indian textiles paid **heavy duties in Britain** (already from the 1720 laws) – so "tax-free import of Indian textiles into the UK" is false.
[1:5] - **Imperial preference:** special privileges (lower duties) for **British imports in India**.
[1:6] - **Cotton excise (1894-96)** on Indian mill cloth to offset the import duty on British goods; **Suez Canal (1869)**.
[1:7] - **Railways:** built by private British companies with a **5% guarantee from Indian revenue** (not financed by the British government); they carried British goods inland and raw materials out – **accelerated the decay of handicrafts**.
[1:8] - Karl Marx: British economic policy in India "disgusting"; the British **"inundated the very mother country of cotton with cottons"**.
[1:9] # Deindustrialisation
[1:10] - Weavers coerced (1769 directive on silk; Bolt 1772); Lancashire cloth in Bengal **1786**; by **1850** India took **one-fourth** of British cotton exports.
[1:11] - **Industrial Revolution (first half of the 19th century):** **Indian handicrafts were ruined**; **raw cotton** exports rose (not textile exports).
[1:12] - Process intensified **after 1813**, aggravated by the end of the Company's monopoly (UPPSC key: both statements).
[1:13] - Other causes: loss of princely patrons, export of raw materials, transit duties, British privileges (**B.D. Basu**); shipbuilding crushed by the 1813-14 laws.
[1:14] - **Share of world manufactures:** about **24.5% (1750)** → under **2% (1900)** (Paul Bairoch).
[1:15] - **Results:** urban handicrafts' sudden collapse; **artisans crowded into agriculture**; **Dhaka** (muslin) declined while Calcutta, Madras, Bombay grew.
[1:16] - **No independent industrial development:** **absence of heavy (capital-goods) industries**; laissez-faire did **not** promote indigenous capitalism.

## SUB 2 | Commercialisation of Agriculture; Opium and Peasant Laws
[2:0] - **Commercialisation of Indian agriculture** = a main 19th-century economic result of British rule (UPSC 2018) – not more handicraft exports, Indian factories or rapid urban growth.
[2:1] - Forced by **cash revenue**; cash crops replaced food crops → less food security, **famines**.
[2:2] - Poorer peasants because they were **exploited by merchants and middlemen** (sold at any price after harvest to the grain merchant-moneylender).
[2:3] - **Ganjam / Deccan:** American Civil War cotton boom, slump in 1866 → debt, famine, Deccan riots.
[2:4] TABLE head: Crop | Main region
  [2:4.0] Indigo | Bengal, Bihar – Tinkathiya (3/20 of holding); Indigo Revolt 1859-60
  [2:4.1] Cotton | Deccan, Gujarat, Berar
  [2:4.2] Jute | Bengal
  [2:4.3] Opium | Bihar (and Bengal, Malwa)
  [2:4.4] Tea | Assam
[2:5] - **Triangular trade** India–China–England rested on **opium**: Indian opium → China; Chinese tea and silver → Britain; British goods → India. **Opium Wars: Britain v. China** (1839-42, 1856-60).
[2:6] - By the late 19th century India was a large exporter of **cotton yarn and wheat** (NDA 2014 key).
[2:7] TABLE head: Law | Protected peasants against
  [2:7.0] Deccan Agriculturists' Relief Act, 1879 | Moneylenders, after the Deccan riots (1875)
  [2:7.1] Punjab Land Alienation Act, 1900 | Transfer of land to non-agriculturists (moneylenders)

## SUB 3 | Andhra Crafts and Industries Under British Rule
[3:0] - **Textiles = second-largest employer after agriculture** in British-period Andhra.
[3:1] - **Decline:** machine cloth from England + Company duties and loom tax (moturpha) → exports from Visakhapatnam fell from Rs 7 lakh to Rs 1 lakh (1830s), Machilipatnam Rs 30 lakh → Rs 2,750 (1843-44), Nellore Rs 1.8 lakh → Rs 360 (1842-43); the industry **lost foreign and local markets** (only "declined" is correct); weavers migrated to Burma, Sri Lanka, Mauritius.
[3:2] # Carpets
[3:3] - **Eluru carpets** and **Kurnool rugs** were known in the international market; the Eluru craft grew under **Persian immigrants during Muslim rule**; shown at the **London Exhibition, 1851**; declined by the mid-19th century.
[3:4] TABLE head: Place | Product
  [3:4.0] Srikakulam | Muslin
  [3:4.1] Machilipatnam | Dyed cloth; chintz (16th-17th c.); first Dutch factory
  [3:4.2] Nellore | Towels
  [3:4.3] Eluru | Carpets
  [3:4.4] Kurnool | Rugs, blankets
  [3:4.5] Peddapuram, Dharmavaram, Berhampur | Silk
  [3:4.6] Uppada | Jamdani silk
  [3:4.7] Ponduru (Srikakulam) | Fine hand-spun khadi
[3:5] - **Kamsalis** = goldsmiths (Viswabrahmin artisans) in Andhra records.
[3:6] # Minerals and Industry
[3:7] - **Manganese:** **Visakhapatnam** district (Vizianagaram mines) stood **first in the world, 1892-1929** – not Nellore; **Nellore = mica**.
[3:8] - Jute mills Chittivalasa and Nellimarla; first railway in Andhra **Puttur–Renigunta (1862)**.

## SUB 4 | Modern Industry, Currency and the Economy at Independence
[4:0] TABLE head: Industry | First / key fact
  [4:0.0] Cotton mill | Fort Gloster (Calcutta) 1818 failed; Bombay, 1854 – Cowasji Nanabhai Davar (Spectrum: set up 1853)
  [4:0.1] Jute mill | Rishra (Bengal), 1855
  [4:0.2] Tea company | Assam Company, 1839 – formed in England; HQ Nazira
  [4:0.3] Iron and steel | TISCO, 1907
[4:1] - **Cotton mills Indian-owned; jute mills foreign-owned (Bengal)**; capital goods only about one-fourth of output.
[4:2] - **Hilton Young Commission (1926):** rupee at **1s 6d** (Indian business, Purshottamdas Thakurdas, wanted 1s 4d) – to aid **remittances from India and keep India's creditworthiness**; made Indian goods dearer abroad; recommended a central bank → **RBI 1935**.
[4:3] - Bombay mill workers lived in **chawls** (one-room tenements).
[4:4] # On the Eve of Independence
[4:5] TABLE head: Indicator | Value
  [4:5.0] World income share (Angus Maddison) | 27% (1700) → 3% (1950)
  [4:5.1] Per capita income | about Rs 265
  [4:5.2] Life expectancy | 32 years
  [4:5.3] Literacy | under 16%
  [4:5.4] Agriculture | over 75% of workers, about 50% of GDP
[4:6] - India had a **strong pre-British industrial base**; British policy caused **deindustrialisation**; agriculture **stagnant, low-productivity, monsoon-dependent**; trade = raw materials out, finished goods in; infrastructure served British interests.
[4:7] - Slow industrialisation for want of **capital goods industries**; public sector only railways, power, ports, communications.
[4:8] - Under zamindari the **profit from agriculture went to the zamindars**; fertiliser use was negligible.
[4:9] - Ruined: Indian industries and trade, agriculture stagnated – **not** "ruin of feudalism".
[4:10] - First Plan priority: agriculture, irrigation, power.
[4:11] - **Imperial Gazetteer of India:** compiled by **W.W. Hunter**; **James Mill** (*History of British India*, 1817) divided Indian history into **Hindu, Muslim and British** periods.
