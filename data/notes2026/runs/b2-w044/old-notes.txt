=== NOTES 2:44 | High Courts, Subordinate Judiciary, Judicial Review, Activism and Pil
## SUB 0 | High Court Judges – Appointment, Tenure, Transfer, Removal
[44:0:0] # Appointment and qualifications (Art. 217)
[44:0:1] - Every HC judge is appointed by the **President** by warrant (not the Governor, not the CJI). The **Governor cannot appoint or remove** HC judges – but he does appoint the **CM, SPSC members and the Advocate General**.
[44:0:2] - **Chief Justice**: President after consulting the **CJI and the Governor**. **Other judges**: also the **Chief Justice of that HC** → consultees = **HC CJ, CJI, Governor** (not the Attorney General). Common HC: **Governors of all the States** concerned.
[44:0:3] - **Collegium**: HC CJ + **2** senior-most HC judges propose → **CJI + 2** senior-most SC judges (Third Judges case, 1998); NJAC (99th Amendment) struck down in **2015**.
[44:0:4] - **Qualifications**: citizen; **10 years judicial office** or **10 years HC advocate**; no minimum age; **no "distinguished jurist" route** (only SC, Art. 124(3)(c)).
[44:0:5] - **Strength** of each HC: fixed by the **President** (Art. 216) – not Parliament, Cabinet, PM or Governor; strength differs from HC to HC. (SC strength: Parliament by law.)
[44:0:6] - Sanctioned strength (18 Sep 2025): **1,122** for all HCs, **804** filled. **8 Aug 2026**: 30 HC judges appointed (Madras 15, Karnataka 6).
[44:0:7] - **Justice H.R. Khanna** (8th Law Commission, **80th Report**, 1979): at least **one-third** of HC judges should be from another State.
[44:0:8] # Oath, tenure, resignation
[44:0:9] - **Oath** before the **Governor** or a person appointed by him (Art. 219) – not the President or CJI.
[44:0:10] - Holds office till **62** – raised from **60** by the **15th Amendment, 1963**; the **114th Amendment Bill (2010)** sought **65**. Not "during pleasure", not "as long as they desire". (SC judges: **65**.)
[44:0:11] - **Resignation** in writing to the **President** (Art. 217(1)(a)) – not the CJI, HC CJ or Governor.
[44:0:12] # Transfer (Art. 222)
[44:0:13] - HC judges **can be transferred**; the **President** transfers after consulting the **CJI** (Art. 222(1)) – the CJI/collegium only advises; the PM or Law Minister has no power. Only **HC judges**: **no provision to transfer SC judges**.
[44:0:14] # Removal
[44:0:15] - Same manner and grounds as an SC judge (Arts. **217(1)(b), 218**, 124(4)): **President's order** after an address by **both Houses** with special majority. Grounds only **proved misbehaviour or incapacity** – **difference of opinion with the Chief Justice** is never a ground; neither the Governor nor the State Legislature can remove.
[44:0:16] - Procedure: **Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968** – motion by 100 LS / 50 RS members; 3-member committee (SC judge, HC CJ, jurist). No HC judge impeached so far.

## SUB 1 | Judicial Activism and PIL
[44:1:0] # Terms
[44:1:1] TABLE head: Term | Meaning
  [44:1:1.0] Judicial activism (= judicial dynamism) | Proactive role of courts protecting rights and filling gaps; opposite of restraint
  [44:1:1.1] PIL | "A legal action initiated in a court of law for the enforcement of public interest or general interest in which the public or a class of the community have pecuniary or some other interest" (SC definition)
  [44:1:1.2] Social Action Litigation | Other name of PIL (also SIL, Class Action Litigation)
  [44:1:1.3] Locus standi relaxed | Any member of the public with "sufficient interest" can move court for others' rights
  [44:1:1.4] Epistolary jurisdiction | Letters, postcards, newspaper reports treated as petitions
[44:1:2] - **Why activism arises**: mainly the **failure of the legislature and executive** to discharge their duties – not judges' wish for supremacy or populism.
[44:1:3] - **PIL = outcome of judicial activism** (its most popular form) – not of judicial restraint; "intertwined with" activism. PIL evolved by **judicial initiative / activism** – **not** by a constitutional amendment, Act of Parliament or Presidential order.
[44:1:4] - PIL under **Art. 32 (SC)** or **Art. 226 (HC)**; in line with **Art. 39A**.
[44:1:5] - **Example of activism**: SC decision on the **rights of the third gender** (**NALSA, 2014**) – not routine river-water, tax-appeal or land-ownership decisions.
[44:1:6] # Origin and pioneers
[44:1:7] - **Judicial activism**: originated in the **USA**; term coined **1947** by **Arthur Schlesinger Jr.**; in India from the **mid-1970s** (post-Emergency). PIL: originated in the **USA in the 1960s**; in India early **1980s** (first cases 1979).
[44:1:8] - **Pioneers of PIL**: **Justice V.R. Krishna Iyer and Justice P.N. Bhagwati** – the first SC judges to admit PILs; with O. Chinnappa Reddy and D.A. Desai they laid the foundation of activism. Judge "who introduced PIL" = **P.N. Bhagwati**.
[44:1:9] - **V.R. Krishna Iyer** was **never Chief Justice of India** (UPSC 2008: only "progenitor of PIL" is correct). Bhagwati was CJI 1985-86.
[44:1:10] - **First PIL**: **Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar (1979)** – **undertrial prisoners spending long years in Bihar jails** (speedy trial); filed by **Kapila Hingorani ("Mother of PILs")**.
[44:1:11] # Doctrines in a match
[44:1:12] TABLE head: Doctrine | Application
  [44:1:12.0] PIL | Any member of the public with sufficient interest can approach the court for others' rights
  [44:1:12.1] Writ jurisdiction | Writs for enforcing FRs and correcting injustices
  [44:1:12.2] Proportionality | Government action balanced; no branch abuses power
  [44:1:12.3] Separation of powers | Keeps the judiciary independent of legislature and executive
[44:1:13] # Instruments and SC evolutions
[44:1:14] - **Suo motu cognisance**; **continuing mandamus** (case kept open to monitor); **guidelines** filling a vacuum (**Vishaka 1997** → POSH Act 2013); **Art. 142**; expert committees.
[44:1:15] - Genuine SC evolutions: **Basic Structure**, **PIL**, **removal of handcuffs**, continuing mandamus, expansion of Art. 21. **Not** an SC evolution: "**exemptions to FRs**" (Arts **31A–31C, 33**) – constitutional text.

  [44:1:16.0] Hussainara Khatoon | 1979 | First PIL, speedy trial
  [44:1:16.1] Olga Tellis | 1985 | Livelihood of pavement dwellers (Art. 21)
  [44:1:16.2] M.C. Mehta | 1980s on | Environment – activism, not restraint
  [44:1:16.3] Vishaka | 1997 | Workplace harassment guidelines
  [44:1:16.4] Prakash Singh | 2006 | Police reforms
  [44:1:16.5] Puttaswamy | 2017 | Privacy a fundamental right
  [44:1:16.6] Swaraj Abhiyan | 2016 | Drought management guidelines
  [44:1:16.7] State of Rajasthan v. UoI | 1977 | Declined political question – restraint
  [44:1:16.8] Aravali Golf Club v. Chander Hass | 2007 | Judges not to encroach on legislature/executive – restraint
[44:1:17] - **DPSPs are non-justiciable (Art. 37)**: for their non-enforcement a citizen cannot approach any court.

[44:1:19] - **Seeds of PIL:** sown by **V.R. Krishna Iyer** in **Mumbai Kamgar Sabha v. Abdul Bhai (1976)** – the case usually given as the first appearance of the PIL concept; it took shape with Hussainara Khatoon (1979) and S.P. Gupta (1981).

## SUB 2 | High Courts in India – Number, History, Jurisdiction
[44:2:0] # Constitutional basis
[44:2:1] - HCs: **Part VI, Chapter V, Articles 214–231**. **Art. 214**: a HC for each State. **Art. 231** (7th Amendment, 1956): **Parliament by law** may set up a **common HC** for two or more States (or States + a UT) – not the President's order, an SC order or an amendment. **Art. 230**: Parliament extends/excludes HC jurisdiction over a UT (7th Amendment, 1956). **Art. 241**: Parliament may constitute a HC for a UT.
[44:2:2] - A **new HC** is created by a **law of Parliament** and brought into being by a **Presidential notification** (e.g. AP HC from 1 Jan 2019 under the AP Reorganisation Act).
[44:2:3] # History
[44:2:4] - **Indian High Courts Act, 1861** → first three in **1862**: **Calcutta (2 July 1862, oldest), Bombay, Madras**. Fourth: **Agra 1866**, shifted to **Allahabad 1869** – **150th anniversary in 2016**.
[44:2:5] - First HCs **after independence (1948)**: **Orissa** (Cuttack) and **Assam/Gauhati** (Assam HC, renamed Gauhati 1971); Rajasthan 1949. Punjab HC 1947; Delhi 1966; HP 1971; Sikkim 1975.

## SUB 3 | Judicial Review – Meaning, Origin, Basis
[44:3:0] - **Meaning**: power of the **SC and HCs** to pronounce upon the **constitutionality of laws and executive orders** (Centre and States) and declare them **void** – not questioning the **wisdom** of laws, not pre-assent review, not reviewing its own judgments (that is Art. 137 review), not hearing appeals from lower courts. Hence the SC "can declare illegal any law of the State".
[44:3:1] - Examines the **constitutional validity of laws** – not a "periodic review of the Constitution" or "review of the functioning of the judiciary".
[44:3:2] - Enjoyed by the **Supreme Court as well as the High Courts** (Arts **32** and **226**; NextGen: Arts **13 and 226** confer it on HCs) – not all courts.
[44:3:3] - The term is **not used in the Constitution**; part of the **basic structure** (**Kesavananda 1973**; **L. Chandra Kumar 1997**).
[44:3:4] - **Origin USA**: **Marbury v. Madison (1803)**, CJ **John Marshall**; India borrowed it from the **USA**. Prevalent in **both India and the USA**; **UK** – parliamentary sovereignty, courts cannot void Acts.
[44:3:5] - **Indian vs British parliamentary system**: the difference is **judicial review** (India) – both have real and nominal executives, collective responsibility and bicameral legislatures.
[44:3:6] - **Checks legislative excesses** of Parliament and State Assemblies (not President/Governor, no-confidence or elections); **restricts the sovereignty of Parliament** – laws cannot break the Basic Structure.
[44:3:7] - India = **balance of parliamentary sovereignty (British) and judicial supremacy (American)** – neither pure parliamentary sovereignty, judicial supremacy nor cabinet supremacy.
[44:3:8] - **Custodian / guardian of Fundamental Rights**: the **Supreme Court** (Art. 32) – with the **High Courts** (Art. 226); not the President, PM, Parliament or Rajya Sabha.
[44:3:9] - Basis: written Constitution + **federalism** + FRs. **Symbol of federal government** (APPSC key) – courts as umpire of the Centre-State division. **Theory**: **supremacy of the Constitution** / limited government.
[44:3:10] - **Grounds**: violation of FRs; lack of **legislative competence**; repugnance to the Constitution / Basic Structure – not disagreement with majority policy.
[44:3:11] - FRs held amendable first in **Shankari Prasad (1951)**.

## SUB 5 | Court of Record and Contempt of Court
[44:5:0] - **Arts 129 (SC) and 215 (HC)**: the **SC and the HCs** are Courts of Record (not district courts). Two powers: records of **perpetual memory and testimony**, of **evidentiary value**, not questioned in lower courts, recognised as **legal precedents**; and power to **punish for contempt of itself**.
[44:5:1] - So only the **SC or a HC** can punish for its own contempt (not any court, tribunal or quasi-judicial body); under the 1971 Act a HC also punishes contempt of **subordinate courts**.
[44:5:2] - "Contempt of court" is **not defined in the Constitution** – defined by the **Contempt of Courts Act, 1971**. **Parliament** makes laws on contempt (Union List entry 77; Concurrent List entry 14).
[44:5:3] - **Contempt of Court Act, 1926** – first major law; **H.N. Sanyal Committee (1961)** → **Contempt of Courts Act, 1971** (UPSC 2022: statements 1, 2 and 4 correct; 3 wrong).
[44:5:4] TABLE head: Type | Meaning
  [44:5:4.0] Civil contempt | Wilful disobedience of a judgment/order or wilful breach of an undertaking given to a court
  [44:5:4.1] Criminal contempt | Scandalising or lowering the court's authority; prejudicing or interfering with judicial proceedings; obstructing the administration of justice
[44:5:5] - **Not contempt**: a **complaint against a court to a higher court**, fair criticism, fair reporting.
[44:5:6] - Purpose: to secure **public respect and confidence** in the judicial process – A-R: both true and R explains A.
[44:5:7] - **Justice C.S. Karnan** (Calcutta HC): first sitting HC judge to **appear personally before the SC** in a contempt case (March 2017); sentenced to **six months** for **contempt of court** (May 2017).
[44:5:8] - Jul 2026: SC (CJI Surya Kant bench) shifted "bulldozer justice" contempt pleas to HCs. Sep 2026: SC refused to stay Bombay HC contempt proceedings over a judicial officer's outburst.
[44:5:9] ~~ Sources: Laxmikanth 8th ed. ch.34 (src/polity/laxmikanth-indian-polity-02.txt p.64); NextGen Polity - APPSC Exams p.321 (nextgen-polity-appsc-19.txt p.6, Court of Record); Contempt of Courts Act 1971 s.2; app notes 2:44 (sub 3)
[44:5:10] - **Punishment:** simple imprisonment up to **six months** and/or fine up to Rs 2,000 (Sec. 12); for **civil contempt** the court may order **civil detention** (civil prison) instead of a fine (Sec. 12(3)).

## SUB 9 | High Court Powers – Jurisdiction and Writs
[44:9:0] - Jurisdictions of a HC: **original, writ, appellate, supervisory**, control over subordinate courts, court of record, judicial review – **no advisory jurisdiction** (that is the SC's, Art. 143).
[44:9:1] - **Art. 226**: writs for **FRs "and for any other purpose"** (ordinary legal rights) – **wider than Art. 32**; to **any person, authority or government** within its territory, and **even outside** if the **cause of action** arises within. So "a HC cannot issue orders to any government except its own" is **wrong**. Art. 226 is discretionary; Art. 32 itself is an FR (R does not explain A).
[44:9:2] - **Art. 227**: **administrative and judicial superintendence** over all courts and tribunals in its territory; may act **suo motu**; **227(4)** – not over **Armed Forces** courts/tribunals. The SC has no Art. 227-type power.
[44:9:3] - **Appellate**: a HC is primarily a court of appeal; second civil appeals only on a **substantial question of law**; Sessions Court death sentences need HC confirmation (**BNSS 2023**).
[44:9:4] - Four HCs (Calcutta, Bombay, Madras, Delhi) have **original civil jurisdiction** in high-value cases.

## SUB 6 | State Administrative Tribunals – AP Angle
[45:6:0] - **SATs** are set up by the **Central Government at the request of the State**; a **Joint Administrative Tribunal** may serve **two or more States**; SAT members are chosen by a committee chaired by the State HC Chief Justice.
[45:6:1] - **32nd Amendment, 1973** – **Article 371D** gave constitutional authority for an **AP Administrative Tribunal (APAT)** for public-service grievances.
[45:6:2] - Presidential order of **19 May 1975** under **Art. 371D(3)** constituted APAT; it worked from **6 July 1976**; reconstituted under the 1985 Act from **1 Nov 1989**.
[45:6:3] - **2016:** Telangana removed from its jurisdiction; **abolished in 2020** (Centre's notification of **14 Jan 2020**) – its cases went to the **AP High Court**, not the CAT.

## SUB 3 | India – a "Union of States" (Article 1)
[46:3:0] - **Art. 1(1):** "**India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States**" – the Constitution's names for the country are **India and Bharat** only (not Hindustan, Bharatvarsh).
[46:3:1] - The word "**Federation**" is **nowhere** used in the Constitution; India is described neither as a federation nor as a confederation.
[46:3:2] - "**Union of States**" is taken from the **British North America Act, 1867** (Canada).
[46:3:3] - **Ambedkar's two reasons** for "Union": (i) the federation is **not the result of an agreement** among the States (unlike the USA); (ii) the States have **no right to secede** – the Union is **indestructible**.
[46:3:4] - The Union of India is an **indissoluble Union of States**; it is not formed at the will of States and cannot be dissolved by Parliament, State Assemblies or plebiscite.
[46:3:5] - **Indestructible Union of destructible States:** Parliament can alter the area, boundaries or name of any State **without its consent** by a **simple majority** (Art. 3); USA = indestructible Union of indestructible States.
[46:3:6] - **Secession:** the Constitution gives **no right of self-determination/secession** to any region; advocacy of secession is **not protected** by free speech – the **16th Amendment (1963)** allows restrictions in the interest of **sovereignty and integrity of India**.
[46:3:7] - Territory of India = States + UTs + acquired territories; "**Union of India**" = the **States** only; both listed in the **First Schedule**.
[46:3:8] - **Art. 2** = admission/establishment of new States not part of India; laws under Arts 2-3 that change the First and Fourth Schedules are **not** Art. 368 amendments (Art. 4); an Art. 3 Bill needs the **President's recommendation**.

## SUB 5 | Switching to Unitary Mode – Emergency and Overriding Powers
[46:5:0] - During an **emergency** the Centre becomes all-powerful and the federal structure **converts into a unitary one without a formal amendment** – unique among federations; so the Indian polity is described as **unitary** under the Emergency provisions.
[46:5:1] - Only during a **National Emergency (Art. 352)** – declared in **any part** of the country – does the Union assume **full legislative and executive control**; President's Rule covers one State only, and ordinances or martial law (Art. 34) do not.
[46:5:2] - Emergency provisions are in **Part XVIII (Arts 352-360)**; **three** kinds: National (352), State/President's Rule (356), Financial (360).
[46:5:3] # Overriding powers
[46:5:4] TABLE head: Article | Power
  [46:5:4.0] 249 | Rajya Sabha resolution (2/3 present and voting) in national interest → Parliament legislates on a State subject (1 year, renewable)
  [46:5:4.1] 250 | During National Emergency Parliament legislates on the State List; laws lapse 6 months after it ends
  [46:5:4.2] 252 | Two or more States request Parliament to legislate
  [46:5:4.3] 253 | Laws to implement international agreements
  [46:5:4.4] 354 | President may modify revenue distribution during a National Emergency – the order lasts till the end of the financial year in which the Emergency ends (not six months)
[46:5:5] # Emergencies compared
[46:5:6] TABLE head: Basis | 352 National | 356 President's Rule | 360 Financial
  [46:5:6.0] Ground | War, external aggression, armed rebellion (44th replaced "internal disturbance") | Failure of constitutional machinery | Threat to financial stability
  [46:5:6.1] Approval | 1 month, special majority | 2 months, simple | 2 months, simple
  [46:5:6.2] Duration | 6 months at a time, no maximum | 6 months at a time, max 3 years (beyond 1 year: National Emergency + EC certificate) | Until revoked

[46:5:9] - **Only a National Emergency** affects Fundamental Rights; President's Rule and Financial Emergency do not.

## SUB 6 | Federalism – Meaning, Types and Federal Countries
[46:6:0] - **Federalism:** two sets of governments **co-exist** with a **constitutional distribution of powers**, under a **written Constitution**, with an independent judiciary as umpire.
[46:6:1] - **Unitary government:** one supreme central government; local units are its creations with delegated powers; **no constitutional division of powers**.
[46:6:2] - **Confederation:** a loose league of **sovereign States** that keep the **right to secede**.
[46:6:3] - A **federal polity** involves Union-State relations, inter-State relations, mechanisms for **cooperation** and mechanisms for **resolving disputes**.
[46:6:4] # Unitary government – merits and demerits
[46:6:5] TABLE head: Merits | Demerits
  [46:6:5.0] Quick (swift) decisions, uniformity of laws, flexibility | Over-centralisation; local needs neglected
  [46:6:5.1] Strong state – firm crisis response | Not suited to large, diverse countries
[46:6:6] - "**Ideal for large countries**" is NOT a characteristic of unitary government; adaptability, wide participation and less authoritarianism are federal/democratic merits.
[46:6:7] - **Power sharing** is justified because it reduces conflict, prevents majority imposition and consults all affected – it does **not** speed up decision-making.
[46:6:8] # Coming-together vs holding-together
[46:6:9] TABLE head: Type | Meaning | Examples
  [46:6:9.0] Coming-together | Independent States pool sovereignty | USA, Switzerland, Australia
  [46:6:9.1] Holding-together | A large country divides power; stronger Centre | India, Spain, Belgium

  [46:6:11.0] USA, Canada, Australia, Argentina, Switzerland, Germany, Russia, Brazil, Belgium (since 1993) | Britain (UK), France, Italy, Japan, China, Cuba, Sri Lanka

## SUB 8 | 42nd Amendment Transfers and Residuary Powers
[46:8:0] # 42nd Amendment, 1976 – State List → Concurrent List
[46:8:1] - **Five** subjects moved: **education**, **forests**, **weights and measures**, **protection of wild animals and birds**, **administration of justice** (constitution and organisation of all courts except the SC and HCs).
[46:8:2] - Inserted **Entry 20A – population control and family planning**.
[46:8:3] - **Education** was originally a **State** subject; it went to the **Concurrent List (Entry 25)** by the **42nd Amendment** – not the 24th, 25th or 44th.
[46:8:4] - **Public order, police, agriculture** were **not** transferred.

[46:8:6] # Residuary powers – Article 248
[46:8:7] - **Residuary powers** = matters **not enumerated in any of the three Lists**; Parliament has **exclusive** power over them (**Art. 248** and **Union Entry 97**), including **residuary taxes** (gift tax, wealth tax, expenditure tax).
[46:8:8] - Art. 248 is in **Part XI** (Chapter I, Legislative Relations); the **101st Amendment, 2016** made it "subject to **Article 246A**" (GST).
