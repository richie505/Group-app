## SUB 0 | Ecosystem – Concept, Biotic and Abiotic Components, Producers and Decomposers
[0:0] # Concept
[0:1] - **Ecosystem:** a community of organisms **together with the non-living environment** in which they live, interacting as one functional unit (pond, ocean, forest, even an **aquarium**). Term coined by the British botanist **Sir Arthur G. Tansley in 1935**.
[0:2] - **Two components:** **biotic** (producers, consumers, decomposers) and **abiotic** (energy, water, air, soil, nutrients, climate). "Evolution" is a process, not a component.
[0:3] - An ecosystem is a **natural, open system**: energy and matter enter and leave; it has its own **productivity**; it is **not a closed system with fixed boundaries** (UPSC/APPSC "NOT correct" item).
[0:4] - The **biosphere** (narrow zone where **lithosphere, hydrosphere and atmosphere** meet) is treated in most books as an **open system** – energy enters as sunlight and leaves as heat (UPPSC RO/ARO 2017 key: A true, R false). Strictly, the Earth is open for energy but nearly **closed for matter**.
[0:5] - All components are **interdependent**: a change in one population affects the rest – so "components are not interdependent" is false, while "human activities affect the environment" is true.
[0:6] - **Functions:** energy flow (one-way), nutrient cycling, production, **self-regulation (homeostasis)**; the **food chain** is the **dynamic part** of an ecosystem (APPSC G1 2008), while producers, consumers and decomposers form its structure.
[0:7] - **Biogeochemical (nutrient) cycle:** movement of **chemical elements** (C, N, P) through **biotic and abiotic** components – energy does not cycle; **photosynthesis removes CO₂**; the **phosphorus cycle begins with rock weathering**.
[0:8] # Abiotic components
[0:9] TABLE head: Type | Factors
  [0:9.0] Energy | Sunlight – primary source and driving force
  [0:9.1] Climatic | Light, temperature, rainfall, humidity, wind
  [0:9.2] Edaphic | Soil – texture, pH, minerals, drainage
  [0:9.3] Topographic | Altitude, slope, aspect
  [0:9.4] Materials | CO₂, water, nitrates, phosphates; humus and other organic compounds
[0:10] - **Abiotic factor:** any **non-living** component that affects organisms or their environment – air, water, light are abiotic; plants, bacteria and animals are biotic.
[0:11] - **Liebig's Law of the Minimum:** growth limited by the nutrient in shortest supply. **Shelford's Law of Tolerance:** both too little and too much limit.
[0:12] - **Stenothermal** organisms tolerate only a **narrow** temperature range; **eurythermal** a wide range (likewise stenohaline / euryhaline for salinity).
[0:13] - **Cold-blooded (poikilothermic)** animals – **frogs**, snakes, lizards, fish, crocodiles – take the temperature of their surroundings.
[0:14] # Producers (autotrophs) – trophic level I
[0:15] - **Autotrophs** make their own food; their **carbon and energy needs are met by photosynthesis** (NCERT X): they take in **CO₂ and water** (not oxygen) and, with **sunlight and chlorophyll**, convert them into **carbohydrates**, which give the plant energy.
[0:16] - **Photosynthesis** turns **light energy into chemical energy**; it needs light – normally daytime sunlight, but it also runs under moonlight or artificial light, so it is not tied to clock time (State PSC key "day or night").
[0:17] - On land the major producers are **herbaceous and woody plants**; in water, **phytoplankton** / microscopic **algae** and seaweeds.
[0:18] - **Chemoautotrophs:** bacteria of deep-sea vents / active volcanoes oxidise **hydrogen sulphide**; many **archaea** (methanogens) use energy from chemical compounds – they too are producers.
[0:19] - Some **bacteria** (green sulphur bacteria) photosynthesise with **invisible infrared** light.
[0:20] - **Producers act as intermediaries between the biotic and abiotic components** (official UPPSC key; many books also call **decomposers** the link, because they return nutrients to the abiotic pool).
[0:21] - **Chlorella** (a green alga) has the highest efficiency of converting solar energy into chemical energy among the usual options.
[0:22] - **Oxygen:** most of Earth's oxygen comes from **algae** / marine phytoplankton – ocean phytoplankton and photosynthetic bacteria give **about 50%** of the world's oxygen, more than rainforests; oxygen-rich surface water holds far **less** oxygen than air (UPSC 2025: only statement II correct).
[0:23] - **Cactus** photosynthesises in its green **stem** (leaves reduced to spines).
[0:24] # Consumers and decomposers
[0:25] TABLE head: Component | Examples
  [0:25.0] Primary consumers (herbivores) | Cow, deer, goat, rabbit, caterpillar, zooplankton
  [0:25.1] Secondary consumers | Wolves, frog, fox
  [0:25.2] Tertiary / top carnivores | Lion, tiger, hawk, eagle
  [0:25.3] Omnivores | Man (primary and secondary consumer), bear
  [0:25.4] Decomposers (saprotrophs) | Bacteria and fungi – not viruses
  [0:25.5] Detritivores | Earthworms, millipedes, woodlice, nematodes – not jellyfish or seahorses
[0:26] - **Fungi are heterotrophs** (mostly saprotrophs that absorb food from dead matter; some parasitic or symbiotic).
[0:27] - **Parasitic nutrition:** obtaining food from another living organism **without killing it**.
[0:28] - **Decomposition:** breakdown of dead organic matter by **microorganisms (bacteria, fungi)** into simple inorganic nutrients; biodegradable waste is converted into useful compost by **bacteria**.
[0:29] - Steps: **fragmentation → leaching → catabolism → humification → mineralisation**; fully decomposed litter = **humus**. Fast when detritus is rich in nitrogen and sugars and climate warm and moist; slow with lignin, chitin, cold or anaerobic conditions.
[0:30] - If **decomposers are removed**, **mineral (nutrient) movement is blocked** – energy flow would continue for a while, but nutrients are not recycled.
[0:31] - Macroconsumers = phagotrophs; microconsumers = saprotrophs (osmotrophs). Free-living **Azotobacter** fixes nitrogen.
[0:32] - **Protists** are unicellular **eukaryotes**, not prokaryotes; cyanobacteria are photosynthetic prokaryotes.
[0:33] # Types of ecosystem
[0:34] TABLE head: Type | Examples
  [0:34.0] Natural terrestrial | Forest, grassland, desert, tundra, mountain
  [0:34.1] Natural aquatic | Pond, lake, river, wetland, estuary, ocean
  [0:34.2] Artificial (man-modified) | Crop field, aquarium, park, village; city = extreme example
[0:35] - A **lake / pond** is the classic **self-sufficient (complete, self-regulating) natural ecosystem**; an aquarium, farmland or polyhouse depends on human inputs.

## SUB 1 | Food Chains, Trophic Levels, Food Webs and Biomagnification
[1:0] # Food chain
[1:1] - **Food chain:** the order in which organisms **feed upon each other** – producer → herbivore → carnivore; energy passes along it. It runs **across species**, not within one species' population, and it does **not** show the **numbers** eaten (that is a pyramid / food web) – UPSC 2013: only statement 1 correct.
[1:2] TABLE head: Chain | Starts with | Example
  [1:2.0] Grazing | Living green plants (autotrophs pass energy to herbivores) | Grass → grasshopper → frog → snake (→ eagle); grass → goat → human
  [1:2.1] Detritus (DFC) | Dead organic matter | Leaf litter → earthworm → chicken → hawk
  [1:2.2] Pond / aquatic | Phytoplankton | → zooplankton → small fish → large fish
  [1:2.3] Marine | Diatoms | → crustaceans → herrings (UPSC 2014)
  [1:2.4] Grassland | Grass | → grasshopper or rabbit → snake or hawk
[1:3] - "Grazing food chain begins with **dead leaves**" is **false** (that is the detritus chain); "autotrophs capture energy and transfer it to herbivores" is true (APPSC G2 Mains 2025: A false, R true).
[1:4] - In water the grazing chain is the main energy conduit; on land more energy flows through the detritus chain.
[1:5] # Trophic levels
[1:6] TABLE head: Level | Organisms
  [1:6.0] I | Producers – green plants, phytoplankton, seaweeds, chemosynthetic bacteria (vents, active volcanoes), algae
  [1:6.1] II | Primary consumers / herbivores – deer, ants, caterpillar, goat, zooplankton
  [1:6.2] III | Secondary consumers – frog, fox; a man eating goat flesh
  [1:6.3] IV | Tertiary / top carnivores – snake (in grass → grasshopper → frog → snake), hawk, lion
  [1:6.4] None single | Decomposers and detritivores recycle all levels
[1:7] - The **highest trophic level** is held by **carnivores** (top carnivores), not herbivores or decomposers.
[1:8] - **Man** is an omnivore – **primary as well as secondary consumer**; eating goat flesh makes him a **secondary consumer**.
[1:9] - **Producers outnumber primary consumers** (energy and numbers fall at each level).
[1:10] - A species can occupy more than one level; chains rarely exceed **4–5 levels**.
[1:11] - Missing-level question: "tiger, grass, snake, frog" has **no herbivore – second level missing**.
[1:12] # Food web and stability
[1:13] - **Food web:** network of interlinked food chains; **more complex → more stable** (alternative feeding paths).
[1:14] TABLE head: Term | Meaning
  [1:14.0] Resistance | Withstand a disturbance
  [1:14.1] Resilience | Recover after a disturbance
  [1:14.2] Homeostasis | Self-regulation up to a limit through feedback
[1:15] - Diverse systems (rainforest, coral reef) are more stable than tundra, desert or monoculture; overall the **ocean (marine ecosystem) is the most stable ecosystem** – it stays unchanged over long periods.
[1:16] - Warming and ocean acidification weaken calcifying plankton and corals, disrupting marine food webs.
[1:17] # Bioaccumulation vs biomagnification
[1:18] TABLE head: Bioaccumulation | Biomagnification (biological magnification)
  [1:18.0] Build-up of a pollutant inside one organism over its life | Rising concentration of toxicants at each higher trophic level – maximum in the topmost level
[1:19] - Only **persistent, fat-soluble, non-biodegradable** pollutants (DDT, mercury, PCBs) biomagnify – an increase in **toxicants**, not in algae, insects or weeds.
[1:20] - DDT (a **chlorinated hydrocarbon**): food crop → rat → snake → **hawk** – highest in the hawk; grasshopper → toad → **snake** – highest in the snake; causes **eggshell thinning** and seabird crashes.
[1:21] - **Methylmercury** – Minamata disease (Japan).
[1:22] - Pollutant types: biodegradable = sewage; non-biodegradable = DDT, plastic, asbestos; primary = CO, SO₂, NOx; secondary = O₃, **PAN**, smog.
[1:23] - **Trophic level:** the functional position (feeding step) an organism occupies in a food chain.

## SUB 2 | Environment, Ecology and Levels of Organisation
[2:0] # Environment
[2:1] - **Environment:** the sum total of living and non-living components, influences and events surrounding an organism; **biotic and abiotic factors interact**; it is never static. Composite: **physical** (land, water, air, nutrients), **biological** (organisms) and **cultural** elements – "all of the above" definitions hold.
[2:2] - Term "environment" first used in English by **Thomas Carlyle (1828)**.
[2:3] TABLE head: Category | Includes
  [2:3.0] Physical | Lithosphere, hydrosphere, atmosphere; nutrients, water, air
  [2:3.1] Biotic | Plants, animals, microbes
  [2:3.2] Cultural | Man-made – social, economic, political institutions
[2:4] - Renewable resources come from **both** biotic and abiotic parts (sunlight, wind, water) – "only biotic elements give renewable resources" is false.
[2:5] - **Primary functions:** provide resources, **assimilate wastes**, habitat for biodiversity – "generating synthetic chemicals" is NOT a function.
[2:6] - Every **habitat is an environment**, but an environment may exist without life.
[2:7] # Ecology
[2:8] - **Ecology** (Greek *oikos* = home + *logos* = study): study of the **relationship between organisms (plants, animals) and their environment**; term coined by **Ernst Haeckel in 1866** ("Ökologie"); also called **bionomics** – synonymous with ecology, stressing natural systems that influence human systems.
[2:9] TABLE head: Branch / term | Meaning
  [2:9.0] Autecology | Ecology of a single species in relation to its environment
  [2:9.1] Synecology | Ecology of communities
  [2:9.2] Deep ecology | Term of the Norwegian philosopher Arne Naess (1973)
  [2:9.3] Ecological transition | Concept of John W. Bennett (*The Ecological Transition*, 1976)
  [2:9.4] Ecad | An organism (plant) modified by its environment – a habitat form without genetic change
  [2:9.5] Variation | Differences among individuals of the same species
  [2:9.6] Bionomics | = ecology ("management / laws of life")
[2:10] # Levels of organisation
[2:11] - Order from smallest to largest: **organism → population → community → ecosystem → (landscape) → biome → biosphere** (UPPSC 2017: population – community – ecosystem – landscape).
[2:12] TABLE head: Level | Meaning
  [2:12.0] Population | Individuals of one species in an area
  [2:12.1] Community | All populations of different species in an area
  [2:12.2] Ecosystem | Community + its non-living environment; basic functional unit of ecology
  [2:12.3] Biome | Large region defined by climate + dominant vegetation (rainforest, tundra, desert)
  [2:12.4] Biosphere | Narrow zone of contact of lithosphere, hydrosphere and atmosphere where life exists; all ecosystems together
[2:13] - **Biodiversity** measured as **alpha, beta, gamma**; "biological diversity" coined by **Raymond F. Dasmann**; hotspot concept – **Norman Myers** (≥1,500 endemic vascular plants, ≥70% habitat lost).
[2:14] - **Sere** = whole sequence of communities in succession; **habitat fragmentation** = continuous habitat broken into isolated patches; **anthrome** = human-altered terrestrial biome.
[2:15] # Ecologists and contributions

  [2:16.1] Eduard Suess | Coined "biosphere" (1875)

  [2:16.3] Joseph Grinnell | Ecological niche concept (habitat niche, 1917)
  [2:16.4] Charles Elton | *Animal Ecology* (1927); food chains; pyramid of numbers
  [2:16.5] G.E. Hutchinson | n-dimensional niche (1957)

## SUB 3 | Energy Flow, Productivity and the Ten Percent Law
[3:0] # Energy flow
[3:1] - **Unidirectional**: **sunlight → producers → herbivores → carnivores**; energy is not cycled. **Solar energy** is the primary source and **driving force** of an ecosystem.
[3:2] - **Law of conservation of energy** (first law of thermodynamics): energy can be neither created nor destroyed but **can be transformed** from one form to another – light → chemical energy in photosynthesis. Second law: each transfer loses energy as **heat**.
[3:3] - Under 50% of sunlight is **PAR**; plants fix about **2–10% of PAR**; green plants capture only about **1% of the sunlight** falling on their leaves (UPPSC 2025, SSC 2011).
[3:4] - **Total primary production efficiency** = share of incoming **solar energy captured and used in photosynthesis** – about **1–2%**.
[3:5] # Ten percent law
[3:6] - **Raymond Lindeman (1942)**: only about **10%** of the energy (as food) passes to the next trophic level; about **90%** is lost (respiration, excretion, locomotion, heat) – energy **decreases** at each transfer, hence 4–5 levels.
[3:7] - 1% = solar energy captured by plants; 10% = energy passed between trophic levels.
[3:8] # Productivity
[3:9] TABLE head: Term | Meaning
  [3:9.0] GPP (Gross Primary Production) | Total rate of organic matter (solar energy) fixed by producers in photosynthesis
  [3:9.1] NPP | GPP − producer respiration; available to consumers
  [3:9.2] Primary productivity | Rate at which solar energy is stored in green plants as food (autotroph level)
  [3:9.3] Secondary productivity | Rate of formation of new organic matter by consumers (heterotroph level)
[3:10] - Primary production in an **aquatic ecosystem** depends on **light penetration, nutrients and temperature** (all of these), with phytoplankton as producers.
[3:11] - Highest NPP per unit area: **tropical rainforest**; estuaries, coral reefs, swamps, mangroves also high. Lowest: **deserts and open ocean** (ocean total still large by area).
[3:12] - Decreasing productivity: **mangroves > grasslands > lakes > oceans** (UPSC 2013); ecotones (mangroves, estuaries) beat their surroundings.

## SUB 4 | Habitat, Niche and Population
[4:0] # Habitat vs niche
[4:1] TABLE head: Habitat | Niche
  [4:1.0] Physical place where an organism lives – its "address" | Functional role of a species in its habitat – its "profession"
  [4:1.1] One habitat holds many species | Unique to each species – no two species have identical niches
[4:2] - **Ecological niche** describes not only the physical space occupied but also the **functional role** in the community (UPSC 2013); it covers the range of conditions tolerated, resources used and role played. Concept enunciated by **Grinnell**.
[4:3] - **Fundamental niche:** full range of biotic and abiotic conditions and resources a species could use **free from interference of other species**; **realised niche**: actual range used under competition.
[4:4] - **Niche breadth:** specialists (**koala** – only eucalyptus) vs generalists.
[4:5] - **Resource partitioning:** competing species coexist by using different parts of a shared resource.
[4:6] - **Terrestrial** organisms live on land; **hydrophytes** are aquatic plants (not necessarily rootless).
[4:7] # Population attributes
[4:8] TABLE head: Term | Meaning
  [4:8.0] Natality | Birth of individuals; rate expressed per unit population per unit time, or as individuals produced per female per unit time
  [4:8.1] Mortality | Deaths per unit time
  [4:8.2] Density | Individuals per unit area (or volume)
  [4:8.3] Others | Age structure, sex ratio, dispersal (NOT dominant vegetation)
  [4:8.4] J-curve | Exponential growth, unlimited resources
  [4:8.5] S-curve | Logistic growth levelling at carrying capacity K
[4:9] - Age pyramids: **triangular = expanding; bell = stable; urn = declining**.
[4:10] - **Carrying capacity:** the **maximum population an environment can sustain** for ever **without being degraded**. It is **dynamic** – it changes with resources, climate and technology. Conditions: resource extraction must stay **below** regeneration, and **waste within the absorption capacity** (UPPSC 2025: only 2 correct).
[4:11] TABLE head: r-selected | K-selected
  [4:11.0] Many small offspring, little care, short life, unstable habitats – insects, weeds | Few large offspring, much care, long life, near K – elephants, humans

## SUB 5 | Ecological Pyramids
[5:0] - Show trophic levels; **base = producers**, apex = top consumers; **three types** – numbers, biomass, energy.
[5:1] TABLE head: Pyramid | Upright | Inverted
  [5:1.0] Numbers | Grassland, pond | Tree ecosystem; parasitic chain
  [5:1.1] Biomass | Forest, grassland | Pond, marine (small, fast-turning phytoplankton crop)
  [5:1.2] Energy | Always | Never
[5:2] - **Biomass** = total (dry) weight of organisms per unit area; **standing crop** = living biomass in a trophic level per unit area **at a given time** – a quantity, not a rate.
[5:3] - Limitations: assume a simple chain, ignore food webs; a species cannot be shown at two levels; **saprophytes and decomposers get no place**.
[5:4] - Energy decreases upward mainly through **heat loss**.

## SUB 8 | Terrestrial Ecosystems and Biomes
[8:0] - **Natural**: forests, grasslands, oceans, lakes, deserts. **Artificial**: crop fields, parks; **city** = extreme example. Estuary, marine, wetlands are aquatic, not terrestrial.
[8:1] - **Rainforest**: highest land species richness; fast decay → poor soil. **Taiga**: slow decay, thick litter, acidic soil. **Mature forests** give little net oxygen (respiration balances photosynthesis).
