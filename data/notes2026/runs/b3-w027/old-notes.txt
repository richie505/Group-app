## SUB 1 | Vital Rates – Definitions and Key Terms
[27:1:0] TABLE head: Term | Meaning / base
  [27:1:0.0] Fertility | Ability of women to give live birth (actual reproductive performance)
  [27:1:0.1] CBR / CDR | Births / deaths per 1,000 mid-year population
  [27:1:0.2] IMR | Deaths of infants before age 1 per 1,000 live births
  [27:1:0.3] Neonatal mortality | Death within the first four weeks (28 days) of life
  [27:1:0.4] MMR (maternal mortality ratio) | Maternal deaths per 1,00,000 live births
  [27:1:0.5] TFR | Average number of live births a woman would have over her reproductive span at current age-specific fertility rates
  [27:1:0.6] Replacement-level TFR | 2.1 children per woman (zero growth in the long run)
  [27:1:0.7] Growth rate (natural increase) | Birth rate − death rate in a given period (CBR 30 − CDR 10 = 20 per 1,000 = 2.0%)
  [27:1:0.8] Dependency ratio | (0-14 + 65+) ÷ (15-64)
  [27:1:0.9] Youth (National Youth Policy 2014) | 15-29 years (approved 9 Jan 2014, replaced NYP 2003)
[27:1:1] - Population change = **births, deaths, emigration and immigration** – all four.
[27:1:2] - **Zero population growth:** births (+ immigrants) = deaths (+ emigrants), so the population does not change.
[27:1:3] - Birth-rate data arranged **year by year** = **chronological** classification.
[27:1:4] - **SRS TFR:** 2016 – **2.3** (rural 2.5, urban 1.8); 2018 – 2.2; **NFHS-4 (2015-16) 2.2**; **NFHS-5 (2019-21) 2.0**; NFHS-5 highest Bihar 3.0; AP 1.7.
[27:1:5] - SRS 2024: TFR below replacement for the **fifth year running**.
[27:1:6] > IMR is per 1,000 live births; MMR per 1,00,000 – the classic base trap.
[27:1:7] ~~ Sources: PMF Human & Economic Geography 01 p.60-62 (IMR, U5MR, definitions); NextGen Indian Economy Part 1 - APPSC 04 p.23-24; Wikipedia "Zero population growth" (births plus in-migrants = deaths plus out-migrants, seen in web search); app notes 3:27 sub 2 and 4
[27:1:8] - **Female reproductive age group:** conventionally **15-44** (older Census tables use **14-44**, the keyed answer); NFHS now uses **15-49**.
## SUB 2 | Demographic Dividend and Its Window
[27:2:0] - **Demographic dividend / bonus / window:** growth potential from a rising share of the **working-age (15-64)** population – all three names are used (APPSC G2 2019: "All of these").
[27:2:1] - India is called a dividend country because of its **high population aged 15-64** (UPSC 2011); the "demographic bonus" around 2016 = **surge in the productive age group**.
[27:2:2] - Working age about **62.9%** (PMF: over 62% aged 15-59; 54% below 25).
[27:2:3] - **Census 2011 age shares:** aged 20+ **59.29%**; under 14 about **29% (28.67%)**; 65+ about **5% (4.8%)** – the "5-6%" option.
[27:2:4] - **Window:** **2005-06 to 2055-56** (NITI Aayog: until 2055); peak around **2041**, when 20-59 is about **59%** (Economic Survey 2018-19).
[27:2:5] - 0-19 share **41% (2011) → 25% (2041)**; 60+ **8.6% → 16% (2041)** and **20.5% (2051)**.
[27:2:6] - To obtain full benefits: **promote skill development** (UPSC 2013); without health, education, skills and jobs it becomes a **"demographic nightmare"**.
[27:2:7] - Broad-based age pyramid (underdeveloped country): **many young people and few old people**.
[27:2:8] ~~ Sources: PMF Human & Economic Geography 01 p.43-44 (definition, until 2055, Economic Survey 2018-19, nightmare); NextGen Indian Economy Part 1 - APPSC 04 p.49 (skill development); NextGen Society Group 2 Prelims 02 p.14-15 (2041 peak, shares); app notes 3:27 sub 5
[27:2:9] - **Age structure:** the **working-age group (15-59)** has been the largest share since at least 1980 (over half); 0-14 was about 39% in 1980.
## SUB 3 | Demographic Transition and Population Theories
[27:3:0] # The model
[27:3:1] TABLE head: Stage | Birth rate | Death rate | Result
  [27:3:1.0] I | High | High | Slow growth (pre-industrial)
  [27:3:1.1] II | High (rises or stays high) | Falling | Population explosion
  [27:3:1.2] III | Falling significantly | Low | Growth slows
  [27:3:1.3] IV | Low | Low | Stable or slow growth
[27:3:2] - Order with development: **high birth & high death → high birth & low death → low birth & low death** (UPSC 2012: 2, 3, 1).
[27:3:3] - Third stage = **significant fall in the birth rate** (death rate never becomes zero).
[27:3:4] - Theory: early form **Warren Thompson (1929)**; formal theory **Frank Notestein** (1940s-50s).
[27:3:5] - **India:** late Stage 3, moving into Stage 4; Stage-4 States (2011, low fertility): **Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh**.
[27:3:6] - **Urbanisation reduced both birth and death rates** (education, work, medical care).
[27:3:7] - Low birth rates / ageing / declining population in news: **Italy, Japan, South Korea** (not Nigeria, South Africa).
[27:3:8] # Population theories
[27:3:9] TABLE head: Theory | Propounder
  [27:3:9.0] Principle of Population (1798): population grows geometrically, food arithmetically; positive and preventive checks | T.R. Malthus – a theory of population
  [27:3:9.1] Optimum population | Edwin Cannan
  [27:3:9.2] The Population Bomb (1968) | Paul Ehrlich
[27:3:10] - Population growth: **larger working population is not an adverse effect**; smaller holdings, unemployment and less food per head are.
[27:3:11] ~~ Sources: NextGen Indian Economy Part 1 - APPSC 04 p.23 (Thompson 1929, Notestein); PMF Human & Economic Geography 01 p.35-36 (stages, UPSC 2012); NextGen Society Group 2 Prelims 02 p.16 (effects of population growth); app notes 3:27 sub 0-1
[27:3:12] - Population growth **reduces** wildlife and cultivable land and adds to pollution and floods (through deforestation) – an 'increase in wildlife' is not an effect.
## SUB 4 | Dependency Ratio and Harnessing the Dividend
[27:4:0] - **Dependent population** in the pyramid: **0-14** (and 65+); working age 15-64.
[27:4:1] - India's dependency ratio is falling because the **15-59 (working-age) share is relatively high**, itself due to the **fall in fertility**: **79% (1975) → 58% (2008) → about 48% (2021)**.
[27:4:2] - **Old-age dependency ratio (2011): 14.2%** (60+ ÷ 15-59; 10.9% in 1961).
[27:4:3] - Lower dependency ratio → **potential demographic dividend**.
[27:4:4] - **Female LFPR (PLFS):** 23.3% (2017-18) → **41.7% (2023-24)**; cutting it would weaken the dividend.
[27:4:5] - RBI typology: **ageing – Kerala, Tamil Nadu**; wide window – Bihar, UP; narrowing – Telangana, Uttarakhand; Kerala 60+ **22.8% by 2036**.
[27:4:6] ~~ Sources: app notes 3:27 sub 6-8; PMF Human & Economic Geography 01 p.43 (working age); PYQ explanation UPPCS 2016 (old-age dependency 14.2, MoSPI)
[27:4:7] - **Economic Survey 2016-17:** **two Indias** demographically – a **soon-to-be-ageing** peninsular India and a **young** hinterland India (Bihar, UP, Rajasthan, MP).
## SUB 5 | Fertility Determinants, Kerala and Andhra Pradesh
[27:5:0] - Birth rate falls with **female education and later marriage**, **child survival**, **urbanisation** and **contraception**; **urbanisation lowers the birth rate and high literacy goes with a low birth rate** (UPPCS 2021: both).
[27:5:1] - **Kerala's** lowest population growth: heavy **investment in promoting literacy**, especially female literacy (94% in 2011).
[27:5:2] - **Couple Protection Rate (CPR):** share of eligible couples protected by an approved contraceptive method; a rising CPR cuts the birth rate only when the protected couples are **young** (older couples have finished child-bearing).
[27:5:3] - More boys than girls are born (about **105 : 100**).
[27:5:4] - **Andhra Pradesh:** Stage 4; decadal growth peak **21.13% (1981-91) → 9.21% (2001-11)**; median age **32.5** (India 28); by 2036 under-14 share **25% → 15%**, 60+ **10% → 19%**; NFHS-5 TFR **1.7**.
[27:5:5] - AP: Gram Sabhas (May 2026) for feedback on incentives to reverse falling fertility.
[27:5:6] ~~ Sources: NextGen Indian Economy Part 1 - APPSC 04 p.57 (CPR one-liner); app notes 3:27 sub 8; PYQ explanations IAS 1993 / 1999

=== NOTES 3:29 | Poverty – Concepts (absolute, Relative, Chronic), Poverty Line Debates
## SUB 0 | Poverty Line in India – Basis and Calorie Norms
[29:0:0] - **Poverty line:** the minimum income / consumption level needed for basic needs; a person is poor if income or consumption falls **below that minimum** (APPSC: statements A and B correct).
[29:0:1] - It **varies with time and place** (basic needs and prices differ) and is **not** estimated by the Census every ten years (UPPCS 2025: A and R true, R explains A).
[29:0:2] - **Basis:** **household consumption expenditure** – **monthly per capita consumption expenditure (MPCE)** – not income: incomes of the self-employed and daily-wage workers are hard to judge and change with seasons. MCQs offering only "income method" among invented options key **income method**.
[29:0:3] - **Calorie norm:** **2,400 kcal rural / 2,100 kcal urban** per person per day (ICMR; **Alagh 1979**, Lakdawala 1993); Dandekar-Rath (1971): 2,250 for both.
[29:0:4] - Poverty in India is "defined in terms of" **calorie intake**; BPL = whether a person can obtain a **minimum prescribed food basket**.
[29:0:5] - **Poverty Line Basket (PLB):** socially accepted minimum basket of basic needs bought in the market.
[29:0:6] - Methods used: **Head Count Ratio, calorie intake, household consumption expenditure** (not per capita income); **literacy is not used** to fix the line.
[29:0:7] - **Survey:** **NSSO** household consumption expenditure survey (now **HCES**, MoSPI) about every 5 years; estimates by the Planning Commission / **NITI Aayog**; **BPL census** by the Ministry of Rural Development.
[29:0:8] - Official poverty 1999-2000 (MRP): about **26% (26.1)**.
[29:0:9] ~~ Sources: NextGen Indian Economy Part 1 - APPSC 04 p.24 (NSSO, MPCE, Alagh 2400/2100), p.54 (minimum food basket); NextGen Indian Economy - APPSC 16 p.8 (absolute standard, ICMR); NextGen Indian Economy - APPSC 17 p.4-5, 7, 9 (NSSO, Dandekar-Rath, Lakdawala, PLB); app notes 3:29 sub 2
## SUB 1 | Types and Concepts of Poverty
[29:1:0] TABLE head: Type | Meaning
  [29:1:0.0] Absolute | Below a fixed minimum subsistence / calorie norm; India's concern; UN definition adopted at Copenhagen (World Summit for Social Development), 1995
  [29:1:0.1] Relative | Income well below the median of the society – comparison of income groups (top vs bottom fractiles), not a fixed calorie norm; persists in USA, UK; measured by Lorenz curve / Gini
  [29:1:0.2] Subjective | A person's own perception of his position (perception is subjective)
  [29:1:0.3] Chronic | Always poor + usually poor – long periods or across generations
  [29:1:0.4] Transient | Churning poor (move in and out of poverty – small farmers, seasonal workers) + occasionally poor
  [29:1:0.5] Culture of poverty | Oscar Lewis, American anthropologist – poverty passed on as a way of life
[29:1:1] - **Cyclic / churning poor:** those who **keep moving between poor and non-poor**.
[29:1:2] - Types accepted: **absolute, relative, subjective** – "functional poverty" is not one.
[29:1:3] - **Capability approach:** **Amartya Sen**. **Engel's Law:** as income rises, the **share** spent on food **falls**.
[29:1:4] ~~ Sources: NextGen Indian Economy - APPSC 16 p.8-11 (absolute, relative, chronic and transient poor charts, Engel); app notes 3:29 sub 0
## SUB 2 | Causes and Social Indicators of Poverty
[29:2:0] - Causes: **slow growth, rapid population growth, unemployment** (unemployment raises the number below the line); a **rising rate of investment reduces** poverty.
[29:2:1] - Economic insecurity comes from **poverty, unemployment and falling real wages** – all three.
[29:2:2] - Roots of **urban poverty lie in rural areas**: low rural education → migrants get poor urban jobs (A and R true, R explains A).
[29:2:3] - **Social indicators:** illiteracy, lack of **safe drinking water** and sanitation, lack of health care, lack of **job opportunities**, malnutrition; **size of the house** and **number of means of transport** are **not** counted. **MPCE** (the poverty line itself) is an economic, not a social, indicator.
[29:2:4] - Solving poverty needs **a complex mix of methods**; economic and social issues are both relevant.
[29:2:5] - **MPI** (UNDP-OPHI, Alkire-Santos 2010): **health, education, living standards**.

## SUB 1 | Lorenz Curve and Gini Coefficient
[32:1:0] TABLE head: Person | Measure | Year
  [32:1:0.0] Max O. Lorenz (American) | Lorenz curve – graphical distribution of income / wealth | 1905 ("Methods of measuring the concentration of wealth")
  [32:1:0.1] Corrado Gini (Italian statistician) | Gini coefficient ("Variability and Mutability") | 1912
  [32:1:0.2] Simon Kuznets | Kuznets curve (inverted U) | 1955
[32:1:1] - **Lorenz curve:** X-axis = cumulative % of population (poorest first); Y-axis = cumulative % of income; **45° line = perfect equality**; the farther the curve bows away, the **greater the inequality**. It is a graph, not a number – it **measures inequality of income / distribution of income**.
[32:1:2] - **Gini coefficient:** **derived from the Lorenz curve** = area between the Lorenz curve and the equality line ÷ whole area under the equality line; measures **income inequality**.
[32:1:3] - Range **0 (perfect equality) to 1 (one person has all income)**; Gini index = coefficient × 100 (0.40 → 40); **higher Gini = more inequality** (so "higher Gini, lower inequality" is wrong).
[32:1:4] - **Relative poverty** within India is measured with the **Lorenz curve and Gini coefficient**; absolute poverty with the **poverty line**.
[32:1:5] - India: consumption Gini (World Bank, Spring 2025) **28.8 (2011-12) → 25.5 (2022-23)**; income Gini (World Inequality Database) 52 (2004) → 62 (2023).
[32:1:6] TABLE head: Curve | Shows
  [32:1:6.0] Lorenz | Distribution of income (inequality)
  [32:1:6.1] Phillips | Inflation vs unemployment
  [32:1:6.2] Laffer | Tax rate vs tax revenue
  [32:1:6.3] Marshall (demand curve) | Price vs quantity demanded
  [32:1:6.4] Kuznets | Inequality vs per capita income
[32:1:7] > Most repeated in this row (UPSC CDS 2019, UPPSC 2018, CGPSC 2018, JPSC 2023, SSC 2005, APPSC): what the Lorenz curve / Gini measures, its year, and the statements on Gini.

=== NOTES 3:33 | Unemployment - Types (structural, Frictional, Cyclical, Disguised, Seasonal, Educated)
## SUB 0 | Disguised Unemployment – Meaning, Test and Where Found
[33:0:0] - **Meaning:** **more persons are employed on a job than are actually needed** ("a job which a few can accomplish"); the extra workers appear employed but add nothing to output.
[33:0:1] - **Test:** the **marginal productivity of labour is zero** (or negative) – **withdrawing some workers leaves total output unchanged**, so a shift of people out of agriculture **need not reduce production**.
[33:0:2] - **Where:** mainly in **agriculture / the primary sector**, on family farms in **rural areas** (also the unorganised sector) – not in industry, factories, services, trade, banking or urban areas.
[33:0:3] - **NCERT example:** five family members work a two-hectare plot that needs fewer hands; NCERT calls it hidden **underemployment** – people appear to be working but are not fully employed.
[33:0:4] - **Hidden, not open:** the persons appear employed, so it is **not captured in PLFS headline unemployment rates**.
[33:0:5] - **Nurkse:** disguised unemployment is a **potential source of saving** – surplus farm labour can be withdrawn for capital formation (roads, irrigation, railways, housing) in under-developed countries.
[33:0:6] - Distractors: "persons with no jobs" (open), "unemployment of women / of people above 60", "fall in productivity", "change in technology", "data concealment".
[33:0:7] > Most repeated item of the whole topic (about 25 PYQs, APPSC 1998-2025, UPSC 2013, UPPSC, SSC): meaning = more workers than needed; MPL = zero; found in agriculture (I and II = agriculture + rural). When disguised is not offered, the agriculture answer is **seasonal**.
[33:0:8] ~~ Sources: NextGen Indian Economy - APPSC 19 p.1, p.5; NextGen Indian Economy Part 1 - APPSC 02 p.19, 03 p.3-4; OnlyIAS NCERT Economy 04 p.25, 05 p.5; Rocket Sheets Indian Economy p.49-50, p.72, p.84; app notes 3:33 sub 1
## SUB 1 | Types of Unemployment – Cause and Where Predominant
[33:1:0] TABLE head: Type | Cause / feature | Example | Where predominant
  [33:1:0.0] Seasonal | Work only at certain times of the year | Farm labour idle in off-season months | Agriculture, tourism, agro-industries
  [33:1:0.1] Disguised | Surplus labour, MPL = 0 | Five family members on a plot needing two | Agriculture, family farms, rural
  [33:1:0.2] Structural | Demand for labour short of supply; skill / technology mismatch; long-run | Labour-intensive economy turning capital-intensive | India's basic problem
  [33:1:0.3] Frictional (search) | Time between jobs; workers moving from one job to another | Graduate seeking a first job | Normal; exists even at full employment
  [33:1:0.4] Cyclical (Keynesian, demand-deficient) | Fall in aggregate demand – recession / depression | Lay-offs in a recession | Developed / capitalist economies
  [33:1:0.5] Technological | Change in technology, automation; a subtype of structural | Robots replace car workers | Manufacturing and routine services
  [33:1:0.6] Educated | Qualified persons cannot find matching jobs | – | Urban
  [33:1:0.7] Institutional | Labour laws, high minimum wages, union rules | Hiring halted due to rigid layoff rules | –
  [33:1:0.8] Vulnerable | No formal contract or social security | Construction labour, street vending | Informal sector
  [33:1:0.9] Casual / Classical | Day-to-day contract ends / wages too high (real-wage unemployment) | – | –
[33:1:1] - **Rural = seasonal + disguised; urban = educated** (NCERT rule). Under PLFS, open unemployment is higher in **urban** areas (AP 2023-24: rural 3.4%, urban 5.9%).
[33:1:2] - India has **all** types – structural, seasonal, disguised and underemployment ("All the above").
[33:1:3] - **At full employment** frictional (and structural) unemployment still exist; "functional" in an old APPSC paper is a misprint for **frictional**.
[33:1:4] - **Capital-intensive industries cause unemployment**; a labour-intensive industry is one that **employs more hands**.
[33:1:5] - Automation and AI reduce demand mainly for **low-skilled, routine jobs**.
[33:1:6] - **Structural unemployment** in a developing economy arises from **inadequate productive capacity** (too little capital to employ the growing labour force), not deflation.
[33:1:7] > Depression → cyclical (APPSC 2019); job-to-job → frictional (SSC); agriculture → disguised, or seasonal when disguised is absent (APPSC 2025).

## SUB 2 | Nature and Causes of Unemployment in India
[33:2:0] - **India's problem = chronic unemployment and under-employment** of a **structural** kind (demand for labour falls short of supply), with seasonal and disguised forms; **cyclical** unemployment is negligible in India and typical of developed capitalist economies.
[33:2:1] - **Cause in a developing country:** **lack of complementary factors of production – deficiency of capital**; the rate of capital formation limits jobs for a fast-growing population. Keynes's "lack of effective demand" explains developed economies.
[33:2:2] - India is a **labour-surplus economy**; Indian agriculture is **land-scarce and labour-surplus**; projected skilled-labour surplus about **245.3 million by 2030**.
[33:2:3] - **Causes:** rapid **population growth**, **labour immobility**, shift to **capital-intensive** production, **stagnation of the industrial sector**, neglected secondary sector and **MSMEs**, stringent labour laws, **delay in official paperwork**, **slow infrastructure**, import-oriented economy, **jobless growth** led by IT and finance.
[33:2:4] - About **90%** of the workforce is informal.
[33:2:5] - Odd ones out: "high growth of labour-intensive manufacturing" and "government aid to the unemployed" do NOT cause unemployment; disguised unemployment, poverty and low productivity hold villages back – the **Panchayat system** does not.
[33:2:6] - **Latest:** monthly PLFS (CWS) rate **5.5% in May 2026** (5.2% April); **30-35 million** seasonal labourers migrate every year (7 Sep 2026).
[33:2:7] - **Debate:** a falling rate may reflect **distress entry** into low-productivity work, not real job creation.
[33:2:8] > APPSC asks the "best description" (chronic unemployment and under-employment) and the cause (deficiency of capital / complementary factors); Keynes's effective demand is the developed-country answer.
[33:2:9] ~~ Sources: NextGen Indian Economy - APPSC 18 p.12; NextGen Indian Economy - APPSC 19 p.1, p.7-8; NextGen Indian Economy Part 1 - APPSC 02 p.19; app notes 3:33 sub 8
## SUB 3 | Theories – Keynes, Classical, Phillips, Friedman, Okun
[33:3:0] TABLE head: Economist / school | Idea
  [33:3:0.0] J.M. Keynes | Unemployment in developed economies = deficiency of effective demand; macro equilibrium is an under-employment, short-run equilibrium
  [33:3:0.1] Classical | Assumes full employment; wage cuts reduce unemployment; full employment is consistent with seasonal and frictional unemployment
  [33:3:0.2] A.W. Phillips (1958) | Inverse relation between inflation and unemployment (Phillips curve; Economica 1958, UK data 1861-1957)
  [33:3:0.3] Milton Friedman | Natural rate of unemployment hypothesis
  [33:3:0.4] Friedman-Phelps / monetarists | Long-run Phillips curve is vertical at the natural rate (NAIRU)
  [33:3:0.5] Arthur Okun | 1 point of unemployment above the natural rate ≈ 2% fall in output below potential GDP
[33:3:1] - **Natural rate = frictional + structural** unemployment, not cyclical.
[33:3:2] - **Stagflation** (high inflation + high unemployment, 1970s USA) broke the Phillips trade-off.
[33:3:3] > Phillips = inflation; Okun = output; Friedman = natural rate; Keynes = effective demand.

## SUB 5 | Underemployment and Other Classifications
[33:5:0] TABLE head: Term | Meaning
  [33:5:0.0] Open | Able and willing to work, no job at all
  [33:5:0.1] Voluntary | Unwilling to work at the prevailing wage (generous benefits, high marginal tax, waiting for a better job)
  [33:5:0.2] Involuntary | Willing at the going wage but no job – the labour force counts only involuntary unemployment
  [33:5:0.3] Visible underemployment | Fewer than normal hours of work
  [33:5:0.4] Invisible underemployment | Full-time but very low income or below ability (MBA working as a driver)
  [33:5:0.5] Long-term | Unemployed for more than 12 months
  [33:5:0.6] Unemployment trap | Benefits discourage taking up work
  [33:5:0.7] Chronic | Long-term unemployment persisting in the economy
[33:5:1] - **Disguised ≠ underemployment** in strict use: underemployment = not using full skills or hours; disguised = surplus labour with zero marginal output (NCERT uses "underemployment" loosely for both).

## SUB 2 | Status Approaches – UPS, UPSS, CWS, CDS
[34:2:0] TABLE head: Approach | Reference period / unit | Counted employed if | Captures
  [34:2:0.0] UPS (Usual Principal Status) | 365 days | Worked the major time (183 days or more) | Chronic (long-term) unemployment
  [34:2:0.1] UPSS (Principal + Subsidiary) | 365 days | Also subsidiary work for at least 30 days | Broadest worker count
  [34:2:0.2] CWS (Current Weekly Status) | 7 days | Worked at least 1 hour on any day | Chronic + seasonal
  [34:2:0.3] CDS (Current Daily Status) | Each day of the reference week; unit = half day | 4 hours or more = full day; 1-4 hours = half day | Daily / intermittent; highest rate
[34:2:1] - Persons **unemployed for a major part of the year** = **usual status** unemployment; chronic unemployment is measured with **usual status** data, and the weekly status also captures it (so "both US and CWS").
[34:2:2] - **CDS gives the highest rate** – it also counts the days of unemployment of persons recorded as employed under CWS.
[34:2:3] - NSS uses **three reference periods** (year, week, day); **"Usual / Current Monthly Status" is NOT an NSSO approach**.
[34:2:4] - **Census:** **main worker = worked 183 days or more** in the year; marginal worker = less than 183 days. The 183-day line is not an NSS approach.
[34:2:5] > UPPSC 2005, 2016, 2019 and APPSC Group-I: chronic = usual status; "monthly" approach is the odd one out.

## SUB 3 | Labour Force Indicators – LFPR, WPR, UR
[34:3:0] TABLE head: Indicator | Definition
  [34:3:0.0] Labour force | Employed + unemployed (seeking or available for work); workforce = employed only
  [34:3:0.1] LFPR | Labour force (employed + those willing and available) ÷ working-age population (per 100 / 1000)
  [34:3:0.2] WPR (Worker Population Ratio) | Employed persons as a percentage of the total population
  [34:3:0.3] UR (Unemployment Rate) | Unemployed ÷ labour force × 100
  [34:3:0.4] PU (Proportion Unemployed) | Unemployed ÷ population
