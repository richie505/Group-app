## SUB 0 | Meaning of BoP – BoP vs Balance of Trade
[0:0] # Definition
[0:1] - **Balance of Payments (BoP):** a **systematic and complete record of all economic transactions between the residents of a country and the rest of the world** during a period, **normally one year**.
[0:2] - BoP has **two main accounts – the current account and the capital account** (NextGen); it is not "only capital" or "only current" or "only exports and imports".
[0:3] - **IMF BPM6** uses **three accounts – current, capital and financial**; the **RBI generally combines capital and financial** under "Capital Account".
[0:4] - BoP includes **visible trade, invisible trade and borrowings** – "all of the above"; the term is used in relation to **exports and imports** (foreign trade), not factory sales or tax collection.
[0:5] - UPSC 2013 wording: BoP = systematic record of "**all import and export transactions** of a country during a given period, normally a year" – the official key, the closest fit (goods exports only, government-to-government deals or capital movements alone are parts).
[0:6] # Balance of Trade vs Balance of Payments
[0:7] TABLE head: Basis | Balance of Trade (BoT) | Balance of Payments (BoP)
  [0:7.0] Coverage | Goods only (visible) | All transactions: goods, services, transfers, income, capital
  [0:7.1] Formula | Goods exports − goods imports | Whole external account, double-entry
  [0:7.2] Place | Part of the current account | Current + capital/financial account
  [0:7.3] Picture | Not the complete record | Better, complete picture
[0:8] - **A-R (UPSC 2006):** BoP gives a better picture than BoT **because** it covers both **visible and invisible** items – A and R both true, R explains A.
[0:9] - **BoT surplus**: goods exports > goods imports; **BoT deficit**: goods imports > goods exports. "Exports minus imports" is the BoT, **not** the BoP.
[0:10] - **Favourable BoP** – achieved by **stepping up exports and reducing imports** (both).

## SUB 1 | Structure – Current Account vs Capital Account
[1:0] TABLE head: Current account | Capital (and financial) account
  [1:0.0] Merchandise exports and imports (visible trade; trade balance) | Foreign loans, FDI, portfolio investment (FPI/FII), ADRs/GDRs
  [1:0.1] Invisibles – services: software, travel, transport, banking, insurance | ECB, external assistance
  [1:0.2] Invisibles – primary income: interest, dividends, profits, wages | NRI deposits (FCNR(B)), banking capital
  [1:0.3] Secondary income: private remittances, gifts, grants | Short-term trade credit; change in forex reserves
[1:1] - **Current account** = goods + services + income + transfers; it **includes the Balance of Trade**. **Capital receipts and payments are NOT** part of it (CDS 2014).
[1:2] - **Invisibles** = services + income + transfers; **invisible export = export of services**. Services = **non-factor** (shipping, banking, tourism, software) + factor income.
[1:3]   - "Invisible items" in the classic sense = **service transactions**; goods are the visible items.
[1:4] - **Capital account** (UPSC 2013): **foreign loans, FDI and portfolio investment** – **private remittances are current account** (so codes with remittances are wrong). **FDI is NOT current account.**
[1:5] - **Current-account balance** = exports − imports of goods and services + net primary income + net secondary income.
[1:6] # Credit and debit
[1:7] - Every **inflow of foreign exchange = credit side**; every outflow = debit (double-entry book-keeping).
[1:8] - **Gift to foreigners = debit** in the current account; gift from foreigners = credit; exports of goods/services = credit.
[1:9] - Residents **buying foreign assets = capital-account debit**; **sale of Indian assets to foreigners = credit**.
[1:10] TABLE head: Item | BoP placement
  [1:10.0] Software services exports | Current account (services)
  [1:10.1] Foreign loans, FDI, FPI | Capital account
  [1:10.2] Private remittances | Current account – secondary income (transfers)
  [1:10.3] Crude oil imports | Merchandise trade
[1:11] # The identity
[1:12] - **Current + Capital + Financial account + Net Errors & Omissions = 0** – BoP **always balances** in accounting terms; a surplus in one account = deficit in another. **BoT need not balance.**
[1:13] - **Autonomous** transactions ("above the line") happen for their own sake; **accommodating** ones ("below the line") fill the gap – **official reserve changes, official borrowing**.
[1:14] - Overall BoP **positive → adds to reserves; negative → draws them down**; monetary authority = ultimate financier.
[1:15] ~~ Sources: NextGen Indian Economy Part 1 - APPSC 04 p.16-18 (current account, invisibles, capital-account debit/credit, autonomous vs accommodating); Rocket Indian Economy p.36 (item-account table); NextGen Indian Economy Part 1 - APPSC 04 p.58 (invisible trade = services); app notes 3:75 sub 1-2
## SUB 2 | Convertibility, FERA/FEMA and Rupee Milestones
[2:0] TABLE head: Type | Freedom to convert for
  [2:0.0] Current account convertibility | Trade, income and transfers (current international transactions)
  [2:0.1] Capital account convertibility | Investment and financial assets (FDI, portfolio, borrowing) without capital controls
[2:1] - The rupee is **fully convertible only on the current account** – **not** on the capital account (still partial) and **not into gold** (UPSC 2000).
[2:2] - **IMF Article VIII** makes **current account convertibility** obligatory; India accepted it in **August 1994** (operationalised 19 Aug 1994).
[2:3] TABLE head: Year | Step
  [2:3.0] 1948 | Rupee linked to sterling till 1948
  [2:3.1] 1973 | FERA (prevent outflow of foreign exchange)
  [2:3.2] Sep 1975 | Rupee delinked from pound sterling, pegged to a basket of currencies
  [2:3.3] July 1991 | Rupee devalued in two steps (1 and 3 July) – about 22% – to meet the BoP crisis
  [2:3.4] 1992-93 Budget | LERMS: dual rate – 40% official, 60% market; partial convertibility
  [2:3.5] 1 Mar 1993 | Unified, market-determined exchange rate (trade account convertibility)
  [2:3.6] Aug 1994 | Full current account convertibility – Article VIII accepted
  [2:3.7] 1999 / 1 June 2000 | FEMA enacted / in force, replacing FERA
[2:4] - **FEMA preamble:** "to **facilitate external trade and payments**" and promote orderly development of the forex market – not import control.
[2:5] TABLE head: Feature | FERA 1973 | FEMA 1999
  [2:5.0] Nature | Criminal | Civil
  [2:5.1] Approach | Control of outflow | Management and regulation
  [2:5.2] Character | Strict, restrictive | Liberal
[2:6] # Committees

## SUB 4 | CAD, Invisibles and Remittances – Trends
[4:0] - **CAD**: payments exceed receipts from **goods, services, transfers and net income** (excess of total imports over total exports). A CAD is **not** caused by exporting more.
[4:1] - India runs a **persistent trade deficit** in goods; services and remittances only partly offset it.
[4:2] TABLE head: Period | CAD / current account
  [4:2.0] 1990-91 | 3.1-3.2% of GDP (crisis); IMF drawn in 1991
  [4:2.1] 2001-02 to 2003-04 | Surplus three years running (first in 24 years in 2001-02) – the only such run
  [4:2.2] FY13 | Peak 4.8% of GDP (taper tantrum) – higher than 1990-91
  [4:2.3] 2013-14 | Trade deficit, net invisibles positive, capital account in surplus (financed the CAD)
  [4:2.4] Q4 2023-24 | Surplus $5.7 bn (0.6%) – net services receipts $42.7 bn (software, travel, business services)
  [4:2.5] FY 2025-26 | $25.2 bn (~0.6% of GDP); merchandise deficit $337.3 bn; Q3 FY26 $13.2 bn (1.3%)
  [4:2.6] Q1 2026-27 | $4.2 bn, 0.5% of GDP
[4:3] - **FY24 external recovery:** **moderation in merchandise export growth + sharp decline in import growth**; **services exports cushioned the trade deficit** (up 4.85% to **USD 341.06 bn**).
[4:4] - **Causes of CAD:** structural – oil and gold imports, low export competitiveness, savings-investment gap; **cyclical – temporary global slowdown**, commodity shocks.
[4:5] # Invisibles and remittances
[4:6] - **Largest source of net invisibles among the classic items: private transfers = foreign remittances** (APPSC 2008); since the 2010s **net software services** are larger still – **record services surplus $188.8 bn (FY25)**, about **two-thirds** of the merchandise deficit; **software = largest part**.
[4:7] - **India = world's largest remittance recipient** (also in **2015**): **$135.4 bn (3.5% of GDP) in FY25**; World Bank 2024: India $129 bn > **Mexico** $68 bn > China > Philippines > Pakistan.
[4:8] - **Sources (RBI survey 2023-24): USA 27.7% > UAE 19.2% > UK 10.8%.**
[4:9] - Remittances are **counter-cyclical** and create **no debt**; **net primary income is an outflow** for India.

## SUB 5 | Correcting BoP Disequilibrium
[5:0] TABLE head: Expenditure-switching | Expenditure-reducing / financing
  [5:0.0] Devaluation/depreciation | Tight fiscal policy: cut government spending, raise taxes
  [5:0.1] Import duties (tariffs), quotas, import substitution | Tight monetary policy: higher interest rates
  [5:0.2] Export incentives, tourist promotion | Foreign loans, IMF assistance, foreign investment (FDI); exchange control
  [5:0.3] Higher gold import duty | 
[5:1] - **Not a correction:** **levying higher export duties** (cuts exports); **increasing** government spending on domestic projects (raises demand and imports).
[5:2] - **Encouraging FDI** *finances* a deficit rather than correcting it – the "NOT typically used" answer when devaluation, tariffs and cutting government spending are the other options.
[5:3] - **Removing a BoP surplus** (elastic demand): **appreciate/revalue** the currency; devaluation, import controls or inflation abroad would widen the surplus.
[5:4] - A country with a **large current-account surplus** has a **larger capacity to give aid and loans** abroad.
[5:5] - **1991:** rupee **devalued twice** (1 and 3 July 1991) to adjust the BoP deficit.
[5:6] - **May 2026:** appeal to cut gold and fuel imports + gold import-duty hike = expenditure-switching steps.
[5:7] - **Rekhi Committee (1992) = indirect taxes** (not export-import simplification); Rangarajan = BoP; Goiporia = bank customer service.
[5:8] ~~ Sources: NextGen Indian Economy Part 1 - APPSC 02 p.15-16 (devaluation as expenditure-switching; methods incl. foreign investment); Rocket Indian Economy p.69-70 (two devaluations 1991); app notes 3:75 sub 7; general economics (surplus removal, aid capacity), not checked against a library file
## SUB 6 | IMF and SDR
[6:0] - **IMF main function:** help members solve **balance of payments problems**; loans meet **temporary BoP deficits** (not development projects – that is the World Bank); core function **surveillance**; **lender of last resort** when reserves cannot cover a deficit. India drew on the IMF in **1991**.
[6:1] TABLE head: Facility | Meaning
  [6:1.0] Reserve (gold) tranche | Member's unconditional drawing right from its quota – a credit system of the IMF
  [6:1.1] Rapid Financing Instrument (RFI) | Rapid help for any member with an urgent BoP need
  [6:1.2] Rapid Credit Facility (RCF) | Concessional, for low-income countries
  [6:1.3] Stand-By Arrangement / Extended Fund Facility | Regular lending windows
[6:2] - RBI swaps, SAARC swap framework and **Special Rupee Vostro Accounts** are **NOT** IMF instruments.
[6:3] - **SDR** = IMF reserve asset ("paper gold", 1969), exists only as **book-keeping entries**, not paper currency or metal.
[6:4] - Original SDR rule (reconstitution, abolished 1981): members kept on average **30%** of allocations over five years – so about **70% could be used without repayment**.

## SUB 7 | External Vulnerability and Debt
[7:0] - **Immunity to a global financial crisis (UPSC 2020): not depending on short-term foreign borrowings** – only this; more foreign banks and full capital account convertibility raise exposure.
[7:1] - **Reduce currency-crisis risk (UPSC 2019): IT-sector forex earnings and remittances** – higher government spending does not.
[7:2] - Safeguards: **adequate reserves** (import cover), **low short-term debt**, less reliance on volatile **hot money** (FPI, debt flows). FCNR(B) and ECB inflows are **temporary**; **FDI** supports a self-sustaining capital surplus.
[7:3] - **US Fed rate hikes + strong dollar** → higher US yields → hot money leaves emerging markets → rupee weakens.
[7:4] - **2013 taper tantrum:** Morgan Stanley's "**Fragile Five**" included India. Morgan Stanley 1998 emerging-markets rating: India **3rd** (printed key).
[7:5] - **FY26:** FPIs pulled out **$16.4 bn**; net FDI only **$6.9 bn**.
