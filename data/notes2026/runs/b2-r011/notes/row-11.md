=== Article 21A – Right to Education, 86th Amendment and RTE Act
# Article 21A
- **Text:** the State shall provide **free and compulsory education** to all children of the age of **6 to 14 years** "in such manner as the State may, **by law**, determine". Only **elementary** education is an FR – not higher or professional education.
- **Inserted by:** the **86th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2002** (introduced as the **93rd Amendment Bill**, 2001) – not the 42nd, 44th, 74th, 91st or the RTE Act itself (an Act cannot add an Article). The **93rd Amendment Act, 2005** is a different law (OBC reservation in educational institutions, Art. 15(5)).
- **In force:** Art. 21A and the **RTE Act** both came into force on **1 April 2010** – so the year the right to education "came into force as an FR" is **2010**, though the amendment is of **2002**.
- **Nature:** a **Fundamental Right** (not statutory, natural or a DPSP right); an **extension of the right to life (Art. 21)**; essential to the right to development as a human right. Aim: **"Education for All"**.
- **Earlier position:** free and compulsory education was a **Part IV (DPSP)** provision – the original **Art. 45** asked the State to provide it for all children **until they complete 14 years** (within ten years).
| 86th Amendment changed | New content |
|---|---|
| **Art. 21A** (FR) | Free, compulsory education **6–14 years** |
| **Art. 45** (DPSP) | **Early childhood care and education** for children **below 6** |
| **Art. 51A(k)** (Fundamental Duty) | Parent/guardian to provide opportunities for education to child 6–14 – the **11th duty** |
- **Not** a change of the 86th Amendment: deleting the **Right to Property** – that was the **44th (1978)**.
# Cases before 21A
| Case | Year | Ruling |
|---|---|---|
| **Mohini Jain** v. State of Karnataka | **1992** | Right to education flows from Art. 21 |
| **Unnikrishnan J.P.** v. State of **Andhra Pradesh** | **1993** | Read education into Art. 21: **free education up to 14 years** is a right; beyond that it depends on the State's economic capacity |
- **Sequence:** Mohini Jain (1992) → Unnikrishnan (1993) → 86th Amendment (2002) → RTE Act (2009) → in force (2010).
# RTE Act, 2009 (Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act)
- **Passed by Parliament:** Rajya Sabha 20 July 2009, Lok Sabha **4 August 2009** (assent 26 Aug 2009); **in force 1 April 2010**.
- **Sec. 3:** every child of **6–14** has a right to free and compulsory education in a **neighbourhood school** till elementary education is complete.
- **25%** of entry-level seats (class I or pre-school) in **unaided private schools** for children of weaker and disadvantaged groups of the neighbourhood; schools reimbursed; **not minority schools**.
- **Duties:** appropriate Government and local authority to establish schools in the neighbourhood within **3 years**; **every parent/guardian** to admit the child; **funding is a concurrent responsibility of Centre and States** – the Centre does **not** bear sole responsibility.
- Amendments: 2012, 2017, **2019** (no-detention policy relaxed – regular exams in classes 5 and 8), 2022.

=== Article 20 – Protection in Respect of Conviction for Offences
- **Available to:** every person – **citizens, foreigners and legal persons** (companies). It cannot be suspended in an emergency (see last sub-section).
| Clause | Protection | Scope / NOT covered |
|---|---|---|
| **20(1)** | **No ex post facto criminal law** – no conviction except for violating a "law in force" when the act was done, and **no penalty greater** than that law allowed | Only **criminal** laws – civil or tax laws can be retrospective; bars conviction/heavier penalty, not the trial; not applicable to **preventive detention** |
| **20(2)** | **No double jeopardy** – no person shall be **prosecuted and punished for the same offence more than once** | Only before a **court or judicial tribunal**; **not departmental** or administrative proceedings |
| **20(3)** | **No self-incrimination** – no person **accused** of an offence shall be **compelled to be a witness against himself** | Covers **testimonial compulsion** (oral and documentary); **not** physical evidence – fingerprints, specimen signatures, blood, DNA (**Kathi Kalu Oghad, 1961**); **voice sample** for investigation is allowed (**Ritesh Sinha, 2019**) |
- **Ex post facto law** = a **retrospective** law (not prospective, not a Bill). Hence **Parliament cannot** make a **criminal law with retrospective effect** that convicts or enhances punishment.
- **Double jeopardy:** maxim *nemo debet bis vexari*; Art. 20(2) rests on **autrefois convict** (previously convicted) – both prosecution **and** punishment are needed, so *autrefois acquit* is not covered. CrPC Sec. 300 → **Sec. 337 BNSS**.
- **Nature:** 20(1) is **substantive** law of criminal liability and penalty; 20(2) and 20(3) are **procedural**. "Art. 20 does not protect against testimonial compulsion" is **false**.
- **Selvi v. State of Karnataka (2010):** involuntary **narco-analysis, polygraph and brain-mapping** violate **Art. 20(3)** and **Art. 21** (personal liberty, privacy).
- **Not in Art. 20:** **prohibition against torture** (that is **Art. 21**); preventive detention safeguards (Art. 22).
> Asked as: "which Article – double jeopardy / self-incrimination" (about 10 PYQs), the clause (20(2), 20(3)), maxims (autrefois convict, ex post facto = retrospective), "NOT part of Art. 20" (torture), voice sample, Selvi.

=== Rights Read into Article 21 and the Right to Privacy
# Article 21 at its widest
- **Right to life** and **personal liberty** ("freedom"); available to **citizens and non-citizens**. Many unenumerated rights are read in by the Supreme Court – e.g. **food**, health, **shelter**, **livelihood**, dignity; **no right to permanent housing** is enumerated anywhere.
| Right | Case |
|---|---|
| Travel abroad; fair procedure | **Satwant Singh Sawhney (1967)**; **Maneka Gandhi (1978)** |
| **Privacy** | Govind v. State of MP (1975); Malak Singh (1980); **K.S. Puttaswamy (2017)** |
| Privacy – **phone tapping** | **PUCL v. Union of India (1997)** – tapping infringes Art. 21 |
| Livelihood | **Olga Tellis (1985)** |
| Shelter | **Shantistar Builders v. N.K. Totame (1990)**; **Chameli Singh (1995)** |
| Emergency medical aid | **Parmanand Katara (1989)**; **Paschim Banga Khet Mazdoor Samity (1996)** – emergency medical care |
| Clean environment and air | **Subhash Kumar (1991)** |
| Marry a person of choice | **Lata Singh v. State of UP (2006)**; Shafin Jahan (Hadiya, 2018) |
| Human dignity | **Francis Coralie Mullin (1981)** |
| Free legal aid | **M.H. Hoskot (1978)**; Hussainara Khatoon |
| Speedy trial | **Hussainara Khatoon (1979)** |
| Protection of women in police custody | **Sheela Barse v. State of Maharashtra (1983)**; handcuffing curbed in **Prem Shankar Shukla (1980)** |
| Compensation for violation | **Nilabati Behera (1993)** |
| Workplace sexual harassment | **Vishaka (1997)** |
- **Also under Art. 21:** rights of **transgender persons** (NALSA, 2014), of **craniopagus (conjoined) twins**, of a mentally retarded woman to bear a child (Suchita Srivastava, 2009) – all three (CDS 2017); protection against **torture** and **solitary confinement**.
- **Marriage:** Art. 21 protects the choice of partner (**UPSC 2019: Art. 21**), but **Supriyo Chakraborty (2023)**: there is **no unqualified fundamental right to marry** as such. **Travel abroad** is an FR (Maneka). So APPSC G2 Mains 2025 key: **both statements right**.
# Right to Privacy
- **Justice K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India, 24 Aug 2017:** a **nine-judge** bench, unanimous (**9:0**) – privacy is a Fundamental Right, an intrinsic part of **Article 21 and the freedoms of Part III** (UPSC 2018, 2024: **Art. 21**).
- **Overruled** M.P. Sharma (1954) and Kharak Singh (1962) to the extent they denied privacy as an FR.
- Test for limiting privacy: **legality** (a law), **legitimate aim**, **proportionality**.

=== Article 21 – Procedure Established by Law, Due Process, Natural Justice
- **Text:** "No person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to **procedure established by law**" – phrase from the **Japanese** Constitution; "**due process of law**" is American. Art. 21 does not use the words "due process".
| A.K. Gopalan (1950) | Maneka Gandhi (1978) |
|---|---|
| Any procedure enacted by the legislature suffices | Procedure must be **just, fair and reasonable** |
| Arts. 19, 21, 22 separate | Arts. **14, 19, 21** linked – the golden triangle |
| Protects only against the **executive** | Also against **arbitrary legislation** – in effect American due process read into Art. 21 |
- **Due process of law**, in essence = the **principle of natural justice** (UPSC 2023); the doctrine is now read into **Article 21**.
- **Natural justice:** **audi alteram partem** = "hear the other side" – the **right to be heard**; *nemo judex in causa sua* = no one a judge in his own cause.
- **Protection is against** the State's **executive and legislative** action (Laxmikanth). *(Since **Kaushal Kishor v. State of UP, 2023**, Arts. 19 and 21 can also be enforced against private persons.)*
- **Unguided, uncontrolled executive discretion** breaks **Article 14** (arbitrary).
- **Capital punishment** does not violate Art. 21 – **Bachan Singh (1980)**, "rarest of rare"; so it is "not within the purview" of Art. 21 rights.

=== Article 22 – Arrest and Preventive Detention
# Punitive arrest – 22(1), 22(2)
- Arrested person must be **told the grounds** of arrest; may **consult and be defended by a lawyer of choice**; must be **produced before the nearest magistrate within 24 hours** (**Art. 22(2)**, excluding journey time) – not 12, 48 hours or one week.
- **22(3) exclusions:** **enemy aliens** and persons held under a **preventive detention** law – not ordinary foreigners or OCIs. So detention beyond 24 hours without a magistrate is allowed only in **preventive detention**.
- **Also outside:** arrest on a **court order** (person in jail under judicial order), **civil arrest**, deportation of an alien.
- **Not guaranteed:** **bail as of right**, trial by jury. CrPC Secs. 50, 57 → BNSS Secs. 47, 58. **Vihaan Kumar v. State of Haryana (2025):** grounds must be meaningfully communicated to every arrestee.
# Preventive detention – 22(4) to 22(7)
- **Preventive** = detention **without trial**, on suspicion of future harmful acts; **punitive** = after trial and conviction. Justified for **public order**, not routine **law and order**.
- **22(4):** no detention beyond **3 months** unless an **Advisory Board** (**High Court judges** or persons qualified to be) reports sufficient cause. **44th Amendment (1978)** cut it to **2 months**, but that clause was **never notified** – 3 months still applies.
- **22(5):** grounds communicated + **earliest opportunity of representation**. **22(7):** **Parliament** fixes the maximum period and Advisory Board procedure.
| Who legislates | Subjects |
|---|---|
| **Parliament alone** | **Defence, foreign affairs, security of India** |
| Parliament and States | Public order, security of a State, essential supplies |

=== Right to Die and Euthanasia
| Case | Year | Ruling |
|---|---|---|
| Maruti Shripati Dubal (**Bombay HC**) | 1987 | Right to die part of Art. 21; Sec. 309 IPC unconstitutional |
| Chenna Jagadeeswar (**AP High Court**) | 1988 | Art. 21 gives **no right to die**; Sec. 309 upheld |
| **P. Rathinam** | **1994** | Sec. 309 IPC (attempted suicide) unconstitutional |
| **Gian Kaur v. State of Punjab** | **1996** | Overruled Rathinam: **"right to life" does not include "right to die"** (only a right to die with dignity); Sec. 309 valid |

=== Articles 20 and 21 During an Emergency
- **44th Amendment (1978):** the enforcement of **Articles 20 and 21 cannot be suspended** under Art. 359 even during a National Emergency – the only such FRs (Arts. 14, 19, 22 can be).
- So "right to life and personal liberty" can never be suspended; freedom of movement, residence, profession (Art. 19) can.
