## SUB 0 | Fission vs Fusion – Chain Reaction, Reactor vs Bomb, Energy of the Sun
[0:0] TABLE head: Point | Fission | Fusion
  [0:0.0] Process | Heavy nucleus splits (U-235 + slow neutron → barium + krypton + 2–3 neutrons + energy) | Two light nuclei join (deuterium + tritium → helium + neutron)
  [0:0.1] Trigger / condition | Neutron hit on a fissile nucleus; critical mass | Very high temperature (10⁷–10⁸ K) and pressure – "thermonuclear"
  [0:0.2] Energy | Large | More per unit mass (3–4 times fission)
  [0:0.3] Where | Nuclear reactors (controlled), atom bomb (uncontrolled) | Sun and stars, hydrogen bomb; fusion power still at R&D stage
[0:1] - **Breaking apart of a nucleus = fission**, not fusion – the "NOT correct" statement in APPSC/UPPSC items.
[0:2] - All present-day nuclear electricity comes from **fission**.
[0:3] # Chain reaction and criticality
[0:4] - Each fission releases **more neutrons** (2–3), which split more nuclei → **chain reaction**; fast neutrons are **slowed by a moderator (graphite, heavy water)** so that U-235 captures them. Not every neutron causes a fresh fission, and neutrons do not all react "immediately" (UPSC 2001: only statements I and III).
[0:5] - **Critical mass** = minimum fissile mass that sustains a chain reaction; **criticality** = self-sustaining chain reaction – every operating reactor is "critical".
[0:6] - **Reactor vs atom bomb:** both use a chain reaction; in a **reactor it is controlled** (moderator, control rods), in a **bomb it is uncontrolled**. Reactors do use moderators (CDS 2017: only II and III correct).
[0:7] # Atom bomb and hydrogen bomb
[0:8] TABLE head: Feature | Atom (fission) bomb | Hydrogen (thermonuclear) bomb
  [0:8.0] Principle | Uncontrolled fission of U-235 / Pu-239 | Uncontrolled fusion of deuterium and tritium into helium, triggered by a fission bomb
  [0:8.1] Person | Otto Hahn (with F. Strassmann, 1938-39) – discovered nuclear fission | Edward Teller – "father of the hydrogen bomb"; first US test Nov 1952, Eniwetok
  [0:8.2] Power | Large | Far more powerful
  [0:8.3] Hiroshima, 6 Aug 1945 (first) | Little Boy | U-235
  [0:8.4] Nagasaki, 9 Aug 1945 | Fat Man | Plutonium-239
[0:9] - Only **Hiroshima and Nagasaki** were atom-bombed (not Tokyo, Sendai, Miyazaki).
[0:10] - **North Korea** claimed a successful **hydrogen bomb** test (January 2016; again September 2017).
[0:11] - India's **Shakti 1998** (Pokhran-II) series included a thermonuclear device.
[0:12] # Energy of the Sun and stars
[0:13] - Sun and stars shine by **nuclear fusion** – hydrogen nuclei fuse into **helium** in the core (about 15 million K); not fission, chemical reaction, photoelectric effect or gravity. "Energy generation in stars" is the statement **not** associated with fission.
[0:14] > Exam angle: about 35 PYQs – "principle of the atom bomb / hydrogen bomb", "energy of the Sun", "difference between reactor and bomb = controlled chain reaction". Uncontrolled fusion = H-bomb; controlled fission = reactor.

## SUB 1 | Atomic Structure – Nucleus, Isotopes, Isobars and Isotones
[1:0] TABLE head: Particle | Charge | Mass | Location
  [1:0.0] Proton | +1 (equal to the charge of a positron) | ≈ 1 amu | Nucleus
  [1:0.1] Neutron | Zero | ≈ 1 amu (slightly more than proton) | Nucleus (does not revolve around it)
  [1:0.2] Electron | −1 | ≈ 1/1837 of proton | Shells around the nucleus
[1:1] - **Nucleus = protons + neutrons** (nucleons); it holds **almost all the mass** of the atom. Rutherford's **gold-foil (α-scattering)** experiment showed a small, dense, **positively charged nucleus** at the centre.
[1:2] - Electrons are held to the nucleus by the **electric (electrostatic)** force; electrons in the outermost (highest) shell are **valence electrons**. Atoms of different elements have **different atomic numbers and different numbers of valence electrons**.
[1:3] - Discoverers: electron – J.J. Thomson (1897); nucleus – Rutherford (1911); neutron – **James Chadwick (1932)**.
[1:4] # Atomic number, mass number, atomic weight
[1:5] - **Atomic number (Z) = number of protons** (= electrons in a neutral atom); fixes the element.
[1:6] - **Mass number (A) = protons + neutrons** – a whole number; neutrons = A − Z.
[1:7] TABLE head: Example | Working | Answer
  [1:7.0] ⁹⁴Pu²⁴² | 242 − 94 | 148 neutrons
  [1:7.1] Carbon, 6 protons, A = 12 | 12 − 6 | 6 neutrons
  [1:7.2] 18 electrons, 20 neutrons | 18 + 20 | A = 38
  [1:7.3] 7 electrons in M-shell, 18 neutrons | 2 + 8 + 7 = 17 protons; 17 + 18 | A = 35
  [1:7.4] Atomic weight W, atomic number N | protons = Z | N
[1:8] - **Atomic weight** is the average mass of an element's isotopes, so it **can be a fraction** (mass number cannot).
[1:9] - **Uranium (Z = 92)** is the **heaviest naturally occurring** element.
[1:10] # Isotopes, isobars, isotones
[1:11] TABLE head: Term | Same | Different | Example
  [1:11.0] Isotope | Atomic number (protons) | Mass number (neutrons) | ¹H, ²H, ³H; ¹⁶O, ¹⁷O, ¹⁸O
  [1:11.1] Isobar | Mass number | Atomic number | ³H and ³He
  [1:11.2] Isotone (isoneutronic) | Number of neutrons (A − Z) | Atomic and mass numbers | ³H and ⁴He (2 each); ¹⁴C, ¹⁵N, ¹⁶O (8 each)
[1:12] - Isotopes have the **same chemical properties** (same electrons) but different physical properties (mass).
[1:13] - Hydrogen isotopes: **protium** (no neutron), **deuterium** (1 neutron, "heavy hydrogen"), **tritium** (2 neutrons, radioactive, made by hitting **lithium** with neutrons) – isotopes of **hydrogen**, not carbon.
[1:14] - **Heavy water (D₂O):** **deuterium** (an isotope of **hydrogen**) takes the place of ordinary hydrogen; discovered by **H.C. Urey** (Nobel Chemistry 1934).
[1:15] - **Helium nucleus** = **2 protons + 2 neutrons** (= the alpha particle).

## SUB 2 | Nuclear Fuels – Fissile vs Fertile, Enrichment, What Is Not a Fuel
[2:0] TABLE head: Isotope | Type | Key fact
  [2:0.0] U-235 | Fissile | Only 0.7% of natural uranium; the isotope that powers reactors and fuels bombs
  [2:0.1] U-238 | Fertile | 99.3% of natural uranium; absorbs a neutron → Pu-239
  [2:0.2] Pu-239 | Fissile | Not natural; bred from U-238 – a "good nuclear fuel"
  [2:0.3] Th-232 | Fertile | Th-232 + neutron → fissile U-233
  [2:0.4] U-233 | Fissile | Fuel of Stage III and of KAMINI
[2:1] - **Uranium** is the main fuel of nuclear reactors (the metal "used for producing electricity"); **thorium** is usable only after breeding into U-233.
[2:2] - India's PHWRs burn **natural uranium** (unenriched; mostly U-238 with 0.7% U-235).
[2:3] - **Enrichment** = raising the share of **U-235** (not U-238) by **isotope separation**; most common method: **gas centrifuge** (uranium as UF₆; earlier gaseous diffusion).
[2:4] TABLE head: Use | U-235 level
  [2:4.0] Light-water power reactors | 3–5%
  [2:4.1] Research / some naval reactors | Up to 20%
  [2:4.2] Weapons-grade | About 90% (far above 20%)
[2:5] # Not a nuclear fuel
[2:6] - **Moderators, coolants, absorbers:** graphite, heavy water, sodium, **boron, cadmium**, beryllium oxide (moderator).
[2:7] - **Zirconium** (alloy cladding of fuel rods – corrosion-resistant, absorbs very few neutrons) is "essential for the construction of reactors"; it is **not radioactive**.
[2:8] - Also not fuels: **helium** (inert gas), **lead**, **calcium**, **chromium** (not linked to atomic power), radium (radioactive but not a reactor fuel). **Monazite** (thorium ore), thorium and **beryllium** are linked to atomic power.
[2:9] - "Good nuclear fuel" among U-238, Pu-239, neptunium, "thorium-236": **Pu-239** (fissile); U-238 is only fertile (APPSC 2007).
[2:10] > Exam angle: "Which is NOT a fuel?" – cadmium, lead, calcium, helium, chromium; "uranium isotope used in power plants" – U-235; "thorium + neutrons" – U-233 (APPSC Group 1 2002, repeated).

## SUB 3 | Radioactivity – Alpha, Beta, Gamma and Half-life
[3:0] - Discovered by **Henri Becquerel (1896)** in uranium; Marie and Pierre Curie found polonium and radium (1898). Radioactivity is a **nuclear** (not chemical) property; a radioactive substance emits **alpha, beta and gamma** rays. Measured by the **Geiger (Geiger–Müller) counter**.
[3:1] TABLE head: Emission | Nature | Charge | Stopped by
  [3:1.0] Alpha | Helium nucleus (2p + 2n); mass ≈ helium nucleus/atom | +2 (positive) | Paper or skin – least penetrating
  [3:1.1] Beta | Electron (β⁻) or positron (β⁺) emitted by the nucleus | −1 (negative) / +1 | Few mm of aluminium
  [3:1.2] Gamma | Electromagnetic radiation (photons) | Zero | Lead or concrete – most penetrating
[3:2] - Penetrating power (same energy): **α < β < γ**.
[3:3] - **β⁻ emission:** a neutron turns into a proton + electron → the atom gains **one proton** (Z rises by 1).
[3:4] - **Zero charge:** neutron, **neutrino**, gamma. **Deuteron** (a proton + neutron nucleus) is not a sub-atomic particle in the usual list.
[3:5] - **Half-life** = time for half the nuclei to decay; a substance never decays "completely".
[3:6] TABLE head: Half-life | After | Remaining | Decayed
  [3:6.0] 10 days | 20 days (2 half-lives) | 1/4 | 3/4
  [3:6.1] 5 years | 20 years (4 half-lives) | 1/16 | 15/16
[3:7] - Natural decay series: **uranium** (U-238 → Pb-206), **thorium** (Th-232 → **Pb-208**), **actinium** (U-235 → Pb-207); **neptunium series = artificial**; there is **no "carbon series"**. Lead is the stable **end product of uranium decay**.
[3:8] - Elements above Z = 82–83 are radioactive (plus technetium 43, promethium 61); **astatine, francium, tritium** are radioactive, **zirconium (Z = 40) is not**.
[3:9] - **Xenon-135** = "nuclear poison" – a fission product that absorbs neutrons.
[3:10] > Exam angle: charge of α/β/γ, half-life arithmetic (RAS 2007 repeated), "not radioactive" (zirconium, IAS 2001 and 2007).
[3:11] ~~ Sources: PMF S&T Part 4 p.29-31, 37 (types of radiation, Becquerel, half-life); NextGen S&T Group 2 Part 4 p.20-21, 23; Rocket Chemistry p.40-41; app notes 5:61 sub 2
## SUB 4 | Reactor Components and Reactor Types
[4:0] TABLE head: Part | Function | Substances
  [4:0.0] Fuel | Fissile material | U-235, Pu-239, U-233
  [4:0.1] Moderator | Slows fast neutrons to thermal speed so U-235 can fission (does not absorb them or stop the reaction) | Heavy water (D₂O), graphite, ordinary (light) water, beryllium oxide
  [4:0.2] Control rods | Absorb neutrons, control the rate | Cadmium, boron, hafnium, silver-indium
  [4:0.3] Coolant | Transfers heat from the core to make steam | Heavy water, light water, CO₂, helium, liquid sodium
  [4:0.4] Reactor core | Where fuel, moderator and control rods are located | –
[4:1] - Without control rods the chain reaction goes **out of control**; without a moderator a thermal reactor stops.
[4:2] - **Heavy water** is chiefly a **moderator**; in PHWRs it is **both moderator and coolant**. **Ordinary water** is also both in PWRs/BWRs.
[4:3] TABLE head: Reactor | Fuel | Moderator | Coolant | Example
  [4:3.0] PHWR | Natural uranium | Heavy water | Heavy water | Rajasthan (first), Kaiga, Kakrapar, Narora – Stage I
  [4:3.1] BWR | Enriched uranium | Light water | Light water (boils in core) | Tarapur units 1-2 (India's first, 1969)
  [4:3.2] PWR / VVER | Low-enriched uranium | Light water | Pressurised light water | Kudankulam
  [4:3.3] Gas-cooled (Magnox) | Natural uranium | Graphite | CO₂ | UK
  [4:3.4] FBR | MOX (Pu-239 + U-238) | None | Liquid sodium | FBTR, PFBR – Kalpakkam
  [4:3.5] AHWR | Th-232 + U-233 / Pu | Heavy water | Boiling light water | BARC design
[4:4] - **Light-water reactors** (light water as moderator and coolant) make up **over 85%** of world capacity; PWR alone about 70% of reactors.
[4:5] - **May 2017:** Union Cabinet approved **10 indigenous 700 MWe PHWRs** in "fleet mode".
[4:6] > Exam angle: "function of heavy water / moderator" (UPSC 2011 ×2, IAS 2011) = slow down neutrons; "if control rods are not used" = chain reaction goes out of control (RAS 1992 ×2).

## SUB 5 | Fast Breeder Reactors – FBTR, PFBR, IGCAR and BHAVINI
[5:0] - **Breeder reactor:** produces **more fissile material than it burns**; electricity still comes from **fission**. Fast neutrons (no moderator) turn the **U-238 blanket** into **Pu-239** (and Th-232 into U-233); **Pu-239 fissions** to give energy.
[5:1] - **No moderator; liquid sodium coolant**; fuel MOX from reprocessed Stage I spent fuel. FBRs are **Stage II** (not Stage I).
[5:2] # FBTR (Fast Breeder Test Reactor)
[5:3] - At **IGCAR, Kalpakkam (Tamil Nadu)**; critical **18 October 1985**; design based on French **Rapsodie**.
[5:4] - Coolant **liquid sodium**; fuel indigenous **plutonium-uranium mixed carbide** (plutonium-rich).
[5:5] - **2005** milestone: IGCAR reprocessed FBTR's mixed-carbide fuel.
[5:6] # PFBR, Kalpakkam
[5:7] TABLE head: Item | Fact
  [5:7.0] Capacity | 500 MWe (1,250 MWt); sodium-cooled, pool type
  [5:7.1] Designed by | IGCAR (Indira Gandhi Centre for Atomic Research, Kalpakkam, est. 1971 as Reactor Research Centre)
  [5:7.2] Built and operated by | BHAVINI (Bharatiya Nabhikiya Vidyut Nigam Ltd, PSU of DAE, 2003, HQ Kalpakkam)
  [5:7.3] First criticality | 6 April 2026 → India enters Stage II
  [5:7.4] Delay | Cost doubled; about 16 years late
[5:8] - PFBR = India's **first indigenous FBR**; India the **2nd country after Russia** to run a commercial-scale fast breeder. Next: FBR-600 / 2 × 500 MWe units at Kalpakkam.
[5:9] > ⚠ "Who developed / built PFBR": APPSC keys **BHAVINI** (builder, operator); IGCAR is the designer – read the verb.

## SUB 6 | Three-stage Programme, Thorium and the AHWR
[6:0] - **Homi J. Bhabha** – "father of the Indian nuclear programme"; 1950s plan to use **abundant thorium** against **scarce uranium**; aim: self-reliance through a **closed fuel cycle**.
[6:1] TABLE head: Stage | Reactor | Fuel | Output
  [6:1.0] I | PHWR (D₂O moderator + coolant) | Natural uranium | Power + Pu-239 in spent fuel
  [6:1.1] II | FBR (liquid sodium, no moderator) | MOX: Pu-239 + U-238 | Breeds more Pu-239; Th-232 blanket → U-233
  [6:1.2] III | AHWR / thorium reactors | Th-232 + U-233 | Large-scale thorium use
[6:2] - **Thorium:** India holds about **25%** of world thorium (3rd largest) but only about **2%** of uranium – so thorium is the element with **large reserves in India**.
[6:3] - **Thorium over uranium** (UPSC 2012): far **more abundant**, **more energy per unit mass mined**, **less harmful (long-lived) waste** – all three.
[6:4] - **Monazite** = principal thorium ore (also rare earths – lanthanum, cerium); resources about **13.15 million tonnes** (DAE); beach sands of **Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Odisha, Andhra Pradesh** – not the entire coast; only **IREL** may process and export it.
[6:5] # AHWR (Advanced Heavy Water Reactor)
[6:6] - **300 MWe**, designed by **BARC**; **heavy-water moderated, boiling light-water cooled**; fuel (Th-U-233) MOX and (Th-Pu) MOX – so it also burns **plutonium-bearing** fuel; **not yet built**.
[6:7] - A **bridge from Stage I to Stage III** for large-scale thorium use.
[6:8] - **ANEEL fuel** (thorium + **HALEU**, US firm Clean Core Thorium Energy, May 2026) is usable in existing PHWRs.
[6:9] - Stage III hurdles: thin fissile inventory, long builds – **not** lack of thorium.
[6:10] # Uranium mines
[6:11] TABLE head: Mine | Location
  [6:11.0] Jaduguda | East Singhbhum, Jharkhand – India's first uranium mine; UCIL seat
  [6:11.1] Tummalapalle | YSR Kadapa, AP – UCIL mine and processing plant
[6:12] - About **80%** of imported uranium comes from **Kazakhstan** (Dec 2025).

## SUB 8 | Fusion Research – Tokamak, ITER and IPR
[8:0] - **Fusion reactors** work on the **fusion of light nuclei** (not fission of heavy nuclei), usually in a **tokamak** design, at **very high temperature** (UPPSC RO/ARO 2016: ii and iii).
[8:1] - Plasma confinement: **magnetic** (tokamak) or **inertial** (lasers on fuel pellets).
[8:2] - **ITER:** **Cadarache, southern France**; tokamak, world's largest magnetic-confinement experiment; members **EU + India, China, Japan, South Korea, Russia, USA** – **Canada is not a member**; India joined **2005** and supplies the **cryostat**; about **500 MW out from 50 MW in**.
[8:3] - **Institute for Plasma Research, Gandhinagar** (not BARC): **Aditya** (first Indian tokamak) and **SST-1**.
[8:4] - **December 2022:** first fusion ignition with net energy gain – **National Ignition Facility, Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory, USA**, using lasers.
