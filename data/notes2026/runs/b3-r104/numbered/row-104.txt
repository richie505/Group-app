## SUB 0 | Meaning and Purpose of Deficit Financing
[0:0] # Meaning
[0:1] TABLE head: Usage | Meaning
  [0:1.0] Indian | Budget gap met by borrowing from the RBI; RBI creates new money – the government "buys money" from the RBI
  [0:1.1] Western | Any financing of a budget deficit, mainly borrowing from the public/market
  [0:1.2] Effect | Increase in money supply; creates extra paper currency
[0:2] - **Deficit financing** = the act of financing a **deficit budget** – **spending in excess of revenue** (total expenditure > total receipts) and sustaining that gap (APPSC G1 1999).
[0:3] - In India, deficit financing = borrowing from the **RBI** (money creation), not from the public (APPSC AD Economics).
[0:4] - **Fiscal deficit** (Union Budget) = budgetary deficit + net increase in internal and external borrowings and other liabilities.
[0:5] - An economic unit that **spends more than it receives** in a period is a **deficit unit** (APPSC Degree Lecturers 2017).
[0:6] # Purpose in Indian Planning
[0:7] - Purpose: **raising resources for economic development** – not redemption of public debt, BoP adjustment or cutting foreign debt (UPSC 2013 ×2); a "pragmatic tool" to finance the **Five Year Plans**.
[0:8] - **Small doses** of deficit financing for productive investment need not be inflationary, and it is an **often-used tool** for financing budget deficits (CDS 2011: both true).
[0:9] - Needed after 1947 because of a **low tax base**, poor compliance, weak private banking and **low domestic savings** (not a high savings rate).
[0:10] - NextGen objectives: remedy for depression, raise aggregate demand, subsidies, anti-poverty programmes, PSU losses.
[0:11] - Independence to **late 1960s**: little use, to fund plans. **Second Plan**: about **₹1,100 crore** against ₹1,200 crore planned. **Third Plan**: reliance to be **strictly limited**.
[0:12] - **1970s onwards** (NextGen: "period of deficit financing 1970-1991"): heavy reliance (oil shocks, welfare spending) led to debt and inflation.
[0:13] > Exam angle: "In India deficit financing is used for" = **economic development**.

## SUB 1 | Methods and Their Inflationary Effect
[1:0] # Methods (NextGen)
[1:1] - Running down **cash balances** with the RBI; **issue of new currency**; **borrowing from the RBI** (RBI prints notes); also internal and external borrowing, external aid and grants.
[1:2] - Ramesh Singh's order of preference: external aid/grants → external borrowing → internal borrowing → **printing currency (last resort, most inflationary)**.
[1:3] - **Monetised deficit**: borrowing from the RBI through fresh currency (**high-powered money**); the FRBM Act disallows it in normal times.
[1:4] # Inflationary Ranking
[1:5] TABLE head: Mode | Effect
  [1:5.0] Creation of new money | Most inflationary (UPSC 2013, 2021; State PSC)
  [1:5.1] Borrowing from banks | High
  [1:5.2] Borrowing from public | Lower – uses existing savings
  [1:5.3] Repayment of public debt | Not inflationary – withdraws money
[1:6] - Deficit financing is **inflationary in character** (APPSC G1); it **increases the money supply** (UPPSC 2016); if it fails to raise output it **generates inflation**, not deflation (UPPSC 1993).
[1:7] # Control and Phase-out
[1:8] TABLE head: Year | Step
  [1:8.0] Mid-1950s | Ad hoc Treasury Bills – automatic RBI financing of the government begins
  [1:8.1] Sep 1994 | RBI-Government agreement begins phasing out ad hoc T-bills (₹6,000 crore cap for 1994-95)
  [1:8.2] 31 Mar 1997 | Ad hoc T-bills abolished; Ways and Means Advances (WMA) from 1 April 1997
  [1:8.3] 2003 | FRBM Act
  [1:8.4] 1 Apr 2006 | RBI barred from subscribing to primary issues of G-Secs
  [1:8.5] 2018 | Monetised borrowing only on escape-clause grounds; deviation cap 0.5% of GDP a year
[1:9] - Market borrowing; liquidity is managed by **OMOs**: **sale** of G-Secs **drains** liquidity, **purchase adds** it.
[1:10] - **15 Sep 2026**: RBI announced **OMO sales of ₹1 lakh crore** with 91/182/364-day T-bill auctions.

## SUB 2 | Consequences – Inflation, Debt Trap, Crowding Out
[2:0] TABLE head: Effect | Mechanism
  [2:0.0] Demand-pull inflation | Money supply and demand rise without more goods and services; hurts the poor most
  [2:0.1] Crowding out | Market borrowing raises interest rates, cuts private investment
  [2:0.2] Debt trap | Government borrows to make interest payments (APPSC G2); little room for health, education
  [2:0.3] Others (NextGen) | Adverse effect on savings and investment, rising inequality, BoP deficit, higher cost of production
  [2:0.4] Rating downgrades | Higher borrowing cost for government and firms
[2:1] # Public Debt Heads
[2:2] TABLE head: Head | Items
  [2:2.0] Internal debt | Market loans, Treasury Bills, special securities to NSSF
  [2:2.1] Other liabilities (Public Account) | Small savings, State Provident Funds, reserve funds and deposits
  [2:2.2] External debt | Loans from foreign sources
[2:3] # Current Data
[2:4] - Interest = **20 paise** of every rupee spent (Budget 2026-27); about **40% of revenue receipts**.
[2:5] - States borrowed **₹5.23 trillion** till Oct 2025 (**62%** of FY26 plan); RBI wants a shift from **subsidies to capital expenditure**.
[2:6] - **AP** fiscal deficit 2024-25: **5.05%** of GSDP vs 4% limit; **market loans ≈ 75% of AP's debt** – after bifurcation AP met its heavy deficit mainly through **market loans** (APPSC AD Economics).
