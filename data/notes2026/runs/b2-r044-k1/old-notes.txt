## SUB 2 | High Court Judges
[44:2:0] # Appointment and Qualifications
[44:2:1] - **Chief Justice**: by the **President** after consulting the **CJI and Governor**.
[44:2:2] - **Other judges**: after consulting CJI, Governor and **HC Chief Justice**; for a common HC, **Governors of all States** concerned.
[44:2:3] - Appointed by **warrant**; the **Governor cannot appoint** or remove.
[44:2:4] - **Qualifications**: citizen; **10 years judicial office** or **10 years HC advocate**; **no distinguished jurist** route (only SC, Art. 124(3)(c)).
[44:2:5] - Collegium: **HC CJ + 2** senior-most HC judges → **CJI + 2** senior-most SC judges.
[44:2:6] - Sanctioned strength (18 Sep 2025): **1,122** for all HCs, **804** filled. **8 Aug 2026**: 30 HC judges appointed (Madras 15, Karnataka 6, Calcutta, MP 1).
[44:2:7] # Tenure, Transfer, Removal
[44:2:8] - Retire at **62** – raised from **60** by the **15th Amendment, 1963** (R explains A); **114th Amendment Bill (2010)** sought 65.
[44:2:9] - **Resignation** to the **President** (Art. 217(1)(a)).
[44:2:10] - **Art. 222**: **President** transfers HC judges after consulting the **CJI** – only HC judges; **no provision to transfer SC judges**.
[44:2:11] - **Justice H.R. Khanna (80th Report, 8th Law Commission)**: at least one-third of HC judges from another State.
[44:2:12] - **Removal**: Art. 217(1)(b) / **218** with Art. 124(4) and **Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968** – President's order after Parliament's address.

## SUB 3 | High Court Powers
[44:3:0] - **Art. 226**: writs for **FRs and other legal rights** ("any other purpose") – **wider than Art. 32**; discretionary; can reach outside its territory if the **cause of action** arises within. Art. 32 itself is an FR (R does not explain A).
[44:3:1] - **Art. 227**: administrative and judicial **superintendence** over all subordinate courts and tribunals; may act **suo motu**; **227(4)** – not over Armed Forces courts/tribunals. SC has no Art. 227-type power.
[44:3:2] - **Appellate**: second civil appeals only on a **substantial question of law**; Sessions Court **death sentences** need HC confirmation (**BNSS 2023**, replaced CrPC from **1 July 2024**).
[44:3:3] - **HC Chief Justice**: dual capacity; administrative acts open to **writs under Art. 226** before another judge of the same HC – no absolute immunity.
[44:3:4] # Court of Record and Contempt
[44:3:5] - Records have **evidentiary value**, cannot be questioned in a lower court; power to punish **contempt** (Arts **129 / 215**).
[44:3:6] - **Contempt of Court Act, 1926** – first major law; **H.N. Sanyal Committee (1961)** → **Contempt of Courts Act, 1971**: **civil** = wilful disobedience; **criminal** = scandalising or obstructing justice. Aim – public confidence (R explains A).
[44:3:7] - Jul 2026: SC (CJI Surya Kant bench) shifted "bulldozer justice" contempt pleas to HCs. Sep 2026: SC refused to stay Bombay HC contempt proceedings over a judicial officer's outburst.
[44:3:8] - Notable: **Uttarakhand HC** declared the entire animal kingdom legal entities; Madras HC – RTI applicant must state object of request.
[44:3:9] - **Patna HC (30 April 2004):** persons in **jail or police custody cannot contest** elections (Jan Chaukidar petition); upheld by the SC in **2013**, then undone by an **RPA amendment (2013)**.

## SUB 6 | Judicial Review – Meaning, Origin, Theory
[44:6:0] - Power of the SC and HCs to test **laws and executive orders** against the Constitution and declare them **void**; not a judgment on policy wisdom, not an appeal.
[44:6:1] - The term is **not used in the Constitution**; part of the **basic structure** (**Kesavananda 1973**; **L. Chandra Kumar 1997**).
[44:6:2] - **Origin USA**: **Marbury v. Madison (1803)**, CJ **John Marshall**; roots in **Sir Edward Coke** vs King James I. Exists in Canada, Australia; **UK** – parliamentary sovereignty, no voiding of Acts.
[44:6:3] - FRs amendable first held in **Shankari Prasad**.
[44:6:4] - Basis: written Constitution + **federalism** + FRs. **Symbol of federal government** (APPSC key) – courts as umpire of the Centre-State division. **Theory**: **supremacy of the Constitution** / limited government.
[44:6:5] - India = **American judicial supremacy + British parliamentary sovereignty**; judicial review **limits Parliament's sovereignty** – laws cannot break the Basic Structure.
[44:6:6] - Grounds: violation of FRs; lack of **legislative competence**; contravening the Constitution / Basic Structure – not disagreement with majority policy.

## SUB 8 | Limits, Amendments and Overcoming A Judgment
[44:8:0] - **Narrower than USA**: Art. 21 "**procedure established by law**" vs US "**due process of law**" (US SC = "third chamber").
[44:8:1] - **Political questions** avoided; writs refused where an alternative remedy exists; delimitation review limited but possible if capricious (**Kishorchandra Chhanganlal Rathod, 2024**).
[44:8:2] TABLE head: Amendment | Effect
  [44:8:2.0] 1st (1951) | After land-reform / reservation rulings; created the Ninth Schedule (assent 18 June 1951)
  [44:8:2.1] 38th (1975) | President's / Governor's satisfaction under Arts 123, 213, 352 non-justiciable
  [44:8:2.2] 39th (1975) | Art. 329A shielded PM's election – clause (4) struck down in Indira Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975) as violating basic structure
  [44:8:2.3] 42nd (1976) | Barred HCs from reviewing central laws
  [44:8:2.4] 43rd (1977) | Restored that power
  [44:8:2.5] 44th (1978) | Reversed the 38th bar
[44:8:3] 42nd barred → 43rd restored; 38th barred → 44th reversed.
[44:8:4] # Overcoming a Judgment
[44:8:5] - Legislature **cannot simply overrule** a judgment. Options:
[44:8:6] - 1. **Ordinary / validating law** removing the basis of the judgment (retrospectively if needed).
[44:8:7] - 2. **Constitutional amendment** – subject to the Basic Structure.
[44:8:8] - 3. **Review petition** (Art. 137); **curative petition** as last resort.
[44:8:9] - Example: **Tribunals Reforms Act 2021** re-enacted struck-down conditions → struck down again in **Madras Bar Association (2025)**.
## SUB 9 | Judicial Activism and Pil
[44:9:0] # Terms
[44:9:1] TABLE head: Term | Meaning
  [44:9:1.0] Judicial activism (= judicial dynamism) | Wider intervention shaping policy; opposite of restraint
  [44:9:1.1] PIL (APPSC 2019) | Legal action for public / general interest in which the public or a class has a pecuniary or other interest
  [44:9:1.2] Social Action Litigation | Other name of PIL – outcome of judicial activism, not restraint (APPSC-G2 2023)
  [44:9:1.3] Locus standi relaxed | Any public-spirited citizen can move court
  [44:9:1.4] Epistolary jurisdiction | Letters, postcards, newspaper reports as petitions
[44:9:2] - PIL under **Art. 32 (SC)** or **Art. 226 (HC)**; in line with **Art. 39A**; court-made.
[44:9:3] # Origin and Stages
[44:9:4] - **Origin USA** (APPSC-G2 2025); term coined **1947** by **Arthur Schlesinger Jr.**; restraint also developed in the USA.
[44:9:5] - India: **mid-1970s, post-Emergency**, on the SC's initiative – **V.R. Krishna Iyer, P.N. Bhagwati** (pioneers), with O. Chinnappa Reddy, D.A. Desai.
[44:9:6] - **First PIL**: **Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar (1979)** – speedy trial for undertrials; filed by **Kapila Hingorani ("Mother of PILs")**.
[44:9:7] - PIL era under CJI **Y.V. Chandrachud (1978-85)**; CJI keyed at PIL's introduction – **P.N. Bhagwati** (CJI from 1985).
[44:9:8] TABLE head: Stage | Feature
  [44:9:8.0] Mid-1970s | Open access; activism the "new feature"
  [44:9:8.1] 1979-1980s | PIL; Art. 21 expanded – livelihood, education, health, environment
  [44:9:8.2] 1990s on | Governance – CVC, 2G, coal blocks, candidates' affidavits
  [44:9:8.3] 2017 | Puttaswamy – privacy a fundamental right
[44:9:9] # Instruments and Doctrines
[44:9:10] - **Suo motu cognisance**; **continuing mandamus** (case kept open); **guidelines** filling a vacuum (**Vishaka 1997** → POSH Act 2013); **Art. 142**; court-appointed **expert committees**.
[44:9:11] - Genuine SC evolutions: **Basic Structure**, **PIL**, removal of handcuffs, continuing mandamus, expansion of Art. 21.
[44:9:12] - **Not** an SC evolution: "exemptions to FRs" (Arts **31A-31C, 33**) – constitutional text (APPSC 2017).

## SUB 10 | Judicial Overreach Debate
[44:10:0] TABLE head: Basis | Review / restraint | Activism
  [44:10:0.0] Nature | Interpretative, legalistic | Creative, proactive
  [44:10:0.1] Question | "Is the law valid?" | "Is the law just?"
[44:10:1] - **Overreach**: courts entering the executive / legislative domain → strains **separation of powers**.
[44:10:2] - Concerns: courts doing executive work, weak PIL enforcement, flood of letter petitions, **delays in other cases** (not reduction), PIL misuse; Art. 142 "complete justice" undefined, bypasses democratic scrutiny.
[44:10:3] - **Justice A.S. Anand**: activism should not become **judicial adventurism**. **Upendra Baxi**: courts should fill a vacuum, not replace democratic institutions – ideal **judicial prudence**.
[44:10:4] - Merits: wider access to justice, executive accountability. PIL rethink debate (May 2026) – misuse, not ending access.
[44:10:5] - **Mar 2026**: SC ban on an NCERT chapter on judicial delay and corruption criticised as censorship under Art. 19.
