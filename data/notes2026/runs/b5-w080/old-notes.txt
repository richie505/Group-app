## SUB 1 | Meaning and the Brundtland Definition
[80:1:0] # Brundtland Commission (1987)
[80:1:1] TABLE head: Particular | Fact
  [80:1:1.0] Formal name | World Commission on Environment and Development (WCED) – UN-mandated, set up in the early 1980s
  [80:1:1.1] Chair | Gro Harlem Brundtland, then Prime Minister of Norway ("Mother of Sustainable Development")
  [80:1:1.2] Report | "Our Common Future" = the Brundtland Report
  [80:1:1.3] Year | 1987
  [80:1:1.4] Contribution | First widely accepted (official) definition; popularised the term and linked economic development with environmental stability
[80:1:2] - **Definition:** development that "**meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs**" – given by the **WCED (1987)**, not UNEP, the World Bank, the IMF, UNCED 1992 or the Tbilisi Declaration (1977).
[80:1:3] - **Our Common Future on poverty:** "**Poverty is a major cause and effect of global environmental problems**" – so environment cannot be tackled without tackling poverty and inequality.
[80:1:4] - **Concepts in one line:** sustainable development = the definition above; Our Common Future = the 1987 report; **intergenerational equity** = fair sharing of resources between present and future generations.
[80:1:5] - Exam shorthand: "**improving the conditions of the present generation without affecting the needs of future generations**" = sustainable development exactly.
[80:1:6] > Most-asked (×20): who defined it / which report / which year → **Brundtland, Our Common Future, 1987**. Rio 1992 and Stockholm 1972 are the tempting wrong options.
[80:1:7] ~~ Sources: PMF Environment Part 6 p.6 (21.4.1 Brundtland Commission); NextGen Society Group 2 Part 2 p.10-11; NextGen Rocket Environment p.71; PMF Human & Economic Geography Part 2 p.45; en.wikisource.org Brundtland Report ch.1 (poverty sentence)
## SUB 2 | Principles, Nature and Scope
[80:2:0] TABLE head: Principle | Meaning
  [80:2:0.0] Intergenerational equity | Present must not deplete (natural) resources at the cost of future generations – "inter-generational sensibility" in the use of natural resources
  [80:2:0.1] Carrying capacity | Human use within what ecosystems can support – the Brundtland idea is intertwined with carrying capacity (UPSC 2010)
  [80:2:0.2] Precautionary principle | Lack of full scientific certainty is no reason to postpone measures against harm
  [80:2:0.3] Polluter pays | The polluter (person or company) bears the cost of preventing or remedying the damage – not the government or public
  [80:2:0.4] CBDR | Shared duty; developed nations carry more
  [80:2:0.5] Integration | Environment and economy are integrated, not opposites
[80:2:1] - **NOT a principle:** over-exploitation of resources, growth at the cost of environment, more pollution for industry.
[80:2:2] - **NCERT principles:** respect all life, improve quality of life, **conserve the earth's vitality and diversity**, **minimise depletion of natural resources**, change attitudes, let communities care for their own environment.
[80:2:3] - **Measures:** renewables, recycling, lower and more efficient energy use; all pollution minimised; consumption must **not exceed** the rate of regeneration.
[80:2:4] - **Challenges:** **over-consumption**; **increased use of fossil fuels** is a negative impact of technology.
[80:2:5] - **Education** helps by **promoting environmental awareness**; communication for SD aims at **transformative learning**.
[80:2:6] - **Environmental sustainability needs participatory, open and democratic decision-making**; **environmental protection** maintains (and restores) a healthy natural environment and **is not a luxury** at today's level of development.
[80:2:7] - SD includes a **constant or rising stock of capital assets**; natural **climatic change over time** is not part of the concept.
[80:2:8] - **Scope includes** meeting present needs, **equity within and across generations**, inclusion ("leave no one behind"); **excludes** growth that runs down natural capital and treating environment and development as opposites.
[80:2:9] - **Poverty alleviation** is printed as "not related to environmental protection" (UP 2013 key).
[80:2:10] ~~ Sources: PMF Environment Part 6 p.13-14 (carrying capacity); PMF Environment Part 4 p.48 (polluter pays); NCERT Human Geography (OnlyIAS) Part 10 p.25; NextGen Society Group 2 Part 2 p.10; app notes 5:80 sub 5-6
[80:2:11] - **Principle stressed by international conventions:** **reducing greenhouse-gas emissions** for environmental sustainability (not more fossil fuels or present needs before future ones).
[80:2:12] - **Rules of sustainability:** harvest of renewables must not exceed regeneration (sustainable yield); non-renewables need **renewable substitutes**; waste must stay **within** the environment's assimilative capacity.
## SUB 3 | 2030 Agenda and SDGs – Basics
[80:3:0] - **17 SDGs, 169 targets, 232 indicators**; adopted by **all 193 UN member states** in **September 2015**; in force **1 Jan 2016**; target year **2030**.
[80:3:1] - **Not legally binding** and not a single development model; they **replaced the MDGs (2000-2015)**.
[80:3:2] - **Five Ps:** **People, Planet, Prosperity, Peace, Partnership** (not Patriotism).
[80:3:3] - **Dimensions:** **economic, social, environmental**.
[80:3:4] - **Goal 1:** "**End poverty in all its forms everywhere**" – no 50%/75% target.
[80:3:5] - Aim: get rid of poverty, protect the environment and ensure peace and prosperity for all; scope covers goals like life below water, decent work and cultural diversity (target 4.7) – **not political alliances**.
[80:3:6] - Biggest hurdle for poverty eradication: **economic inequality and unequal distribution of resources**.
[80:3:7] - **UNDP** is the UN development agency that leads SDG support work in countries; in India **NITI Aayog** coordinates.
[80:3:8] - **Andhra Pradesh** adopted **all 17 SDGs** with **212 indicators** to monitor progress to 2030; SDG targets are built into **Swarna Andhra @2047**.
[80:3:9] # Sustainable Development Report (UN SDSN)
[80:3:10] - **SDR 2025:** India **99th of 167** – first time in the top 100.
[80:3:11] - **SDR 2026:** India **94th of 169** (best ever); **Finland** first, Sweden and Denmark next.
[80:3:12] > Detail on each goal and the SDG India Index: see row 81.

## SUB 4 | Rio Earth Summit 1992 and Agenda 21
[80:4:0] # UNCED – the first Earth Summit
[80:4:1] - **UNCED = United Nations Conference on Environment and Development** ("Earth Summit"), **Rio de Janeiro, Brazil, 3-14 June 1992**; 20th anniversary of Stockholm 1972; about **178-179 countries**.
[80:4:2] - **Outcomes:** **Agenda 21**, **Rio Declaration** (**27 principles**), **Forest Principles** (non-binding); legally binding conventions opened for signature: **UNFCCC** and **CBD** (UNCCD followed in 1994).
[80:4:3] - **UNFCCC** was negotiated in New York and **drawn/opened for signature at UNCED, Rio 1992** – not at Stockholm 1972, WSSD 2002 or Copenhagen 2009.
[80:4:4] # Agenda 21
[80:4:5] TABLE head: Point | Fact
  [80:4:5.0] What | Comprehensive (non-binding) global action plan for sustainable development – globally, nationally, locally, wherever humans affect the environment
  [80:4:5.1] Origin | UNCED, Rio 1992 – NOT the Johannesburg WSSD 2002 (UPSC 2016: statement 1 only)
  [80:4:5.2] "21" | 21st century
  [80:4:5.3] Key objective | Every local government should draw its own Local Agenda 21 (LA21)
  [80:4:5.4] Sample programme area | Ch.2: "Promoting sustainable development through trade"; Ch.4 is *changing* (not maintaining) consumption patterns
  [80:4:5.5] Aim | Combat environmental damage, poverty and disease through global co-operation
[80:4:6] - Agenda 21 is not a law that each country must enact, and not a "20th-century only" target.
[80:4:7] > Rio 1992 is the "first Earth Summit" (not Washington, Geneva or Buenos Aires); MPPSC 1992 printed "UN Conference on Sustainable Development" for Rio – formally the 1992 meeting was UNCED.
[80:4:8] ~~ Sources: NextGen Rocket Environment p.2, p.6, p.16, p.71; NextGen Environment APPSC Part 8.1 p.11; Shankar Environment 21 (OCR) p.23-24; PMF Environment Part 6 p.3, p.6-7, p.14; NCERT Human Geography (OnlyIAS) Part 10 p.25; static.un.org Agenda 21 ch.2
## SUB 5 | Three Pillars and Types of Sustainability
[80:5:0] TABLE head: Pillar | Aim
  [80:5:0.0] Economic | Growth that lasts – NOT profit maximisation that ignores social or environmental impact
  [80:5:0.1] Social | Social equity / equality, inclusion, community development
  [80:5:0.2] Environmental | Reduce waste, conserve natural resources, protect ecosystems
[80:5:1] - UN wording: **economic growth, environmental protection, social equity** – interlinked; SD's key goal is to ensure all three together.
[80:5:2] - **Triple bottom line:** **People, Planet, Profit**.
[80:5:3] - **NOT a pillar:** political, military, cultural, technological, legal or financial.
[80:5:4] - "Social sustainability focuses exclusively on growth and jobs" ✗; "economic sustainability = maximising profits" ✗ (A false, R true).
[80:5:5] - **Sustainability** broadly = programmes and actions to preserve a particular resource; SD is achieved by social and economic improvement that protects the environment and supports equality (A/R: both true, R not the explanation).
[80:5:6] ~~ Sources: NextGen Society Group 2 Part 2 p.10 (parameters of SD); PMF Environment Part 6 p.6; app notes 5:80 sub 4
[80:5:7] - **Strategy → example:** renewable energy – **solar power**; sustainable agriculture – **crop rotation**; waste reduction – **reduce, reuse, recycle**.

## SUB 7 | Measuring Sustainability – Natural Capital and Footprints
[80:7:0] TABLE head: Measure | Meaning
  [80:7:0.0] Natural capital | Resources created by nature – air, water, land, minerals, forests; roads are NOT (man-made)
  [80:7:0.1] Ecological footprint | Biologically productive land and sea area needed to supply a person's/population's consumption and absorb its wastes; unit global hectare
  [80:7:0.2] Earth Overshoot Day | Date when humanity's yearly demand exceeds what Earth can regenerate – a sign of environmental degradation; calculated by Global Footprint Network; conceived by Andrew Simms (New Economics Foundation), first campaign 2006
  [80:7:0.3] Green GDP | GDP – net natural capital consumption (depletion + degradation)
  [80:7:0.4] Genuine Progress Indicator | Adds unpaid work; subtracts pollution, crime, resource loss
[80:7:1] - Humanity's footprint ≈ **1.5 Earths**; India ≈ 0.9 (sustainable lifestyle).
[80:7:2] - Biota, biome and niche are not area measures.
[80:7:3] ~~ Sources: Shankar Environment 3 (OCR) p.16-17; Shankar Environment 19 (OCR) p.20; NextGen Rocket Environment p.43; PMF Environment Part 2 p.47-48; app notes 5:80 sub 7

=== NOTES 5:82 | Sustainable Development in Practice – Green Economy, Circular Economy and Esg
## SUB 0 | Green Economy, Green Technology and Green Skills
[82:0:0] # Green economy
[82:0:1] - **Green economy:** reduces environmental risks and ecological scarcities; **decouples growth from resource use and pollution**; **central theme of Rio+20 (2012)**.
[82:0:2] - **Green jobs:** work in agriculture, industry, services or administration that **preserves or restores environmental quality**.
[82:0:3] - **UNEP Green Economy Initiative:** **2008**. **PAGE** (Partnership for Action on Green Economy): **2013**, a response to the **Rio+20** call ("The Future We Want"); UNEP, ILO, UNIDO, UNDP, UNITAR.
[82:0:4] # Green technology
[82:0:5] - **Green technology** pollutes less, uses resources sustainably and recycles more of its waste.
[82:0:6] - **NOT correct:** "green technology is not expensive for developing countries" – **high cost is its main barrier**.
[82:0:7] - **CII–Sohrabji Godrej Green Business Centre:** India's first platinum-rated green building – **Hyderabad**.
[82:0:8] - **ISO 14001** = **Environmental Management System** (9001 quality, 22000 food safety, 27001 information security).
[82:0:9] # Green chemistry and cleaner production
[82:0:10] - **12 Principles of Green Chemistry:** **1998**, **Paul Anastas and John Warner**; core idea – **waste prevention rather than cleanup**; **Green Pharmacy** = green chemistry for medicines.
[82:0:11] - **Green chemistry + cleaner production** reduce waste and pollution across the **whole product life cycle** – hence they serve **EIA** and **Life-Cycle Assessment (LCA)**.
[82:0:12] # Green Skill Development Programme
[82:0:13] - Launched **2017** by **MoEFCC** on the **ENVIS** network; trains youth in **biodiversity conservation, pollution monitoring, waste management, forestry and wildlife** (not semiconductors).

## SUB 1 | Circular Economy and the 3 Rs
[82:1:0] - **Circular economy:** **reuse, recycling and repair** of materials to reduce waste; waste becomes a resource; replaces the linear **take–make–dispose** ("use and dispose") model.
[82:1:1] - **3 Rs (environment-friendly):** **Reduce, Reuse, Recycle**.
[82:1:2] TABLE head: Linear model | Circular model
  [82:1:2.0] Take, make, dispose | Reduce, reuse, repair, recycle; keep materials in use
  [82:1:2.1] Waste = end point | Waste = resource; growth decoupled from resource use
[82:1:3] - **Why it cuts GHG emissions (UPSC 2025):** it **reduces raw-material inputs** and **reduces production waste** – both true and both explain the emission cut.
[82:1:4] - **Not** exclusively about carbon or fossil fuels; **not** linear growth.
[82:1:5] - **India:** NITI Aayog + MoEFCC committees drew up action plans for priority waste streams – **plastics, e-waste, batteries, tyres, metals**.
[82:1:6] ~~ Sources: PMF Environment p.34 (circular economy of reduce, reuse, recycle); app notes 5:82 sub 1
## SUB 2 | European Green Deal
[82:2:0] - **December 2019**: make the EU **climate-neutral (net-zero GHG) by 2050**; at least **55% cut by 2030 vs 1990** – package **"Fit for 55"**.
[82:2:1] - **Poland** did not commit to the 2050 climate-neutrality target at the December 2019 summit.
[82:2:2] TABLE head: Component | Content
  [82:2:2.0] Farm to Fork | Sustainable food system
  [82:2:2.1] Circular Economy Action Plan | Resource efficiency, recycling
  [82:2:2.2] Biodiversity Strategy | Protect and restore ecosystems (so the Deal is NOT only about carbon)
  [82:2:2.3] Just Transition Fund | Support for affected regions
  [82:2:2.4] Renovation Wave | Energy-efficient buildings

## SUB 3 | Greenwashing, Green Finance and ESG
[82:3:0] # Greenwashing
[82:3:1] - **Greenwashing** ("green sheen"): **false or exaggerated claims / promises** that a product, firm or country is environment-friendly.
[82:3:2] - A **clear taxonomy** helps regulators curb **greenwashing and "transition washing"**.
[82:3:3] # Green finance

  [82:3:4.0] Green bond | Pioneer EIB 2007, World Bank 2008

## SUB 0 | Ozone Depletion – CFCs, Chlorine and Bromine
[83:0:0] # Cause
[83:0:1] - **CFCs (chlorofluorocarbons)** are the **main cause** of ozone depletion and of the "ozone hole" – not CO2, CO, methane, N2 or SO2.
[83:0:2] - **Full form:** CFC = **Chlorofluorocarbon** – carbon + chlorine + fluorine; entirely **man-made**, non-toxic, non-flammable.
[83:0:3] - **Mechanism:** under UV in the stratosphere CFCs release **chlorine**; **chlorine and bromine atoms destroy ozone catalytically** (one Cl atom breaks thousands of O3 molecules before it is locked up again) → more UV reaches the Earth.
[83:0:4] - **Bromine** comes from **halons** (fire extinguishers) – about **100 times more potent** than CFCs; **carbon tetrachloride** and **methyl chloroform** also release chlorine.
[83:0:5] - Depletion happens in the **stratosphere** (not in the lower atmosphere where humans live, not in the mesosphere).
[83:0:6] - **CFCs are also greenhouse gases** – they cause both warming and ozone loss (A-R: both true, but "CFCs are used in fridges, ACs, aerosols" is **not the explanation** of that).
[83:0:7] - **A–R (APPSC EO):** "ozone depletion = reduction of stratospheric ozone" ✓; "caused by high chlorine and bromine from CFCs and halons" ✓ → **R explains A**.
[83:0:8] - **Wrong statements trap (TSPSC JL):** ozone is in the **stratosphere, not mesosphere**; it is the **chlorine**, not the carbon, of CFCs that splits O3.
[83:0:9] # Sources of CFCs and related gases
[83:0:10] TABLE head: Gas | Main source
  [83:0:10.0] CFCs | Freon refrigerant, ACs, aerosol propellants, plastic foams, cleaning electronic parts
  [83:0:10.1] Halons | Fire extinguishers
  [83:0:10.2] Nitrogen oxides | Fertiliser plants, vehicles, combustion
  [83:0:10.3] Methane | Bacterial decomposition (wetlands, paddy, livestock)
[83:0:11] - **CFC uses (UPSC 2012):** **plastic foams, cleaning electronic components, aerosol pressurising agents** – **not tubeless tyres** → **1, 3 and 4**.
[83:0:12] > Recall angle (asked ~20 times: UPSC, UPPCS, UKPSC, MPPSC, APPSC G2, Forest, TSPSC): gas most responsible for ozone depletion = **CFC**.

[83:1:1] - **Montreal Protocol:** the action treaty under Vienna to **phase out production and consumption of ozone-depleting substances (ODS)** – not GHGs/global warming, acid rain, smog, DDT, petrol or marine life.

## SUB 2 | Ozone Layer, Dobson Unit and the Ozone Hole
[83:2:0] # Ozone layer
[83:2:1] - **Ozone = O3** (three oxygen atoms); formed when **UV radiation splits O2** (UV + O2 → O3).
[83:2:2] - About **90%** of ozone is in the **stratosphere** (**10–50 km**; main layer ~15–30 km) – **protective**, absorbs harmful **UV (UV-B)**; ~10% is in the troposphere, where it is a **pollutant**.
[83:2:3] - Ozone protects the biosphere from **ultraviolet rays** – not infra-red, X-rays or gamma rays.
[83:2:4] - **Natural regulation:** stratospheric ozone is naturally regulated by **nitrogen oxides – "nitrogen dioxide"** in books and keys (the NO/NO2 cycle; N2O from soils is the source gas).
[83:2:5] # Dobson Unit
[83:2:6] - **Dobson Unit (DU)** measures the **thickness/concentration of ozone in a column of air from the ground to the top of the atmosphere** – not decibel (sound), angstrom (length), knot, poise or Maxwell.
[83:2:7] - **1 DU = 0.01 mm** of pure ozone at **0°C and 1 atm**; an **"ozone hole"** = total column ozone **below 220 DU**.
[83:2:8] # Ozone hole
[83:2:9] - Most affected region: **Antarctica**; seasonal thinning in the **Southern Hemisphere spring (Aug–Nov)**.
[83:2:10] - **Polar stratospheric clouds** in extreme cold turn stable chlorine compounds into **active chlorine**, which destroys ozone when sunlight returns.
[83:2:11] - Ozone began to **drop sharply in the 1980s**; the Antarctic hole was **reported in 1985 by the British Antarctic Survey** (NextGen: first noticed 1983).
[83:2:12] - **Tibetan ozone low ("halo") 2005:** discovered by **G.W. Kent Moore**, University of Toronto.
[83:2:13] - **Recovery:** NASA/WMO report healing of the Antarctic hole, mainly due to the **ban on CFCs** under Montreal.
[83:2:14] TABLE head: Region | Back to 1980 levels
  [83:2:14.0] Most of the world | 2040
  [83:2:14.1] Arctic | 2045
  [83:2:14.2] Antarctic | 2066

## SUB 7 | UV Radiation – Effects of Ozone Loss
[83:7:0] TABLE head: Target | Effect of more UV-B
  [83:7:0.0] Humans | Skin cancer, eye cataracts, sunburn, immune suppression, DNA damage; 1% ozone loss raises harmful UV ~2%
  [83:7:0.1] Crops | Reduced growth and photosynthesis
  [83:7:0.2] Phytoplankton / marine | Damage to plankton and fish larvae (not more phytoplankton)
  [83:7:0.3] Materials | Degrades plastic, rubber, wood
[83:7:1] - UV bands: UV-A 315–400 nm, **UV-B 280–315 nm**, UV-C 100–280 nm.
[83:7:2] TABLE head: Term | Meaning (match, NTRUHS)
  [83:7:2.0] Stratospheric ozone | Protective layer against UV
  [83:7:2.1] Ozone depletion | Allows harmful UV rays to reach Earth
  [83:7:2.2] UV-B increase | Eye damage, skin cancer, sunburn
  [83:7:2.3] Tropospheric ozone | Air pollutant harmful to health, plants, materials
[83:7:3] - Not effects of UV: respiratory infections, hearing loss, digestive disorders.

## SUB 2 | Meaning, Definition and Levels of Biodiversity
[84:2:0] - Term **"biodiversity"** coined by **Walter G. Rosen (1985)**; **popularised by E.O. Wilson** – not Tansley (coined "ecosystem"), Darwin or Barrow.
[84:2:1] - **Biodiversity** = the range/variety of **different species** in an environment; includes **genetic diversity** within populations as well as the diversity of **species and ecosystems** (not atmosphere, lithosphere or "environment").
[84:2:2] - **CBD (1992) definition:** variability among living organisms from **all sources** (terrestrial, marine, other aquatic) and the **ecological complexes** of which they are part – **within species, between species and of ecosystems**.
[84:2:3] TABLE head: Level | Meaning / example
  [84:2:3.0] Genetic | Variation within a species – the basic source; all rice varieties are *Oryza sativa* but differ in size, aroma, nutrients; lets a population adapt and respond to natural selection
  [84:2:3.1] Species | Variety of species in an area – richness + abundance
  [84:2:3.2] Ecosystem (ecological) | Variety of habitats/ecosystems in a region; one ecosystem cannot replace another
[84:2:4] - **Narrow genetic base:** the cultivated **banana** (seedless, vegetatively propagated Cavendish clones) may be wiped out by disease – a classic case for genetic diversity.
[84:2:5] - **Crop diversity in space and time** = **polyculture** (mixed/inter-cropping and rotation), not monoculture.
[84:2:6] - Chinese, Americans, Indians and Black Africans = **one species, *Homo sapiens*** (tigers–lions–cats or pigeons–doves–partridges are different species).
[84:2:7] - **Biomes:** no two biomes are exactly alike because **climate and geographic location** give unique combinations of plants, animals and soils (A and R true, R explains A).
[84:2:8] ~~ Sources: Rocket Environment p.53-54; NextGen Environment APPSC Part 1.2 p.11, p.14; app notes 5:84 sub 0
## SUB 3 | Measuring Biodiversity – Alpha, Beta, Gamma
[84:3:0] TABLE head: Measure | Scale
  [84:3:0.0] Alpha (α) | Within one habitat/area at a local scale – mean species diversity / species richness there
  [84:3:0.1] Beta (β) | Comparison between ecosystems – change (turnover) in species composition along a gradient
  [84:3:0.2] Gamma (γ) | Overall diversity across the ecosystems of a whole region (total geographical area)
  [84:3:0.3] Endemism | Species restricted to a specific region
  [84:3:0.4] Species richness / evenness | Number of species / their relative abundance (low evenness = few species dominate)
[84:3:1] - Terms alpha, beta, gamma by **R.H. Whittaker**; indices: **Shannon-Wiener** and **Simpson**.
[84:3:2] - Forest A (2 tigers, 6 deer, 7 rabbits) vs B (1, 6, 8): **same richness, A more even**.
[84:3:3] TABLE head: Measure | Key characteristic (match, NTRUHS)
  [84:3:3.0] Phylogenetic diversity | Evolutionary relationships between species
  [84:3:3.1] Ecological diversity | Diversity of habitats/ecosystems in a region
  [84:3:3.2] Genetic diversity | Variety of genetic traits within species populations
  [84:3:3.3] Species diversity | Species richness and evenness in a community
[84:3:4] - **Functional diversity:** range of **ecological roles** performed by organisms.
[84:3:5] - Field sampling: **mist nets** capture **birds and bats**; dipnets, creel surveys and seines are aquatic methods.

## SUB 4 | Richness Patterns – Latitude, Altitude, Himalaya
[84:4:0] - **Latitudinal gradient:** biodiversity **increases towards the equator** and falls towards the poles – greatest in **lower latitudes** and, along mountains, in **lower altitudes** (UPSC 2011: both true).
[84:4:1] - Ants: ~**10 species near the North Pole vs ~2,000 at the equator**.
[84:4:2] - Richest: **tropical rainforests** (land), **coral reefs** (sea).
[84:4:3] - Tropics richest: **long undisturbed evolutionary time (no glaciation)**, stable climate, more solar energy → productivity.
[84:4:4] - **Butterflies:** maximum species in **tropical countries**; books and keys give "butterflies cannot tolerate low temperatures" as the reason (A and R true, R explains A).
[84:4:5] - **Himalaya** rich because it is a **confluence of different biogeographical zones** (Palearctic + Indo-Malayan) – not rainfall, absence of invasives or low human interference.
[84:4:6] - Estimates: about **1.5 million** living species described (+300,000 fossil); **Wilson (1992) 10–50 million**; **Robert May ~7 million**. **Over 70%** of recorded species are animals (**insects** richest); plants ~**22%**.
[84:4:7] - **Species-area relationship** (**Humboldt**, log S = log C + Z log A).
[84:4:8] ~~ Sources: NextGen Environment APPSC Part 1.2 p.12-14; NextGen Part 2.1 p.10, p.14; app notes 5:84 sub 2
[84:4:9] - **Tropical rainforests** cover only ~7% of the land but hold about **half (50%)** of the world's species.
[84:4:10] - **Factors that raise an ecosystem's biodiversity:** high **productivity**, **intermediate disturbance** and greater **age** of the ecosystem; **fewer trophic levels** do not raise it.
[84:4:11] - **Insects** = about **half (~50%+)** of all recorded species on earth (over 70% of animal species).

## SUB 2 | Hotspot Concept, Criteria and Count
[85:2:0] - **Definition:** a biogeographic region with a significant reservoir of biodiversity that is **threatened with destruction** – i.e. **exceptional (endemic) plant diversity + serious habitat loss**, not just "maximum biodiversity".
[85:2:1] - Concept introduced by **Norman Myers (British ecologist), 1988**; adopted by **Conservation International (CI) in 1989**, which **reassessed** it from 1996. **Not** BirdLife International, IUCN or WWF.
[85:2:2] - Basis: **richness, endemism and threat**. **Habitat destruction** is the main cause of biodiversity loss.
[85:2:3] TABLE head: Criterion (both needed – "two strict criteria") | Threshold
  [85:2:3.0] Endemism | At least 1,500 endemic vascular plant species (over 0.5% of world total)
  [85:2:3.1] Threat | Lost at least 70% of original habitat (30% or less natural vegetation left) – not 90%
[85:2:4] - **UPSC 2011:** Western Ghats–Sri Lanka and Indo-Burma recognised for **species richness, endemism, threat perception** (1, 3 and 5) – not vegetation density, ethno-botany or adaptation.
[85:2:5] - **APPSC Group 2:** hotspots are characterised by **endemic flowering plants and threat perception**.
[85:2:6] TABLE head: Year | Event
  [85:2:6.0] 1988 | Myers proposes the concept
  [85:2:6.1] 1989 | CI adopts it
  [85:2:6.2] 1999 | CI identifies 25 hotspots (numerical thresholds; Myers et al., 2000)
  [85:2:6.3] 2011 | 35th: Forests of East Australia
  [85:2:6.4] 2016 | 36th: North American Coastal Plain
[85:2:7] - Count: 25 → 34 → 35 → **36** (present; "as of 2023" also 36).
[85:2:8] - Once **15.7%** of Earth's land; about **86%** of that habitat lost; now only about **2.3–2.5%** of land.
[85:2:9] - Hold about **50% of vascular plants** (1,50,000 species) and **42% of terrestrial vertebrates** as endemics.
[85:2:10] - Most lie in the **tropics**, but hotspots are **not only tropical**: **temperate** ones include the **Mediterranean Basin** (incl. the **Eastern Mediterranean**), California Floristic Province, North American Coastal Plain. **North-western Australia is not** a hotspot (the Australian ones are SW Australia and East Australian forests) → UPSC 2009: **Eastern Himalayas and Eastern Mediterranean only**.
[85:2:11] - **Hottest hotspots:** **8** with highest endemism under greatest threat; include **Western Ghats–Sri Lanka**, Indo-Burma, Sundaland, Madagascar, Philippines.
[85:2:12] - **Coldspots:** relatively low diversity but losing habitat fast; still matter for rare species, ecosystem functions and genetic diversity.
[85:2:13] TABLE head: Concept | Body | Basis
  [85:2:13.0] Biodiversity hotspot | Conservation International (Myers) | 1,500+ endemic plants and 70%+ habitat lost; large regions
  [85:2:13.1] Key Biodiversity Area | IUCN-led partnership (global standard, 2016) | Single sites important for global persistence of biodiversity
  [85:2:13.2] Global 200 | WWF | Priority ecoregions (terrestrial, freshwater, marine) chosen for representativeness
  [85:2:13.3] Important Bird and Biodiversity Areas (IBAs) | BirdLife International (a global partnership of conservation NGOs) | Bird sites – BirdLife did not originate the hotspot concept (UPSC 2015)
[85:2:14] - **India's share (TSPSC 2023):** **2.4%** of world land, about **7–8%** of recorded species; main threat = habitat loss and fragmentation (urbanisation, infrastructure, deforestation, pollution, climate change).
