## SUB 0 | Ocean Currents of the World – Warm and Cold
[0:0] - **Warm** currents flow from the equator towards the poles, usually on the **east coasts** of continents in low and middle latitudes; **cold** currents flow towards the equator, usually on the **west coasts** (NCERT).

  [0:1.2] North Equatorial, North Pacific Drift | Warm | Tropics / North Pacific
  [0:1.3] Kuroshio | Warm | North Pacific (Japan) – the Pacific twin of the Gulf Stream
  [0:1.4] Brazil | Warm | South Atlantic
  [0:1.5] Agulhas, Mozambique | Warm | Indian Ocean (SE Africa)

  [0:1.7] Caribbean | Warm | Caribbean Sea

## SUB 1 | Waves and Tsunamis
[1:0] TABLE head: Term | Meaning
  [1:0.0] Wavelength | Distance between two successive crests – two consecutive points of the same phase
  [1:0.1] Wave height / amplitude | Trough to crest / half the height
  [1:0.2] Period / frequency | Time between crests / waves per second
  [1:0.3] Swells | Slow, steady, long-period waves from distant storms
  [1:0.4] Breakers | Waves break when depth < half the wavelength
  [1:0.5] Rip current | Narrow, fast channel flowing away from the shore
  [1:0.6] Kallakkadal | Kerala swell surge from far southern storms
[1:1] - Waves carry **energy, not water**; wind gives them energy.
[1:2] # Tsunami
[1:3] - Japanese "**harbour wave**". Caused by sudden **displacement of the sea floor**: **tectonic activity**, chiefly **fault movement in undersea earthquakes** (most important cause), also submarine landslides and volcanoes – not wind, the Moon or sunspots.
[1:4] - Speed depends on depth (about √(g × depth)): **faster in deep water** (over 700 km/h), so in the open sea it is a **gentle rise and fall** and a **ship in the deep sea is not affected**.
[1:5] - Near the shore it **slows down** and grows to **enormous height** (NDA 2013: I and II) – so its impact is greater at the coast; "speed is more in shallow water" is **false** (APPSC FRO 2022: A true, R false).
[1:6] - **Warning sign**: the sea **recedes far, exposing the sea floor**. Mitigation: build houses on **elevated sites**.
[1:7] - **26 Dec 2004** (Sumatra quake, M 9.1): in India the **Coromandel coast** (Tamil Nadu) was hit hardest; Indira Point was submerged.
[1:8] - **ITEWS** (INCOIS, **Hyderabad**, since 2007) confirms a tsunami with **bottom pressure recorders** in the deep ocean (seismic stations detect only the quake; tide gauges are coastal). Tide gauges, BPRs and seismic networks are the working tools; satellite observation is not an effective real-time tsunami warning system (APPSC key).
[1:9] TABLE head: Institution | Role
  [1:9.0] INCOIS, Hyderabad | Runs ITEWS; PFZ advisories; SAMUDRA app
  [1:9.1] IMD | Cyclone and multi-hazard warnings
  [1:9.2] NIOT, Chennai | OMNI buoys (temperature, salinity, currents); station at Nellore (AP)
  [1:9.3] RTGS Vijayawada (AP) | Cyclone, high-wave and fish-shoal alerts to fishermen
[1:10] ~~ Sources: NextGen Indian Geography 03 p.8–9; NCERT Fundamentals ch.12 (OnlyIAS Physical Geography 08 p.24–25); INCOIS (incois.gov.in, ITEWS); app notes 4:24 sub 1, 9
## SUB 2 | Tides
[2:0] # Causes
[2:1] - Tides = periodic rise and fall of sea level, once or twice a day, caused by the gravitational pull of the **Sun and Moon on the Earth**; the **Moon's pull is greater** (nearer). **Centrifugal force** balances gravity.
[2:2] - **Direct tide** = bulge facing the Moon (gravity); **indirect tide** = opposite side (centrifugal force).
[2:3] - Earth rotating west to east is true but does not explain tides (CDS 2018: both true, II not the explanation).
[2:4] - **Surges** = irregular, weather-driven (winds, pressure) – not tides; sea water spreading over nearby low land = coastal **inundation** ("oceanic logging").
[2:5] # Spring and neap tides
[2:6] TABLE head: Basis | Spring tide | Neap tide
  [2:6.0] Sun–Earth–Moon | In a straight line | At right angles
  [2:6.1] Moon phase | New and full moon | First and third quarter (not new moon)
  [2:6.2] Height | About 20% higher than normal | About 20% lower
  [2:6.3] Tidal range | Maximum | Minimum: high tide lower, low tide higher
[2:7] - **Tidal range** = difference between high and low tide. **Perigean** tides: unusually high when the Moon is closest.
[2:8] TABLE head: Type | Feature
  [2:8.0] Semi-diurnal | Two highs + two lows a day (most common)
  [2:8.1] Diurnal | One high + one low
  [2:8.2] Mixed | Unequal heights
[2:9] - High tides recur about every **12 h 25 min**, so about **50 minutes** later each day (not exactly every 12 h).
[2:10] # Tidal places and energy
[2:11] TABLE head: Place | Feature
  [2:11.0] Bay of Fundy (Canada) | World's greatest tidal range
  [2:11.1] Gulf of Khambhat (Bhavnagar), Gujarat | 5–10 m; about 7,000 MW; India's most potential tidal-energy area
  [2:11.2] Gulf of Kutch | 5–8 m
  [2:11.3] Sundarbans | About 5 m
  [2:11.4] Chandipur (Odisha) | Sea recedes a few km at ebb tide twice a day; you can walk on the sea floor (UPSC 2017)
[2:12] - Gulfs with **wide fronts and narrow rears** get the highest tides (so "narrow fronts and wider rears" is wrong, UPSC 2004); a tidal **bore** forms in narrow, shallow estuaries; the Hooghly's tides matter to **Kolkata port**.
[2:13] - Tidal energy: **barrage** (tidal range) or **underwater turbines** (tidal stream).

## SUB 3 | Effects of Currents – Fishing Grounds, Climate and Upwelling
[3:0] - **Best fishing grounds** form **where warm and cold ocean currents meet** (mixing brings nutrients and plankton), mainly between **40° and 60°** – asked 4 times (APPSC G1 2002 twice, UPSC 2013 twice).
[3:1] TABLE head: Place | Current(s) | Effect
  [3:1.0] Grand Banks (SE of Newfoundland, east coast of North America) | Labrador (cold) + Gulf Stream (warm) | Fishing ground; dense fog
  [3:1.1] Off Japan | Kuroshio + Oyashio | Fishing ground
  [3:1.2] NW Europe, Norway, Murmansk | North Atlantic Drift | Ice-free ports, mild winters; warm air meeting polar air brings rain to Western Europe
  [3:1.3] Peru–Chile | Humboldt | Upwelling of cold water; fisheries; Atacama desert
  [3:1.4] Namibia | Benguela | Namib, Kalahari deserts
  [3:1.5] California | California | Sonoran desert
  [3:1.6] Somalia | Findlater / Somali | Upwelling
[3:2] - The Labrador Current meets the Gulf Stream at the Grand Banks, **not near Vancouver Island** (NDA 2010: I and II only).
[3:3] - **Cold currents** on west coasts help form **arid** climates; warm currents warm the coasts beside them; currents keep Earth's **heat balance**.
[3:4] ~~ Sources: NextGen Indian Geography 03 p.14; NCERT Fundamentals ch.12 (OnlyIAS Physical Geography 08 p.28); app notes 4:24 sub 7
## SUB 4 | Causes of Ocean Currents
[4:0] TABLE head: Factor | Effect
  [4:0.0] Planetary winds | Main driver; trades drive equatorial currents west, westerlies drive drifts east
  [4:0.1] Coriolis force | Deflects right in the NH, left in the SH → gyres
  [4:0.2] Solar heating | Water expands near the equator → slight slope
  [4:0.3] Temperature, salinity (density) | Saltier and colder water is denser; cold polar water sinks and creeps slowly to the equator (thermohaline)
  [4:0.4] Coastline shape, floor relief | Deflect / steer currents (a landmass does not always obstruct them)
[4:1] - **Revolution of the Earth is NOT a cause** (CDS 2013).
[4:2] - Ocean currents = large masses of surface water circulating in regular patterns; the slow **surface movement** of water, set in motion **mainly by prevailing winds**, help keep the **heat balance**, are affected by the **configuration of the ocean** – UPSC 2002: **all four** statements correct.
[4:3] - **Equatorial Counter Current** flows **west to east** between the North and South Equatorial Currents (through the doldrums) because their **convergence piles up water in the west** (UPSC 2015, asked twice).
[4:4] - Surface currents = upper 400 m, about 10% of ocean water; deep currents = 90%.
[4:5] - Waves = oscillations moving energy; tides = periodic vertical rise and fall; **currents = continuous horizontal flow** with major effects on **climate, fisheries and navigation**; currents **redistribute heat**, so they influence climate.
[4:6] ~~ Sources: NextGen Indian Geography 03 p.11–13; NCERT Fundamentals ch.12 (OnlyIAS Physical Geography 08 p.27); Rocket General Geography p.64, 73; PMF Physical Geography 07 (counter current); app notes 4:24 sub 4
## SUB 5 | Gyres and the Sargasso Sea
[5:0] TABLE head: Gyre | Components
  [5:0.0] North Atlantic | N Equatorial → Gulf Stream → N Atlantic Drift → Canary
  [5:0.1] South Atlantic | S Equatorial → Brazil → West Wind Drift → Benguela (no Canary)
  [5:0.2] North Pacific | N Equatorial → Kuroshio → N Pacific Drift → California
  [5:0.3] South Pacific | S Equatorial → East Australian → West Wind Drift → Humboldt
  [5:0.4] South Indian | S Equatorial → Agulhas → West Wind Drift → West Australian
[5:1] - Gyres turn **clockwise in the NH**, anticlockwise in the SH.
[5:2] - **Sargasso Sea** (**North Atlantic**): the **only sea with no coastline** – bounded by currents: **Gulf Stream (W), North Atlantic Drift (N), Canary (E), North Equatorial (S)**; calm, warm, salty; named after its floating **Sargassum** weed (**typical marine vegetation**).
[5:3] ~~ Sources: G.C. Leong ch.12 (gc-leong-geography-10.2); NCERT Fundamentals ch.12; app notes 4:24 sub 5
## SUB 6 | Indian Ocean Currents
[6:0] - **North Indian Ocean** currents **reverse with the monsoon** (**monsoon drift**) – the reason the Indian Ocean's currents change direction (UPSC 1997, asked twice); the South Indian Ocean keeps a permanent anticlockwise gyre.
[6:1] TABLE head: Season | Bay of Bengal / Arabian Sea | WICC | EICC
  [6:1.0] SW monsoon (summer) | Clockwise | Southward | Northward
  [6:1.1] NE monsoon (winter) | Anticlockwise | Northward (poleward), November–February | Southward
[6:2] - **Findlater Current / Somali Jet**: western **Arabian Sea**; upwelling off the **Horn of Africa**.
[6:3] ~~ Sources: PMF Physical Geography 07 (Indian Ocean currents); INCOIS; app notes 4:24 sub 6
