## SUB 0 | Articles 15 and 16 – Clauses and Special Provisions
[9:0:0] # Article 15 – clauses
[9:0:1] TABLE head: Clause | Content | Added by
  [9:0:1.0] 15(1) | State shall not discriminate on grounds only of religion, race, caste, sex, place of birth (descent is not among them) | Original
  [9:0:1.1] 15(2) | Access to shops, public restaurants, hotels, places of entertainment; use of wells, tanks, bathing ghats, roads – binds private persons as well as the State | Original
  [9:0:1.2] 15(3) | Special provisions for women and children | Original
  [9:0:1.3] 15(4) | Special provisions for socially and educationally backward classes (SEBCs/OBCs), SCs and STs – "nothing in this Article or in clause (2) of Article 29 shall prevent the State …" | 1st Amendment, 1951 (after Champakam Dorairajan)
  [9:0:1.4] 15(5) | Reservation for SEBC/SC/ST in admission to educational institutions, including private aided or unaided; excludes Art. 30 minority institutions | 93rd Amendment, 2005
  [9:0:1.5] 15(6) | EWS – up to 10% in education | 103rd Amendment, 2019
[9:0:2] - The wording is "socially and **educationally** backward classes" – **not "socially and economically"**; a statement using "economically" is the wrong one.
[9:0:3] - Article 15 special provisions cover women, children, SEBCs, SCs, STs, EWS; **aged persons are NOT covered**.
[9:0:4] - Art. 15 is **limited to citizens** – "Art. 15 is not limited to citizens" is the wrong statement.
[9:0:5] # Article 16 – clauses
[9:0:6] TABLE head: Clause | Content | Added by
  [9:0:6.0] 16(1) | Equality of opportunity for all citizens in employment or appointment to any office under the State | Original
  [9:0:6.1] 16(2) | No citizen ineligible or discriminated on grounds only of religion, race, caste, sex, descent, place of birth, residence | Original
  [9:0:6.2] 16(3) | Parliament only may prescribe a residence requirement for certain jobs | Original
  [9:0:6.3] 16(4) | Reservation in appointments for backward classes not adequately represented in State services | Original
  [9:0:6.4] 16(4A) | SC/ST reservation in promotion – then with consequential seniority (nullified the "catch-up" rule) | 77th (1995); 85th (2001)
  [9:0:6.5] 16(4B) | Backlog (carried-forward) vacancies a separate group, outside the 50% ceiling | 81st (2000)
  [9:0:6.6] 16(5) | Office in a religious or denominational institution may be kept for persons of that religion/denomination | Original
  [9:0:6.7] 16(6) | EWS – up to 10% in public employment | 103rd (2019)
[9:0:7] - **Equal opportunity in public employment** = **16(1) and 16(2)**, for **all citizens** (not only citizens born in India, not all persons).
[9:0:8] - **Reservation in employment** = **16(4)**; **OBC reservation** = **Arts. 15(4) and 16(4)**.
[9:0:9] - **Reservation in promotions:** first provided by the **77th Amendment, 1995** (Art. 16(4A)); the **85th (2001)** extended it with consequential seniority. 16(4A) is **enabling** – it lets, not obliges, the State to reserve.
[9:0:10] - **103rd Amendment, 2019** (passed as the **124th Amendment Bill**) – Arts. **15(6) and 16(6)**, EWS up to **10%**; the 102nd = NCBC, not EWS.
[9:0:11] - **105th Amendment (2021)** restored the States' power to identify SEBCs (reversed the Maratha-case reading of the **102nd**).

## SUB 1 | Articles 14–18 – Which Article Says What
[9:1:0] # The five Articles of the Right to Equality
[9:1:1] - The Right to Equality = **five Articles, 14 to 18** (Part III) – not 13–17, 15–19 or 16–20. Article 13 is not one of them; **Art. 19 belongs to the Right to Freedom** (the odd one out in a 14/15/16/19 list).
[9:1:2] TABLE head: Article | Content | Available to
  [9:1:2.0] 14 | "The State shall not deny to any person equality before the law or the equal protection of the laws within the territory of India" | All persons (citizens and foreigners)
  [9:1:2.1] 15 | Prohibition of discrimination on grounds only of religion, race, caste, sex, place of birth | Citizens only
  [9:1:2.2] 16 | Equality of opportunity in public employment | Citizens only
  [9:1:2.3] 17 | "Untouchability" abolished; its practice in any form forbidden; enforcing any disability from it is an offence punishable by law | All (also binds private persons)
  [9:1:2.4] 18 | Titles abolished, except military and academic distinctions | —
[9:1:3] - **Match (UPSC 2004):** 14 = equality before law / equal protection; 15 = no discrimination on religion, race, caste, sex, place of birth; 16 = equal opportunity in public employment; 17 = untouchability abolished.
[9:1:4] - Art. 14 operates **within the territory of India** (not "deep space" or "high seas").
[9:1:5] - "All are equal before law" / "equal protection of the laws" = **Article 14** (not 12, 13, 15, 21 or 22).
[9:1:6] - Article 15 prohibits discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex, place of birth – not Arts. 19, 23 or 25.
[9:1:7] - Art. 14 = all persons; **Arts. 15, 16, 19, 29** = citizens only. Equal protection of the law is open to all persons.
[9:1:8] - Related: **Art. 23** prohibits **human trafficking** and forced labour (Right against Exploitation); **Art. 30** – minorities' right to establish educational institutions; Art. 29 – cultural and educational rights of any section.
[9:1:9] ~~ Sources: NextGen Polity - APPSC Exams p.169, p.184-185, p.188-197; PMF Polity Notes p.61-62
## SUB 2 | Article 14 – Equality Before Law, Equal Protection, Reasonable Classification
[9:2:0] # Two faces of Article 14
[9:2:1] TABLE head: Equality before law | Equal protection of the laws
  [9:2:1.0] British origin (Dicey) | American origin (US 14th Amendment)
  [9:2:1.1] Negative concept: absence of special privileges; no one above law | Positive concept: like treated alike in equal circumstances
  [9:2:1.2] All equally subject to ordinary law and ordinary courts | Permits reasonable classification
[9:2:2] - Art. 14 contains **both** concepts – "Art. 14 has only equality before law, not equal protection" is false.
[9:2:3] - An implication of equality is the **absence of privileges** (not of restraints, competition or ideology).
[9:2:4] - "Equals must be treated equally, unequals differently": the State **can treat unequals differently** to create a level playing field, and **among equals the law should be equal and equally administered** (A and R both true, R explains A).
[9:2:5] # Reasonable classification and arbitrariness
[9:2:6] - Art. 14 **forbids class legislation**, **not reasonable classification** (not rural/urban legislation).
[9:2:7] - **Twin test**: **intelligible differentia** + **rational nexus** with the law's object.
[9:2:8] - **E.P. Royappa v. State of Tamil Nadu (1974)**: "Equality and arbitrariness are **sworn enemies**" / equality is **antithetic to arbitrariness**.
[9:2:9] - **Maneka Gandhi (1978)**: any arbitrary State action breaks Art. 14. A law giving the executive **unguided and uncontrolled discretionary power** violates **Article 14** (UPSC 2021; not Arts. 28, 32, 44).
[9:2:10] - Article 14 puts a **limitation on legislative power** (UPPCS 1999 key: Art. 14).
[9:2:11] - **11 Apr 2026**: SC held the State cannot treat **pensioners** differently from serving employees on **dearness allowance** without valid classification (Kerala/KSRTC).
[9:2:12] - **Feb 2026**: SC questioned the caste-discrimination clause of the **UGC equity regulations** (**Mritunjay Tiwari v. Union of India**).
[9:2:13] # Exceptions – equality is not absolute
[9:2:14] TABLE head: Provision | Immunity / special status
  [9:2:14.0] Art. 361 | President and Governors not answerable to courts; no criminal case during term; civil case only after two months' notice
  [9:2:14.1] Art. 361A (44th, 1978) | No liability for a true, non-malicious report of proceedings of Parliament/State legislatures
  [9:2:14.2] Arts. 105 / 194 | MPs / MLAs: immunity for anything said or vote given in the House
  [9:2:14.3] International law | Foreign sovereigns, ambassadors, diplomats and UN agencies immune from civil and criminal proceedings
[9:2:15] - APPSC G1: **Arts. 194 and 361A** show equality in India is **not absolute**; in India equality is guaranteed by the **Constitution** (Dicey's "rights from courts" idea does not fit India).
[9:2:16] ~~ Sources: NextGen Polity - APPSC Exams p.184-188; PMF Polity Notes p.61; E.P. Royappa (1974) and Maneka Gandhi (1978) as cited there
[9:2:17] - **Case → Article match:** Maneka Gandhi (1978) – **Art. 21**; Shayara Bano (2017, triple talaq) – **Art. 14**; Indian Young Lawyers Assn. (2018, Sabarimala) – **Art. 15** (with 25); Indra Sawhney (1992) – **Art. 16**.
## SUB 3 | Article 17 – Untouchability
[9:3:0] - **Article 17** abolishes untouchability and forbids its practice in any form (not Arts. 14, 15, 16, 18, 19 or 21). Adopted amid cries of "**Mahatma Gandhi ki Jai**".
[9:3:1] - "Untouchability" is **not defined** in the Constitution or the Act. Art. 17 also operates **against private individuals**.
[9:3:2] - Untouchability as a social practice = **boycotting**, **insult and oppression**, **exploitation** (not equality or integration).
[9:3:3] - **Untouchability (Offences) Act, 1955** → renamed **Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955** by the **1976** amendment (the title keeps 1955). A person convicted under it is disqualified for election to Parliament/State legislature.
[9:3:4] - **SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989** – further law against caste atrocities.
[9:3:5] - **False evidence (Sec. 3(2)(i), PoA Act):** a non-SC/ST person who fabricates false evidence so that an SC/ST member is convicted of a **capital offence** gets **imprisonment for life and fine**; if the innocent SC/ST member is **executed**, the punishment is **death**.
[9:3:6] - MPPSC 2015: the Article through which SCs/STs are given fundamental socio-economic and cultural protection = **Article 17**.
[9:3:7] ~~ Sources: NextGen Polity - APPSC Exams p.195-196; PMF Polity Notes p.62
## SUB 4 | Rule of Law – Dicey
[9:4:0] - **A.V. Dicey** (British jurist), *Introduction to the Study of the Law of the Constitution* (**1885**). India borrowed the Rule of Law from **Britain** (with parliamentary government, single citizenship, cabinet system) – **not from the GoI Act 1935** (that gave the federal scheme, Governor, emergency powers).
[9:4:1] - **Three elements**: (1) **absence of arbitrary power** (**limitation of powers**) – regular law supreme, no punishment except for a distinct breach of law; (2) **equality before law**; (3) **primacy of individual rights** (**liberty and civil rights**) – rights from judicial decisions.
[9:4:2] - **Not an element**: "**people's responsibility to the Government**" → features asked = **1, 2 and 4** (UPSC 2018). "The State has inherent unlimited police powers" – false.
[9:4:3] - Rule of Law means every citizen is **equal before law**, gets **equal protection**, suffers **no discrimination** and enjoys **no special privileges**.
[9:4:4] - Dicey opposed the French **droit administratif** (officials subject only to special administrative tribunals).
[9:4:5] - **India accepts (1) and (2) but reverses (3)**: in India the **Constitution is the source of rights**.
[9:4:6] - Rule of Law in India rests on **Article 14**; it is a **basic feature** – **Kesavananda Bharati (1973)**, **Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975)**.
[9:4:7] - Rule of Law operates in a **democracy** (not dictatorship, monarchy, autocracy); APPSC calls it the force that **moves the machine of democracy**.
[9:4:8] TABLE head: Rule of Law | Rule by Law
  [9:4:8.0] Government subordinate to law | Ruler above law; law as a weapon of state control
  [9:4:8.1] Laws just, fair, non-arbitrary | Governs citizens only
[9:4:9] - **Rule of Law Index** – released by the **World Justice Project** (not Amnesty, ICJ or OHCHR); measuring since 2008.
[9:4:10] > The Rule of Law is British, not American: the Indian Constitution resembles the **US** in **Fundamental Rights** (Bill of Rights), judicial review and equal protection.
[9:4:11] ~~ Sources: NextGen Polity - APPSC Exams p.155 (borrowed features), p.184-187; PMF Polity Notes p.61; World Justice Project (worldjusticeproject.org)
## SUB 5 | Article 18 – Titles and Awards
[9:5:0] TABLE head: Clause | Provision
  [9:5:0.0] 18(1) | State confers no title except military or academic distinctions
  [9:5:0.1] 18(2) | A citizen shall not accept a foreign title at all – no consent route
  [9:5:0.2] 18(3) | A non-citizen holding an office of profit or trust needs the President's consent to accept a foreign title
  [9:5:0.3] 18(4) | No office-holder may accept a present, emolument or office from a foreign State without the President's consent
[9:5:1] - So the Constitution bans **both** the State conferring titles **and** citizens accepting foreign titles.
[9:5:2] - **Abolishes titles = Article 18** (not 17, 24 or 25).
[9:5:3] - **Bharat Ratna, Padma Vibhushan, Padma Shri** are given under **Article 18** (military/academic-type distinctions, not titles).
[9:5:4] - **Balaji Raghavan v. Union of India (Dec 1995)**: **Bharat Ratna and Padma awards are not titles**, but must **not be used as prefixes or suffixes**; equality admits distinction of merit, not hereditary titles.
[9:5:5] - Art. 18 helps **social equality** by ending artificial distinctions.
[9:5:6] ~~ Sources: NextGen Polity - APPSC Exams p.196-197; PMF Polity Notes p.62
## SUB 6 | Types of Equality
[9:6:0] TABLE head: Type | Articles
  [9:6:0.0] Social | 15, 17, 18 – Arts. 17 and 18 provide social equality
  [9:6:0.1] Economic | DPSP 39(b), 39(c), 39(d)
  [9:6:0.2] Political | 325, 326 – no exclusion from rolls, adult suffrage
[9:6:1] - **Economic equality is NOT part of the FR to equality.** Included: equality before law (14), social equality (15), equal opportunity (16).
[9:6:2] - "**Political freedom without economic equality is an illusion**" – attributed in Indian textbooks to **G.D.H. Cole** (the idea goes back to Bakunin's "a pretence, a fraud, a lie").
[9:6:3] - Preamble promises "**equality of status and of opportunity**".
[9:6:4] - **Ambedkar**: liberty, equality, fraternity = an **inseparable trinity**.

## SUB 7 | Reservation Jurisprudence
[9:7:0] TABLE head: Case | Year | Holding
  [9:7:0.0] Champakam Dorairajan | 1951 | Madras Communal G.O. struck down under Art. 29(2) → 1st Amendment, Art. 15(4)
  [9:7:0.1] M.R. Balaji v. State of Mysore | 1962 | Caste not the sole test; reservation reasonable, generally below 50%
  [9:7:0.2] Janki Prasad Parimoo v. J&K | 1973 | Caste alone not the sole determinant of backwardness under 15(4)
  [9:7:0.3] Indra Sawhney (Mandal) | 16 Nov 1992 | 9 judges, 6:3; upheld 27% OBC; creamy layer excluded; 50% ceiling; no reservation in promotions; ordered a permanent body (NCBC)
  [9:7:0.4] Samatha v. State of AP | 1997 | No tribal land leases in Schedule V areas to non-tribals/private parties
  [9:7:0.5] M. Nagaraj | 2006 | Upheld 77th, 81st, 85th with conditions: quantifiable data on backwardness, inadequate representation, efficiency (Art. 335)
  [9:7:0.6] Ashoka Kumar Thakur | 2008 | Upheld the 93rd Amendment; 27% OBC in central institutions
  [9:7:0.7] Niyamgiri (Orissa Mining Corp.) | 2013 | Gram Sabha's role under the Forest Rights Act, 2006; Dongria Kondh
  [9:7:0.8] Jarnail Singh v. Lachhmi Narain Gupta | 2018 | No proof of backwardness for SC/ST promotions; creamy layer extended to SC/ST promotions
  [9:7:0.9] Jaishri Laxmanrao Patil (Maratha) | 2021 | Maratha quota struck for crossing 50%
  [9:7:0.10] Janhit Abhiyan | 2022 | Upheld 103rd, 10% EWS, 3:2
  [9:7:0.11] State of Punjab v. Davinder Singh | 1 Aug 2024 | 7 judges, 6:1; overruled E.V. Chinnaiah (2004); sub-classification of SCs/STs allowed
[9:7:1] - Order: **Champakam → Balaji → Indra Sawhney → Nagaraj**.
[9:7:2] - Creamy layer and the 50% cap **originate in Indra Sawhney (1992)**; OBC creamy layer **₹8 lakh a year (2017)**.
[9:7:3] - Exceptions to 50%: **carried-forward vacancies (81st)**; extraordinary situations; **EWS 10%** (Janhit Abhiyan). No State legislature has free power to cross the ceiling.
[9:7:4] - **Apr 2026**: Andhra Pradesh aligned its OBC creamy layer with the **central income limits**.
[9:7:5] - **Aug 2026**: Centre **opposes** an income-based creamy layer for **SCs and STs**.

[10:0:0] - **Article 19** guarantees **six freedoms** to citizens (not Arts. 14, 18, 20, 21 or 22) – **seven originally** (asked as "seven freedoms" in older papers); the 7th, property, was deleted.

  [10:0:2.3] 19(1)(d) | Move freely throughout the territory of India | 19(5)
  [10:0:2.4] 19(1)(e) | Reside and settle in any part of India | 19(5)

## SUB 1 | Freedom of Speech and Expression – Press and Implied Rights
[10:1:0] - Freedom of speech is **the most essential ingredient of democracy** among the freedoms.
[10:1:1] - **Freedom of the press is not named in the Constitution**; it is **implied in / secured under 19(1)(a)** (not specially provided, not from a law of Parliament, executive power, Art. 361A or the Rule of Law). Trade, residence and association **are** expressly listed.
[10:1:2] - Speech is protected **irrespective of the medium of communication** – press, TV, internet.
[10:1:3] - The **right to know, receive and impart information** is part of 19(1)(a); RTI itself is not named in the Constitution.

## SUB 2 | Reasonable Restrictions – 19(2) to 19(6)
[10:2:0] - In a democracy the right to freedom = **freedom with reasonable restrictions** – not unlimited, not as fixed by the Government or civil society.
[10:2:1] TABLE head: Clause | Freedom | Grounds

## SUB 3 | Availability – Citizens Only; Detenus; Golden Triangle
[10:3:0] - Art. 19 is for **citizens only** – not foreigners, not a company as a legal person (citizen shareholders may sue). Freedom of speech is guaranteed **only to citizens**; life and liberty, equality before law and freedom of religion extend to foreigners too.
[10:3:1] - A **British citizen** in India cannot claim **freedom of trade and profession** (19(1)(g)).
[10:3:2] - Available against **State action**; but **Kaushal Kishor (2023)**: Arts. 19 and 21 enforceable against **non-State actors** too.
[10:3:3] - A person whose movement is taken away by **imprisonment or detention** does **not** lose his **freedom of expression** – he may write and publish books (State of Maharashtra v. Prabhakar Pandurang Sanzgiri, 1966).
[10:3:4] - **Golden triangle**: Arts. **14, 19, 21** – **Maneka Gandhi (1978)**.

=== NOTES 2:11 | Articles 20, 21, 21A and 22 – Protection in Conviction, Life and Liberty, Education, Preventive Detention
## SUB 0 | Article 21A – Right to Education, 86th Amendment and RTE Act
[11:0:0] # Article 21A
[11:0:1] - **Text:** the State shall provide **free and compulsory education** to all children of the age of **6 to 14 years** "in such manner as the State may, **by law**, determine". Only **elementary** education is an FR – not higher or professional education.
[11:0:2] - **Inserted by:** the **86th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2002** (introduced as the **93rd Amendment Bill**, 2001) – not the 42nd, 44th, 74th, 91st or the RTE Act itself (an Act cannot add an Article). The **93rd Amendment Act, 2005** is a different law (OBC reservation in educational institutions, Art. 15(5)).
[11:0:3] - **In force:** Art. 21A and the **RTE Act** both came into force on **1 April 2010** – so the year the right to education "came into force as an FR" is **2010**, though the amendment is of **2002**.
[11:0:4] - **Nature:** a **Fundamental Right** (not statutory, natural or a DPSP right); an **extension of the right to life (Art. 21)**; essential to the right to development as a human right. Aim: **"Education for All"**.
[11:0:5] - **Earlier position:** free and compulsory education was a **Part IV (DPSP)** provision – the original **Art. 45** asked the State to provide it for all children **until they complete 14 years** (within ten years).
[11:0:6] TABLE head: 86th Amendment changed | New content
  [11:0:6.0] Art. 21A (FR) | Free, compulsory education 6–14 years
  [11:0:6.1] Art. 45 (DPSP) | Early childhood care and education for children below 6
  [11:0:6.2] Art. 51A(k) (Fundamental Duty) | Parent/guardian to provide opportunities for education to child 6–14 – the 11th duty
[11:0:7] - **Not** a change of the 86th Amendment: deleting the **Right to Property** – that was the **44th (1978)**.
[11:0:8] # Cases before 21A
[11:0:9] TABLE head: Case | Year | Ruling
  [11:0:9.0] Mohini Jain v. State of Karnataka | 1992 | Right to education flows from Art. 21
  [11:0:9.1] Unnikrishnan J.P. v. State of Andhra Pradesh | 1993 | Read education into Art. 21: free education up to 14 years is a right; beyond that it depends on the State's economic capacity
[11:0:10] - **Sequence:** Mohini Jain (1992) → Unnikrishnan (1993) → 86th Amendment (2002) → RTE Act (2009) → in force (2010).
[11:0:11] # RTE Act, 2009 (Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act)
[11:0:12] - **Passed by Parliament:** Rajya Sabha 20 July 2009, Lok Sabha **4 August 2009** (assent 26 Aug 2009); **in force 1 April 2010**.
[11:0:13] - **Sec. 3:** every child of **6–14** has a right to free and compulsory education in a **neighbourhood school** till elementary education is complete.
[11:0:14] - **25%** of entry-level seats (class I or pre-school) in **unaided private schools** for children of weaker and disadvantaged groups of the neighbourhood; schools reimbursed; **not minority schools**.
[11:0:15] - **Duties:** appropriate Government and local authority to establish schools in the neighbourhood within **3 years**; **every parent/guardian** to admit the child; **funding is a concurrent responsibility of Centre and States** – the Centre does **not** bear sole responsibility.
[11:0:16] - Amendments: 2012, 2017, **2019** (no-detention policy relaxed – regular exams in classes 5 and 8), 2022.

## SUB 1 | Article 20 – Protection in Respect of Conviction for Offences
[11:1:0] - **Available to:** every person – **citizens, foreigners and legal persons** (companies). It cannot be suspended in an emergency (see last sub-section).
[11:1:1] TABLE head: Clause | Protection | Scope / NOT covered
  [11:1:1.0] 20(1) | No ex post facto criminal law – no conviction except for violating a "law in force" when the act was done, and no penalty greater than that law allowed | Only criminal laws – civil or tax laws can be retrospective; bars conviction/heavier penalty, not the trial; not applicable to preventive detention
  [11:1:1.1] 20(2) | No double jeopardy – no person shall be prosecuted and punished for the same offence more than once | Only before a court or judicial tribunal; not departmental or administrative proceedings
  [11:1:1.2] 20(3) | No self-incrimination – no person accused of an offence shall be compelled to be a witness against himself | Covers testimonial compulsion (oral and documentary); not physical evidence – fingerprints, specimen signatures, blood, DNA (Kathi Kalu Oghad, 1961); voice sample for investigation is allowed (Ritesh Sinha, 2019)
[11:1:2] - **Ex post facto law** = a **retrospective** law (not prospective, not a Bill). Hence **Parliament cannot** make a **criminal law with retrospective effect** that convicts or enhances punishment.
[11:1:3] - **Double jeopardy:** maxim *nemo debet bis vexari*; Art. 20(2) rests on **autrefois convict** (previously convicted) – both prosecution **and** punishment are needed, so *autrefois acquit* is not covered. CrPC Sec. 300 → **Sec. 337 BNSS**.
[11:1:4] - **Nature:** 20(1) is **substantive** law of criminal liability and penalty; 20(2) and 20(3) are **procedural**. "Art. 20 does not protect against testimonial compulsion" is **false**.
[11:1:5] - **Selvi v. State of Karnataka (2010):** involuntary **narco-analysis, polygraph and brain-mapping** violate **Art. 20(3)** and **Art. 21** (personal liberty, privacy).
[11:1:6] - **Not in Art. 20:** **prohibition against torture** (that is **Art. 21**); preventive detention safeguards (Art. 22).

## SUB 3 | Article 22 – Arrest and Preventive Detention
[11:3:0] # Punitive arrest – 22(1), 22(2)
[11:3:1] - Arrested person must be **told the grounds** of arrest; may **consult and be defended by a lawyer of choice**; must be **produced before the nearest magistrate within 24 hours** (**Art. 22(2)**, excluding journey time) – not 12, 48 hours or one week.
[11:3:2] - **22(3) exclusions:** **enemy aliens** and persons held under a **preventive detention** law – not ordinary foreigners or OCIs. So detention beyond 24 hours without a magistrate is allowed only in **preventive detention**.
[11:3:3] - **Also outside:** arrest on a **court order** (person in jail under judicial order), **civil arrest**, deportation of an alien.
[11:3:4] - **Not guaranteed:** **bail as of right**, trial by jury. CrPC Secs. 50, 57 → BNSS Secs. 47, 58. **Vihaan Kumar v. State of Haryana (2025):** grounds must be meaningfully communicated to every arrestee.
[11:3:5] # Preventive detention – 22(4) to 22(7)
[11:3:6] - **Preventive** = detention **without trial**, on suspicion of future harmful acts; **punitive** = after trial and conviction. Justified for **public order**, not routine **law and order**.
[11:3:7] - **22(4):** no detention beyond **3 months** unless an **Advisory Board** (**High Court judges** or persons qualified to be) reports sufficient cause. **44th Amendment (1978)** cut it to **2 months**, but that clause was **never notified** – 3 months still applies.
[11:3:8] - **22(5):** grounds communicated + **earliest opportunity of representation**. **22(7):** **Parliament** fixes the maximum period and Advisory Board procedure.

[11:4:0] - **Text:** "No person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to **procedure established by law**" – phrase from the **Japanese** Constitution; "**due process of law**" is American. Art. 21 does not use the words "due process".
[11:4:1] TABLE head: A.K. Gopalan (1950) | Maneka Gandhi (1978)

  [11:4:1.1] Arts. 19, 21, 22 separate | Arts. 14, 19, 21 linked – the golden triangle
  [11:4:1.2] Protects only against the executive | Also against arbitrary legislation – in effect American due process read into Art. 21
[11:4:2] - **Due process of law**, in essence = the **principle of natural justice** (UPSC 2023); the doctrine is now read into **Article 21**.
[11:4:3] - **Natural justice:** **audi alteram partem** = "hear the other side" – the **right to be heard**; *nemo judex in causa sua* = no one a judge in his own cause.
[11:4:4] - **Protection is against** the State's **executive and legislative** action (Laxmikanth). *(Since **Kaushal Kishor v. State of UP, 2023**, Arts. 19 and 21 can also be enforced against private persons.)*

## SUB 0 | Articles 23-24 – Right Against Exploitation
[12:0:0] # Article 23
[12:0:1] - **23(1):** prohibits **traffic in human beings, begar** and other **forced labour**; available to **citizens and non-citizens**, and protects against the **State and private persons**.
[12:0:2] - **Begar** = involuntary forced labour **without payment**, exacted by officials/zamindars.
[12:0:3] - **23(2):** State may impose **compulsory service for public purposes** (military/social service); it need not pay in every case, but may **not discriminate on religion, race, caste or class**.
[12:0:4] # Article 24
[12:0:5] - **No child below 14** shall be employed in any **factory, mine or other hazardous employment** – an **absolute prohibition**; harmless or innocent work is not barred.
[12:0:6] - Child labour in hazardous work is prohibited by the **Constitution (Art. 24)** and by the **Supreme Court (M.C. Mehta v. State of Tamil Nadu, 10 Dec 1996)**; the international instrument is the **UN Convention on the Rights of the Child (1989, Art. 32)** – not the UN Charter.
[12:0:7] - Art. 24 is the FR **directly related to the exploitation of children**. Related DPSPs: **39(e), 39(f)**.
[12:0:8] - **Arts. 21A and 24** together (education + no hazardous work) make the right meaningful for children (**APPSC G1 2023**).

## SUB 1 | Article 25 – Freedom of Conscience and Religion
[12:1:0] # Text and limits
[12:1:1] - **25(1):** "all persons are equally entitled to **freedom of conscience** and the right freely to **profess, practise and propagate** religion" – subject to **public order, morality and health** and to the **other provisions of Part III**. Quoted text → **Art. 25(1)**.
[12:1:2] - **Grounds of restriction:** public order, morality, health – **humanism is not** a ground. So the right to religion is **not without limitations**.
[12:1:3] - Right to religion is a **Fundamental Right** (Arts. 25–28), available to **all persons**, citizens and foreigners. Freedom of conscience = **Art. 25**; "freedom to practise any religion" = **Art. 25**.
[12:1:4] - **Prisoners** do not lose FRs merely by lawful detention (**Sunil Batra**).
[12:1:5] TABLE head: Term | Meaning
  [12:1:5.0] Conscience | Inner freedom to mould one's relation with God; includes freedom to follow no religion
  [12:1:5.1] Profess | Declare one's beliefs openly
  [12:1:5.2] Practise | Worship, rituals, ceremonies
  [12:1:5.3] Propagate | Spread one's beliefs – not a right to convert another (it would impinge on the other's freedom of conscience)
[12:1:6] # State powers – 25(2)
[12:1:7] - **25(2)(a):** regulate **economic, financial, political or other secular activity** associated with religion – not spiritual belief.
[12:1:8] - **25(2)(b):** **social welfare and reform**, and **throw open Hindu religious institutions of a public character to all classes and sections of Hindus** (temple entry). The State may step in to root out a social evil.
[12:1:9] # Explanations
[12:1:10] - **Explanation I:** wearing and carrying **kirpans** is part of the profession of the **Sikh** religion – a **Right to Freedom of Religion** (Art. 25).
[12:1:11] - **Explanation II:** "**Hindus**" includes **Sikhs, Jains and Buddhists** – **not Parsis**.

## SUB 2 | AP Endowments Act, 1987 – Authorities, Temple Property and the Tribunal
[12:2:0] # The Act and its officers

## SUB 3 | Secularism – Meaning and the Indian Model
[12:3:0] - **Secularism** = delinking of the State from religion: **no official (state) religion**, **equal status/respect for all religions**, no religious discrimination, freedom of religion – India is secular for **all** these reasons. It is **not** anti-God, anti-religion or atheist.
[12:3:1] - **Applies to all**, including **irreligious persons** – "not applicable to irreligious persons" is NOT a component.
[12:3:2] - **Modern Indian concept:** **principled distance** – the State **intervenes when a religious practice violates fundamental rights** (reform, Art. 25(2)); not complete distance, not protection of minorities only.
[12:3:3] - **Articles 25–28** emphasise the secular character of Part III; also Arts. 14–16, 29–30, **325** (no exclusion from rolls on religion).
[12:3:4] - 'Secular' added to the Preamble by the **42nd Amendment (1976)**; the Constituent Assembly **rejected 'God'** in the Preamble (1949). Roots: **Karachi Resolution (1931)**, Nehru Report (1928).
[12:3:5] - **Sarva Dharma Sambhava** = state neutrality + **positive intervention for reform**.
[12:3:6] TABLE head: Indian (positive) | Western (negative)
  [12:3:6.0] Principled distance, equal respect | Strict separation
  [12:3:6.1] State may reform, temple entry | Religion purely private
  [12:3:6.2] Aid allowed (Haj, minority institutions) | No state aid
[12:3:7] - **Theocratic state**: an official religion guides law and policy – **not** India.
[12:3:8] TABLE head: Case | Holding
  [12:3:8.0] Kesavananda Bharati (1973) | Basic structure doctrine

[12:3:9] - Indian pluralism also protects language: **Art. 343** – **Hindi** (Devanagari) is the **official language of the Union**; India has **no national language**.

=== NOTES 2:13 | Cultural and Educational Rights – Articles 29-30; the Right to Property and the 44TH Amendment
## SUB 0 | Right to Property Deleted – the 44th Amendment
[13:0:0] # What was deleted, by whom, when
[13:0:1] - **Amendment:** the **44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978** deleted the right to property from the list of Fundamental Rights – not the 42nd (1976), 43rd, 24th, 25th or 62nd.
[13:0:2] - **Articles omitted:** **Art. 19(1)(f)** (acquire, hold and dispose of property) and **Art. 31** (compulsory acquisition) were taken out of **Part III**; a new **Art. 300A** was inserted in **Part XII (Chapter IV)**.
[13:0:3] - **Government:** passed under the **Janata Party government of Morarji Desai** (1977-79) – not Indira Gandhi, Narasimha Rao or Vajpayee; nor a "Congress Government".
[13:0:4] - **Date:** the Act is "of 1978" (exam answer for "which year": **1978**); it got assent on **30 April 1979**, and the omission of Art. 31 (s.6) took effect from **20 June 1979** – so property **ceased to be an FR from 1979**.
[13:0:5] - **Why:** property rights were the most litigated FR and kept blocking **land reform** and acquisition for public use.
[13:0:6] - **Result:** FRs were **originally seven, now six**; property is the **only FR ever deleted** (not speech, religion, equality or constitutional remedies).
[13:0:7] > Asked as: "Which amendment deleted property?" (44th – APPSC G2 2008, MPDO 1998; SSC 2008, 2017; MPPSC 2020; UPPSC; CGPSC), "The right to ___ has ceased to be an FR" (property – G2 2011), "Which is not an FR?" (property), A-R "300A because 44th removed it from Part III" (A and R true, R explains A – APPSC G2, NDA 2000).

## SUB 1 | Right to Property Today – Article 300A, a Legal Right
[13:1:0] # Article 300A
[13:1:1] - **Text:** "**No person shall be deprived of his property save by authority of law**" – the Article that bars the State from taking property except by law (**300A**, not Art. 300 = suits by/against Government, Art. 299 = contracts, Art. 301 = freedom of trade; there is no 299A).
[13:1:2] - **Status:** a **legal right** (also called a **constitutional right**, since it is in the Constitution) – **not a Fundamental Right**, not a DPSP, not a natural right.
[13:1:3] - **Who:** **any person** – citizens, non-citizens and **companies** (UPSC 2021: "legal right available to any person").
[13:1:4] - **Enforcement:** through **Art. 226** or an ordinary suit – **not Art. 32**; it protects against **executive** action without law, not against legislation; the State is not constitutionally bound to pay compensation.
[13:1:5] - **Human right:** the Supreme Court has repeatedly held that the right to property is **also a human right** (forcible dispossession without due process = human-rights violation); UDHR 1948 Art. 17 recognises it.
[13:1:6] - **Not basic structure:** in **Kesavananda Bharati (1973)** the Court held the right to property is **not part of the basic structure** – so "right to property is part of the basic structure" is the **wrong** statement.
[13:1:7] - **AP angle:** **GGR Housing v. State** (AP High Court, May 2026) – strict statutory due process in land acquisition under Art. 300A.

## SUB 2 | Cultural and Educational Rights – Articles 29 and 30
[13:2:0] # Article 29 – protection of interests of minorities
[13:2:1] TABLE head: Clause | Provision
  [13:2:1.0] 29(1) | Any section of citizens with a distinct language, script or culture has the right to conserve it (not limited to minorities)
  [13:2:1.1] 29(2) | No citizen denied admission to a State-maintained or State-aided institution on grounds only of religion, race, caste, language (sex and place of birth are not in the list)
[13:2:2] - **Marginal heading:** Art. 29 = "**Protection of interests of minorities**" – the Article that "protects the interests of minorities" (not 30, 28 or 32).
[13:2:3] - **Objective of the group:** to help **minorities conserve their culture** – not to evolve a single culture or eradicate illiteracy.
[13:2:4] # Article 30 – minority educational institutions
[13:2:5] TABLE head: Clause | Provision
  [13:2:5.0] 30(1) | All minorities, religious or linguistic, may establish and administer educational institutions of their choice – the "Charter of Education Rights"; madrasas run under it
  [13:2:5.1] 30(1A) (44th Amendment, 1978) | On compulsory acquisition of a minority institution's property, the amount fixed must not restrict or abrogate the Art. 30 right
  [13:2:5.2] 30(2) | No discrimination in grant of aid because an institution is under minority management
[13:2:6] - Art. 29 applies to **citizens only**; Art. 30's text is not confined to citizens.
[13:2:7] - Art. 30 is **not absolute**: the State may regulate **standards**, but may **not take away the minority character** ("right to administer is not a right to maladminister").
[13:2:8] - **Spreading religion** (propagation) is **Art. 25** – a right of all persons, minorities included.
[13:2:9] # Article 350A – mother tongue
[13:2:10] - **Art. 350A:** every State and local authority to provide **instruction in the mother tongue at the primary stage** to children of **linguistic minority** groups (not 300A, 351A, 301A); **Art. 350B** = Special Officer for Linguistic Minorities.

## SUB 3 | Legal Right or Fundamental Right? – Vote and Information
[13:3:0] # The distinction
[13:3:1] - **Every FR is a constitutional right, but not every constitutional right is an FR**; a **legal right** comes from ordinary law and can be changed by it; every FR is a human right, but not the reverse.
[13:3:2] TABLE head: Right | Status | Where
  [13:3:2.0] Property | Legal (constitutional) right | Art. 300A
  [13:3:2.1] Vote and to be elected | Constitutional right – UPSC 2017 official key; statutory/legal right – Kuldip Nayar (2009), UPPCS keys and NextGen | Art. 326 (adult suffrage, 18+); Sec. 62 RPA 1951; registration Sec. 19 RPA 1950
  [13:3:2.2] Information | Legal right under the RTI Act, 2005; the Supreme Court also reads it into Art. 19(1)(a) (so "both fundamental and legal" is also accepted – NDA 2010) | RTI Act 2005; Art. 19(1)(a)
  [13:3:2.3] Work | DPSP | Art. 41
  [13:3:2.4] Education (6-14) | Fundamental Right | Art. 21A
[13:3:3] - **Kuldip Nayar v. Union of India (2009)**: voting is only a **statutory/legal** right.
[13:3:4] - **Voting paradox**: voting is statutory, yet the **right to know candidate details, NOTA and ballot secrecy** are recognised under **Art. 19(1)(a)**.

## SUB 4 | Who Is a Minority?
[13:4:0] - **Not defined:** "minority" is used in **Arts. 29, 30, 350A, 350B** but **not defined** in the Constitution (Art. 30 does not define it); no 10% or 10,000 population test exists.
[13:4:1] - **Recognised kinds:** the Constitution recognises **religious and linguistic** minorities only – not ethnic minorities (UPSC 1999).
[13:4:2] - **NCM Act, 1992:** "minority" = a community **notified by the Central Government**; the **NCM** is a **statutory** body (quasi-judicial, civil-court powers), not constitutional.
[13:4:3] - **Unit = the State:** religious and linguistic minorities are determined **State-wise** (**T.M.A. Pai, 2002**); tests: numerical inferiority, non-dominance, distinct identity.
[13:4:4] TABLE head: Basis | Determined by
  [13:4:4.0] Religious minority | NCM, Ministry of Minority Affairs – national notification
  [13:4:4.1] Linguistic minority | State-wise
[13:4:5] - **Six notified minorities:** **Muslims, Christians, Sikhs, Buddhists, Parsis, Jains** (Jains added 2014); linguistic minorities are not among these religious categories.
[13:4:6] - National notification brings a group within NCM and central schemes (scholarships, **PMJVK**, **PM VIKAS**).
[13:4:7] - **Mar 2026:** Kerala's **Malayalam Bhasha Bill** contested as harming the **Kannada** linguistic minority in **Kasaragod**.

## SUB 5 | Land Acquisition Laws and Enemy Property
[13:5:0] # Land Acquisition Act, 1894 → LARR Act, 2013
[13:5:1] - **1894 Act:** made by the **British**; in force **1 March 1894**; purpose was **not land reform** but acquisition for public purposes, institutions and companies; became disputed and was **replaced** by the **LARR Act, 2013**.
[13:5:2] - **LARR Act, 2013:** **Social Impact Assessment compulsory**; compensation up to **4x** market value (**rural**), **2x** (**urban**); **unused land returned after 5 years**.
[13:5:3] # Enemy Property (Amendment and Validation) Act, 2017 (Bill of 2016)
[13:5:4] - Enemy property stays **vested in the Custodian**; **the law of succession/inheritance does not apply** to it, and no legal heir can claim it – it is **not returned to heirs**.
[13:5:5] ~~ Sources: Indian Polity Part 1 p.116; app notes 2:13 sub 4; prsindia.org Enemy Property (Amendment and Validation) Bill 2016 (new s.5B)
