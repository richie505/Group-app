h: Heat budget – 100 units
t: Of 100 units of insolation | Units
   row: Reflected to space (clouds **27**, scattering 6, snow/ice 2) = **planetary albedo** | **35**
   row: Absorbed by the atmosphere | **14**
   row: Absorbed by the surface | **51**

   row: Methane ("marsh gas") | ~20%

page title: Insolation – Meaning, Factors and Distribution
h: Meaning and factors (asked 7 times)
b: **Insolation = incoming solar radiation** – energy received by the Earth's surface as short waves.
b: Factors: **angle of the Sun's rays (set by latitude)**, **length of the day**, **transparency of the atmosphere** (clouds, dust, water vapour), Earth–Sun distance, land–sea distribution, slope and aspect.
b: Intensity depends mainly on **latitude** – not on **longitude** (APSLPRB SI 2023: longitude has no influence).
b: **Perihelion ~3 January, ~147 million km**; **aphelion ~4 July, ~152 million km** – distance matters little; tilt and angle matter more.
h: Distribution
b: Equator gets **direct** rays, poles **oblique** rays → insolation **decreases** from the Equator to the poles.
b: **Least seasonal variation: equatorial regions**; **greatest seasonal contrast: polar zones**.
b: At the same latitude insolation is **higher over continents than oceans**; **maximum insolation and highest temperatures** over the **subtropical deserts** (clear skies, dry air), not the Equator.
b: Equator receives on the whole only about **2.5 times** (not 10 times) the annual insolation of the poles.
b: Heat **surplus in the tropics**, deficit at the poles; winds and ocean currents carry heat poleward.

h: Cloud basics (asked 15 times)
b: **Cloud** = mass of minute water droplets or ice crystals formed by **condensation** of water vapour in free air around **hygroscopic nuclei**, mainly by **adiabatic cooling** of rising air below its dew point – clouds are the result of **condensation**, not evaporation.
b: Clouds **float** because of their **low density**: the droplets are tiny, fall extremely slowly and are held up by gentle updrafts.
b: Four basic forms: **cirrus, cumulus, stratus, nimbus** ("nimbus" = rain-bearing; black or dark grey, dense, opaque).
t: Family (height) | Cloud | Appearance | Weather
   row: High (8,000–12,000 m; **ice crystals**) | **Cirrus** | Thin, detached, **feathery, silky**, always white | Fair; no rain
   row: High | **Cirrostratus** | Thin **milky** sheet; produces the **halo** | Warm front coming
   row: High | **Cirrocumulus** | **Small globular masses**, ripples ("mackerel sky") | Fair
   row: Middle (2–6 km) | **Altostratus / altocumulus** | Grey-blue sheet; Sun as through **ground glass** | Light continuous rain
   row: Low (below 2 km) | **Stratus, stratocumulus, nimbostratus** | Layered; nimbostratus dark, thick, shapeless | Stratus – **drizzle**; nimbostratus – **steady, continuous rain**
   row: Vertical development | **Cumulus** | Heaped **cauliflower**, **flat base**, cotton wool (4,000–7,000 m) | Fair; convective; grows into Cb
   row: Vertical development (to ~15 km) | **Cumulonimbus** | Towering, **anvil** top | Thunderstorm, heavy short rain, hail, cloudburst
b: Match: **Cirrus – silky; Cirrostratus – milky; Cirrocumulus – small globular masses; Cumulus – cauliflower** (A-II, B-I, C-IV, D-III).
b: **Cirrus clouds are composed of ice crystals**; rising domes with flat base = cumulus, and cumulus is **not** a high cloud.
b: **Low cloud set: stratus, nimbus (nimbostratus), cumulus**; **nimbostratus is a low cloud**, cirro- = high, alto- = middle.
b: **Continuous precipitation = nimbostratus**; **highly intense rain = cumulonimbus**.
b: Highest of a given set: **cirrostratus** is the only high cloud among altocumulus, altostratus, cumulus, cirrostratus.
b: **Greatest vertical extent**, convectional rain with lightning and thunder = **cumulonimbus**; rain-bearing clouds = **nimbostratus and cumulonimbus**.
b: **Tornadoes form from cumulonimbus** (supercell) clouds; the funnel cloud and wall cloud are parts hanging from the Cb, not the parent cloud.
b: Rain clouds look **dark/black** because they are thick and dense with droplets, so very little sunlight gets through to the base (strictly the light is mostly scattered/reflected away).
h: Thunderclouds and thunder
b: Thundercloud (Cb) development needs **condensation, high temperature and humidity, orography and vertical winds – all four** (orographic thunderstorms are common).
b: **Thunder** is the sound of air **suddenly heated and expanded by lightning** (shock wave) – not clouds meeting, lightning "separating" clouds or upward air movement → **none of these**.
b: **Waterspout**: funnel extending from a cumulus/cumulonimbus cloud **over water**; one recorded over the Brahmaputra in Barpeta, Assam (26 Aug 2026).

## page 7a5dd1ee28ab
topic: Air Masses and Fronts – Classification and Associated Weather
page title: Weather at Fronts, Map Symbols, k/w Subtypes and Front Zones
b: **Bergeron (1928)** gave the air-mass classification; a third letter adds the **thermodynamic** type: k (German *kalt*) = air **colder** than the surface it crosses → **unstable**; w = **warmer** than the surface → **stable** (e.g., **mPk**, **mTw**).
b: **Lake-effect snow:** cold, dry **cP** air crossing the warmer **Great Lakes** picks up heat and moisture → heavy snow on the lee shores – a textbook air-mass modification.
b: **Main frontal zones:** **Polar front** – between polar (cP/mP) and tropical (mT) air; **Arctic front** – between Arctic and polar air; **Mediterranean front** – in winter, between cold European air and warm N African air.
b: **Mediterranean front** spawns the winter **temperate cyclones** that travel east as **western disturbances** to NW India (rain, Himalayan snow, good for **rabi wheat**).
b: **Slopes:** a **cold front** is steep (about **1:50–1:100**); a **warm front** is gentle (about **1:100–1:200**) – hence narrow, intense rain belts at cold fronts and broad, light rain ahead of warm fronts.
b: **Speed:** cold fronts move **faster** than warm fronts, so the cold front catches up → **occlusion**.
b: **Warm sector:** the wedge of **mT air between the warm and cold fronts** of a temperate cyclone – mild, humid, often drizzly.
b: **Warm-front passage:** pressure falls then steadies, **temperature and dew point rise**, wind **veers** (Northern Hemisphere: SE → SW), steady rain stops.
b: **Cold-front passage:** **sharp temperature fall**, **pressure rises**, wind veers **SW → NW**, gusty showers or thunderstorms, then **clear, cold skies**.
b: **Squall line:** a line of thunderstorms that often forms **ahead of a fast cold front** in the warm sector.
b: **Occlusion types:** **cold occlusion** – air behind the cold front is colder than the air ahead of the warm front (it undercuts); **warm occlusion** – the reverse (it rides up).
b: **Weather-map symbols:** **cold** – blue line with **triangles**; **warm** – red line with **semicircles**; **occluded** – purple with both on the **same side**; **stationary** – alternating red semicircles and blue triangles on **opposite sides**.
b: **No fronts in tropical cyclones:** they form within **one warm, moist air mass**; fronts belong to **mid-latitude (extratropical) cyclones**.
b: **ITCZ ≠ true front:** once called the **Inter-Tropical Front**, it is a convergence of trade winds with little temperature contrast.
b: **Frontogenesis** needs contrasting air masses and **converging** flow; fronts are best developed in **winter**, when the pole–equator temperature contrast is greatest.
b: **Dry line:** boundary between moist **mT** (Gulf) air and dry **cT** (desert) air over the US Great Plains – a trigger for severe storms.
b: **Air-mass thunderstorm** – afternoon convection inside one warm, humid air mass (e.g., **equatorial 4 o'clock showers**); **frontal thunderstorm** – forced uplift along a cold front.
b: **Foehn / Chinook** are **local warm, dry downslope winds** on the lee side of mountains – not air masses or fronts.
b: **Europe vs N America:** westerlies bring **mild, moist mP air** to W Europe (mild winters); no E–W mountain barrier lets **cP air** sweep south across the US Great Plains (cold waves, "northers").
