h: Features
b: **Justiciable:** a person can move the **Supreme Court directly (Art. 32)** and **High Courts (Art. 226)**; FRs are **above ordinary laws** (Art. 13).
b: **Not absolute but qualified:** the State can impose **reasonable restrictions** by law; the **courts judge reasonableness**; **Art. 19** lists **11 grounds** of restriction in all.
b: **Claimed against the State:** most FRs are available against **arbitrary State action** and **limit the power of the State – even Parliament**; some (15(2), 17, 23, 24) also bind private persons.
b: **Not sacrosanct:** Parliament can curtail them **only by a constitutional amendment** (not an ordinary law), without destroying the **basic structure**; they can be **suspended only as the Constitution prescribes** (Arts. 358-359).
b: **Negative and positive:** many are **negatively worded** (14, 15(1), 16(2), 18(1), 20, 22(1), 28(1)); **21A, 29, 30** confer privileges; **15, 17, 18, 20, 24** are absolute limits on legislative power.
b: **Both natural and legal**; they protect **individuals and minority groups** from arbitrary State action (both statements correct).
b: **Britain** has **no written FRs**; it relies on conventions and the rule of law.
h: Statement traps
b: **Wrong:** "FRs are **unamendable**", "**absolute**", "partly justiciable and absolute", "not limited by the Constitution", "all FRs are suspended in an emergency".
b: **Right:** "not absolute", "some only for citizens", "drawn from the American Constitution", "justiciable", "six in number", "granted by the Constitution".
b: FRs are fundamental because they are protected and enforced by the Constitution, can be suspended only as it prescribes, and are enforceable against the State (I, II, III) – **not** because they cannot be amended.
b: A–R: the Constitution is the longest (A true) and the FR chapter follows the American model (R true), but R does not explain A.
