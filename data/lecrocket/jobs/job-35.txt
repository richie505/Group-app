h: The Policy
b: **Date:** **24 July 1991** – PM **P.V. Narasimha Rao**, FM **Manmohan Singh**; announced amid the **balance-of-payments crisis** (forex for about two weeks of imports, June 1991).
b: **Liberal industrial policy = 1991:** start of **LPG**; ended the **'Licence-Permit-Quota Raj'** (term coined by C. Rajagopalachari, Swatantra Party) – licensing, expansion limits, price controls and import-export curbs removed.
b: **Liberalisation started with** **substantial changes in industrial licensing policy** – not rupee convertibility or tax cuts.
b: Result: **liberalisation of industrial licensing and foreign-trade regulations** – not privatisation of core PSEs, not an immediate abolition of FERA.
b: **Repealed Act:** **FERA 1973** was replaced by **FEMA 1999** (in force 1 June 2000) – the Act repealed as part of liberalisation; FEMA is the successor, not the repealed Act.
b: **Liberalisation tools:** **delicensing, import deregulation, lower import duty on capital goods** → cheaper technology and capital equipment, more competitive home manufacturing.
b: Government role moved from **regulator → facilitator**; NIP 1991 was the primary anchor of **liberalisation**.

## page 581b94d72bb0
topic: Industrial Policy – 1948, 1956, 1977, 1980 and New Industrial Policy 1991
page title: Industrial Policy Resolution 1956 – the 'Economic Constitution'
h: Background
b: Announced **30 April 1956** (placed before Parliament by **Jawaharlal Nehru**); reviewed IPR 1948 after the First Plan (1951-56).
b: Goal: **"Socialistic pattern of society"** – Lok Sabha resolution Dec 1954, Congress **Avadi session, 1955**.
b: Labels: **"Economic Constitution of India"**, **"Bible of State Capitalism"**; **basis of the Second Five-Year Plan** (Mahalanobis model – heavy and basic industries).
b: Significance: established the **public-sector-led, licence-based** model that lasted, with minor changes, until **1991**.
h: Three Schedules
t: Schedule | Industries | Role of the State
   row: A | **17** basic and strategic (defence, heavy industry, minerals, transport and communication, power); arms and ammunition, atomic energy, railways, air transport = State monopolies | Exclusive responsibility of the State
   row: B | **12** (other minerals, road and sea transport, machine tools, ferro-alloys, basic chemicals, drugs, fertilisers, synthetic rubber, **aluminium** and non-ferrous metals) | Progressively State-owned; private sector may supplement
   row: C | All remaining | Left to **private initiative**, subject to licensing
h: Major Provisions
b: **Provision of licensing, expansion of the public sector, emphasis on small and cottage industries**; also **balanced regional development**, labour welfare, khadi and village industries.
b: **Licence raj:** compulsory licensing for Schedule B and many Schedule C industries was cemented in IPR 1956 – the start of the 'Licence-Quota-Permit raj'.
b: Licensing itself drew its legal strength from the **Industries (Development and Regulation) Act, 1951** (not from IPR 1948 or 1956, and not from the MRTP Act).
b: **Basic industries:** iron and steel, cement, fertilisers, coal, power – **paper is not a basic industry** (it is a consumer-goods industry).
n: Ramesh Singh 10th ed. part 05 p.9.4-9.5

## page 1b7b691d93fe
topic: Industrial Sickness – Meaning, Causes, Consequences and Remedies
page title: Causes of Industrial Sickness – Stage-wise, Sector-wise and Present-day Causes
b: **Stages:** healthy unit → **tendency towards sickness** → **incipient sickness** → **sickness**; early detection at the incipient stage gives the best chance of revival.
b: **Planning-stage causes:** wrong **location**, wrong **technology**, under-estimated project cost, over-optimistic **demand** estimates → **sickness at birth**.
b: **Implementation-stage causes:** **time and cost overruns** (delays in land, clearances, machinery), raising interest burden before production starts.
b: **Production / operational causes:** **low capacity utilisation**, **obsolete technology**, poor **quality control**, high-cost raw materials, poor maintenance.
b: **Marketing causes:** faulty demand forecasting, dependence on a **single buyer**, weak distribution, failure to adapt products.
b: **Financial causes:** high **gearing** (debt far above equity), **inadequate working capital**, poor inventory and receivables management, **diversion of funds**.
b: **Managerial and labour causes:** incompetent or dishonest management, family disputes, **strikes, lockouts**, overstaffing; studies of sick units found **internal mismanagement** the single largest cause.
b: **Induced (wilful) sickness:** promoters **siphon off funds** and let the unit fail to escape dues – the "rich promoter, sick company" problem.
b: **External causes:** **power shortages**, raw-material scarcity, **demand recession**, sudden changes in licensing, price control, tax or **import policy**, natural calamities.
b: **1991 liberalisation:** withdrawal of licensing and tariff protection exposed inefficient units to **import competition**.
b: **Sector cases:** **cotton textiles** – old looms, powerloom competition and the **1982 Bombay textile strike** (Datta Samant); **jute** – obsolete mills and **synthetic** substitutes; **sugar** – cane prices (**FRP/SAP**) vs controlled sugar prices.
b: **PSU sickness:** political interference, overstaffing, pricing controls and a **soft budget constraint** (losses always covered by the State).
b: **Twin Balance Sheet problem** (Economic Survey **2016-17**): over-leveraged firms + bad-loan-laden banks, from over-optimistic infrastructure investment in the 2004-08 boom, cost overruns and policy shocks such as the Supreme Court's cancellation of **214 coal blocks (2014)**.
b: **MSME causes:** inadequate and costly credit, weak bargaining power and **delayed payments**; MSMED Act 2006 makes buyers pay MSEs within **45 days**; **Section 43B(h)** of the Income-tax Act (Finance Act **2023**, from FY 2023-24) disallows the deduction if dues are not paid in time.
b: **COVID-19 shock (2020):** collapse of cash flows → **ECLGS** (May 2020) for MSMEs.
b: **Early-warning signals:** delayed repayments, frequent **overdrawing**, falling capacity use, delays in stock statements; RBI tags stressed loans **SMA-0 (1-30 days)**, **SMA-1 (31-60)**, **SMA-2 (61-90)**, then NPA.
b: **IBC trigger:** minimum default for insolvency raised from ₹1 lakh to **₹1 crore** (March 2020); SICA's net-worth test no longer applies.

b: **Banks:** loans to sick units become **NPAs** → higher **provisioning**, lower profits, **erosion of capital**; gross NPA ratio peaked at about **11.2% (March 2018)** and fell to **1.8% (March 2026)**, a multi-decadal low (RBI FSR, June 2026).

b: **Investors:** shareholders lose wealth; repeated failures dent **investor and FDI confidence**.
b: **Consumers:** shortages and higher prices where sick units are major suppliers; greater **import dependence**.
b: **IBC outcomes (to June 2026):** about **9,166 CIRPs** admitted; **1,484** ended in **resolution plans** and **3,074** in **liquidation** – about **2 liquidations per resolution**.
