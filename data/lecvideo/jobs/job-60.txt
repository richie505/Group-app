## topic group2 4-17 | Insolation and Heat Budget; Distribution of Temperature, Inversion
### part 0: Heating of the Atmosphere – Radiation, Conduction, Convection, Advection
[0] h: How the air is heated
[1] table: Process | Mechanism
  [1.0] **Insolation** | **Direct heating** by incoming short-wave solar radiation
  [1.1] **Conduction** | Heats the **lower layers** of air in contact with the warm ground
  [1.2] **Convection** | **Vertical** transfer – warm air rises, cool air sinks (troposphere)
  [1.3] **Advection** | **Horizontal** transfer by wind (e.g. the **Loo**)
  [1.4] **Terrestrial radiation** | Long-wave infra-red from the Earth = **main heater** of the air
[2] table: Fact | Key detail
  [2.0] Heating from below | The troposphere is warmest near the ground because it is **heated by the Earth's surface** – not because it is closest to the Sun or makes its own heat, and not mainly by incoming sunlight
  [2.1] Sun's radiation | **Short-wave**: mostly **visible light + short-wave (near) infra-red**; about half of insolation is infra-red
  [2.2] Earth's radiation | **Long-wave infra-red** (not ultraviolet)
  [2.3] Absorbers | Long-wave terrestrial radiation is absorbed mainly by **carbon dioxide and water vapour** (concentrated in the lower atmosphere)
  [2.4] Infra-red | Not part of the visible spectrum
  [2.5] Long-wave heat transfer | **Conduction, convection and radiation**; **scattering** is a short-wave process
  [2.6] Medium | **Radiation needs no medium**; conduction and convection need matter
  [2.7] Wind on a hot summer day | A **convection current** of air; trade winds too arise from **convection** (Hadley cell)
### part 1: Albedo, Heat Budget and Greenhouse Effect
[0] h: Albedo
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] **Albedo** | **Reflecting power** of a surface – the share of incoming radiation it reflects
  [1.1] Order of albedo | **Fresh snow** (highest) > sand > grass > forest > **water** (lowest)
  [1.2] **Low, thick clouds** | **Reflect** sunlight – cooling
  [1.3] **High, thin clouds** | **Trap heat** – warming
  [1.4] **Ice-albedo feedback** | Melting ice exposes darker ocean that absorbs more heat; the **Arctic warms 3–4 times** the global average (Apr 2025)
[2] table: Surface | Albedo (approx.)
  [2.0] **Fresh snow** | **75–95%**
  [2.1] Dry sand / desert | 20–40%
  [2.2] Grass | 20–25%
  [2.3] Crop land (paddy) | 15–25%
  [2.4] Water (sun high) | Below 10%
[3] h: Heat budget – 100 units
[4] table: Of 100 units of insolation | Units
  [4.0] Reflected to space (clouds **27**, scattering 6, snow/ice 2) = **planetary albedo** | **35**
  [4.1] Absorbed by the atmosphere | **14**
  [4.2] Absorbed by the surface | **51**
[5] table: Fact | Key detail
  [5.0] **Earth's albedo** | About **30–35%** of insolation goes back to space without heating the Earth
  [5.1] Surface gives back all 51 | **17 directly to space**, 34 to the atmosphere
  [5.2] Atmosphere radiates | **48** (14 + 34) to space
[6] x: **Heat balance:** 35 + 17 + 48 = **100** – the Earth neither warms nor cools.
[7] h: Greenhouse effect
[8] table: Fact | Key detail
  [8.0] Atmosphere | **Transparent to short waves**, **opaque to long waves**; greenhouse gases send part of the long-wave heat back
  [8.1] Without the greenhouse effect | **About −18°C** instead of **+15°C**; day–night extremes would **increase**

### part 1: Evaporation, Latent Heat, Boiling Point and Adiabatic Change
[0] h: Evaporation
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] **Evaporation / vaporisation** | Change from liquid to gas; a **physical change**; goes on at all temperatures from the surface
  [1.1] Rate rises with | **Larger surface area**, higher temperature, **lower humidity**, **stronger wind**, lower pressure
  [1.2] Rate falls with | Salinity
  [1.3] Sources | Oceans give **84%** of annual evaporation, continents 16%
[2] h: Latent heat
[3] table: Fact | Key detail
  [3.0] **Evaporation** | **Absorbs** latent heat
  [3.1] **Condensation** | **Releases** latent heat → powers **thunderstorms and cyclones**
  [3.2] Freezing (snow forming in clouds) | Releases heat = **exothermic**
  [3.3] Melting ice | Absorbs heat = **endothermic**
[4] table: Change | Latent heat | Note
  [4.0] Fusion of ice | **80 cal/g** (3.34 × 10⁵ J/kg) | Melts at 0°C; temperature constant
  [4.1] Vaporisation of water | **540 cal/g** (2.26 × 10⁶ J/kg) | Boils at 100°C; temperature constant
[5] table: Fact | Key detail
  [5.0] **Latent heat of vaporisation** | Heat taken to turn liquid into gas **without change in temperature**; at the boiling point the **temperature remains constant**
  [5.1] Vaporisation vs fusion | Latent heat of vaporisation is **much greater**
  [5.2] **Steam burns are worse** | **Steam at 100°C holds more heat** than water at 100°C – it releases latent heat on condensing
  [5.3] Air-conditioner | Extracts heat at the **evaporator** coils, rejects it at the **condenser** coils
  [5.4] **Refrigerator** | Refrigerant absorbs heat on evaporation, releases it on condensation
[6] h: Pressure and boiling point
[7] b: **Lower pressure lowers the boiling point**: at high altitude water boils below 100°C because **air pressure is less** → a **pressure cooker** raises pressure and boiling point.
[8] h: Adiabatic change and the uplift chain
[9] table: Fact | Key detail
  [9.0] **Adiabatic** temperature change | By **expansion (cooling) and compression (warming)** of air, without heat being added or removed

### part 0: Air Masses – Nature, Source Regions and Classification
[0] h: Air masses
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] **Air mass** | A large body of air with relatively **uniform temperature and humidity**, formed over a uniform **source region** (land **or** ocean)
  [1.1] Controls | **Temperature, humidity, stability and precipitation**; a new air mass changes the weather
  [1.2] Ideal source regions | **High-pressure areas with gentle, divergent (anticyclonic) circulation** – subtropical highs and polar highs (Siberian, Canadian)
  [1.3] Unsuitable | Air masses do not develop in **cyclonic** conditions; no major source regions in the mid-latitudes
  [1.4] **Bergeron** code | **First letter = moisture** (c = continental, dry; m = maritime, moist); **second letter = thermal source region** (T tropical, P polar, A arctic, E equatorial)
[2] table: Code | Source region | Properties
  [2.0] **mE** (**Maritime Equatorial**) | **Warm equatorial oceans** | Hot, very humid

  [2.2] cT | Hot deserts – Sahara, Arabia, Australia | Hot, dry, stable
  [2.3] mP | Cold high-latitude (polar) oceans, 40°–60° | Cool, moist
  [2.4] cP | Siberia, northern Canada (cold land) | Cold, dry, stable
  [2.5] cA | Arctic basin, Greenland ice | Very cold, very dry
[3] b: **Polar anticyclones** (Siberian, Canadian, Antarctic Highs) are built by cold, dense **cP/cA** air: **cold-core, strongest near the surface**; clear skies, fog, frost.

### part 2: Fronts and the Polar Front Theory
[0] h: Fronts
[1] b: **Front** = sloping boundary zone between two contrasting air masses; lighter **warm air rises over denser cold air** (never the reverse) → adiabatic cooling, cloud, rain. **Mid-latitude (temperate) cyclones form along fronts**.
[2] table: Front | Formation | Slope | Weather
  [2.0] Warm | Warm air rides over cold | **Gentle**, gradual uplift | Prolonged light–moderate rain
  [2.1] Cold | Cold air undercuts warm, lifts it sharply | **Steep** | Cumulonimbus, heavy short showers, squalls, sudden temperature fall
  [2.2] Occluded | **Cold front overtakes warm front** | – | Mixed; **last stage** of a temperate cyclone
  [2.3] Stationary | Neither air mass advances | – | Cloud and rain for days
[3] table: Fact | Key detail
  [3.0] **Frontogenesis** | Forming / sharpening of a front
  [3.1] **Frontolysis** | Weakening and dissolution of a front
  [3.2] Warm front approach | **Cirrus → cirrostratus → altostratus → nimbostratus**
  [3.3] After a cold front | **Cold, clear air**
  [3.4] **Arcus (shelf/roll) clouds** | Form at the leading edge of thunderstorm outflows or cold fronts

[5] table: Fact | Key detail
  [5.0] Authors | **Vilhelm and Jacob Bjerknes**, **Bergen School (Norway)**

### part 4: Tropical Cyclones – Formation and Where They Form
[0] h: Conditions of formation
[1] table: Condition | Role
  [1.0] **Warm ocean, SST above 26.5–27°C** over a large area | Heat and moisture – most essential
  [1.1] Large supply of warm, moist air | **Latent heat of condensation** = energy source
  [1.2] Coriolis force | Rotation; too weak within about 5° of the Equator
  [1.3] Unstable troposphere, high humidity | Vigorous convection
  [1.4] Low vertical wind shear | Keeps the vertical structure organised
  [1.5] Pre-existing disturbance; upper-level divergence | Seed low / cyclonic circulation
[2] table: Fact | Key detail
  [2.0] Cyclone | A **violent storm of strong wind and heavy rain**; forms **only over warm oceans**, not rivers, lakes or ponds
  [2.1] Warm sea, high humidity, instability | **Formative / initial stage**
  [2.2] **Moving away from the sea its force decreases** | The latent-heat (moisture) supply is cut off – weakens after **landfall**
[3] h: Where they form
[4] table: Fact | Key detail
  [4.0] Belt | **Between 30°N and 30°S**; mainly **5°–30°** N and S, western parts of oceans
  [4.1] Absent | In mid-latitudes; **none at the Equator** (no Coriolis)
  [4.2] **None in the South Atlantic and SE Pacific** | The **ITCZ seldom occurs** there; **cold currents** (Benguela, Humboldt) keep SST low
  [4.3] Typhoons | At **6°–20° N and S**; most frequent **July–October**; winds **over 100 mph**; torrential rain with thunder and lightning
  [4.4] Marine heatwaves | Raise cyclone winds by up to 20% and rain by up to 12% (2026 study)
### part 5: Structure, Rotation and Steering of Cyclones
[0] h: Structure
[1] table: Part | Feature
  [1.0] **Eye** | Calm, clear, sinking air; **lowest pressure**; **higher temperature** (warm core); 20–50 km across
  [1.1] **Eyewall** | Ring of towering cumulonimbus; **strongest winds, heaviest rain**
  [1.2] Spiral rainbands | Curved bands of rain and wind – a mature cyclone shows a **spiral pattern**
[2] table: Fact | Key detail
  [2.0] Air flow | Moves **inward and rises**
  [2.1] Rotation | **Anticlockwise (counter-clockwise) in NH, clockwise in SH** – spin from the **Coriolis effect**
  [2.2] Approach of a cyclone | **Falling** barometer
  [2.3] Size | Hurricanes usually **grow larger** poleward; some are **under 150 km** across (Tracy 1974)
  [2.4] Steering | **Trade winds** push tropical cyclones **west**; mature storms **recurve north-east** under the **westerlies**
  [2.5] Threats | High winds, heavy rain, flooding, **storm surge** – abnormal rise of sea level, the **biggest killer**
  [2.6] **Life span** | Usually about **a week – on average ~6 days** – from formation to dissipation

## topic group2 4-23 | Ocean-atmosphere Interaction – El Nino, La Nina, Enso, Iod, Walker Circulation
### part 0: El Nino – Meaning, Mechanism and Effects
[0] h: What El Nino is
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] **El Nino** | An **abnormally warm ocean current** off **Peru and Ecuador** in the **eastern (and central) tropical Pacific**; flows south along the west coast of South America, **replacing the cold Peruvian (Humboldt) current**
  [1.1] Nature | An **ocean** event – not an air mass, a wind or a low-pressure centre
  [1.2] **Ocean** | Develops only in the **Pacific Ocean**
  [1.3] **Name** | Spanish for **"the little boy / Christ child"** – appears around **Christmas (December)**
  [1.4] **Recurrence** | **Irregular**, about every **3–7 years** (2–7 in some accounts); not every year
  [1.5] Water | **Warmer, less saline** (the warm, rain-freshened pool of the western Pacific spreads east)
  [1.6] **Atmospheric counterpart** | **The Southern Oscillation**; together = **ENSO**
[2] h: Mechanism
[3] table: Step | Normal year | El Nino year
  [3.0] Pressure | **Low over Indonesia / N Australia**, **high off Peru** | Pressure falls over the central/eastern Pacific; a weak high forms in the west
  [3.1] Trade winds | Strong **easterlies** push warm water **west** | **Weaken or even reverse**
  [3.2] Peru coast | Cold, **nutrient-rich upwelling** – rich fishing | Equatorial counter current piles warm water off Peru; thermocline drops; **upwelling stops**
  [3.3] Peru sea temperature | Cold | Warms by up to about **10°C**
  [3.4] Plankton and fish | Abundant (anchoveta) | **Plankton falls; fish catch collapses**
[4] h: World effects
[5] table: Region | El Nino effect
  [5.0] **Peru, Ecuador, coastal Colombia, N Chile** | **Heavy rain and flooding** of the normally arid coast
  [5.1] **Indonesia, northern Australia** | **Drought, drier**
  [5.2] Central Pacific | Convective storms, hurricanes
  [5.3] **India** | **Delayed, weak monsoon; drought** (e.g. **2014 deficit**, about 12% below normal; 2014–16 El Nino droughts)
### part 1: ENSO, La Nina and the Indian Monsoon
[0] h: Southern Oscillation and ENSO
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] **Southern Oscillation** | A **pressure seesaw between the Pacific and Indian Oceans** – **Tahiti (French Polynesia) vs Port Darwin (N Australia)**
  [1.1] Discovery | **Sir Gilbert Walker (1920s)**
  [1.2] **SOI** | Tahiti minus Darwin pressure
  [1.3] **Positive SOI** (high at Tahiti, low at Darwin) | Good Indian monsoon, **La Nina**-like
  [1.4] Sustained **negative SOI** | **El Nino**, bad monsoon
  [1.5] **ENSO** | El Nino + Southern Oscillation; used for **short-term (seasonal) climate prediction** of **weak Indian monsoon** years
  [1.6] Relation | **Monsoon and ENSO are inversely related**
[2] h: El Nino vs La Nina
[3] table: Feature | El Nino | La Nina
  [3.0] Central/east Pacific sea temperature | **Warmer** | **Cooler** (strong upwelling)
  [3.1] Trade winds | Weak / reversed | **Stronger than usual**
  [3.2] Walker circulation | Weakens, shifts east | **Strengthens**
  [3.3] Indian monsoon | **Weak, below normal – drought** | **Normal to above normal – good rain**
  [3.4] North India winter | – | **Colder, frequent cold waves** (e.g. **January 2012**)
  [3.5] Bay of Bengal | Fewer post-monsoon cyclones (INCOIS study) | More floods, severe cyclones
  [3.6] Australia / Indonesia | **Drier** | **Heavy rain, floods** (2010–11 Australian floods)
[4] table: Fact | Key detail
  [4.0] **La Nina** | A cooling of the **central/eastern equatorial Pacific** (not the Indian Ocean); **affects** the monsoon favourably
  [4.1] Over India | El Nino = harbinger of drought; **La Nina brings rain**
  [4.2] **Positive IOD** | Can also offset El Nino; the Pacific opposite of El Nino is La Nina
  [4.3] **El Nino** vs Gulf Stream, western intensification, North Atlantic Oscillation | El Nino affects India's climate
  [4.4] El Nino and drought | About **6 of 10** El Nino years brought depressed Indian rainfall; 1997–98 strong El Nino, no drought (positive IOD); moderate 2002 El Nino, severe drought
  [4.5] Nature | El Nino and La Nina are **climate patterns, not storms**
  [4.6] Recent | Last global El Nino: **2023–24**; **2025** = warmest La Nina year on record
### part 2: Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD) and Madden-Julian Oscillation (MJO)
[0] h: Indian Ocean Dipole ("Indian Nino")
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] **IOD** | Difference in **sea-surface temperature** between **two poles of the tropical Indian Ocean**: a **western pole (Arabian Sea / off Africa)** and an **eastern pole (south of Indonesia)**
  [1.1] Identified | **1999** (Saji et al., IISc)
  [1.2] Cycle | Develops from about April, peaks in October
  [1.3] Atmospheric part | **EQUINOO**
  [1.4] Link with El Nino | **An IOD can influence an El Nino's impact on the monsoon**
  [1.5] **1997** | A strong **positive IOD** saved India from drought despite a **Super El Nino**
  [1.6] Link with ENSO | A separate Indian Ocean mode that **runs independently of ENSO** but interacts with it; positive IOD events often develop in El Nino years
[2] table: IOD phase | West (Arabian Sea) | East (south of Indonesia) | India
  [2.0] **Positive** | **Warmer** | **Cooler, dry** | **More monsoon rain; can offset El Nino** (1983, 1994, **1997**); more Arabian Sea cyclones
  [2.1] **Negative** | **Cooler** | **Warmer, rainy** | **Reduced rainfall**; more Bay of Bengal cyclones
[3] h: Madden-Julian Oscillation
[4] table: Fact | Key detail
  [4.0] Identified | **Roland Madden and Paul Julian (1971–72)**
  [4.1] Nature | An **eastward**-moving pulse of clouds and rain; starts over the warm **tropical Indian Ocean**, fades in the eastern Pacific
  [4.2] Recurrence | Every **30–60 days**; convection up to about 10 km
  [4.3] Effect | **Active and break spells** of the monsoon
### part 3: Walker Circulation, El Nino Types and ENSO Monitoring
[0] h: Walker circulation (normal year)
[1] b: East–west (zonal) loop over the tropical Pacific: air **rises over the warm western Pacific / Indonesia**, flows east aloft, **sinks over the cool eastern Pacific**; surface **easterly trade winds** return west. Driven by **high pressure over the eastern Pacific and low over Indonesia**; linked to the Peru upwelling.
[2] b: El Nino **weakens** (sometimes reverses) the Walker cell and shifts it east; La Nina strengthens it.
[3] b: **Bjerknes feedback:** weaker trades → warm water shifts east → winds weaken further → air sinks over the Indian Ocean (high pressure) → **late or weak Indian monsoon**.
[4] h: Types of El Nino
[5] table: Type | Maximum warming
  [5.0] **Canonical (conventional)** | **Eastern Pacific, near Peru**
  [5.1] **El Nino Modoki** (Japanese: **"similar but different"**) | **Central Pacific**, with cooler water on both the eastern and western sides (two-cell Walker circulation)
[6] h: Monitoring and 2026 outlook
[7] table: Fact | Key detail
  [7.0] **Nino 3.4 region** | **5°N–5°S, 120°W–170°W** (central equatorial Pacific)
  [7.1] **NOAA** El Nino rule | **Oceanic Nino Index** (3-month SST anomaly in Nino 3.4) **≥ +0.5°C** for **5 consecutive overlapping seasons**
  [7.2] IOD measure | **Dipole Mode Index (DMI)**
  [7.3] **Super El Nino** | Unusually strong; Nino 3.4 SST anomaly **above +2.0°C** (1982–83, 1997–98, 2015–16)
  [7.4] **Early signal of El Nino** | **Sentinel-6 Michael Freilich** (sea-level satellite) tracked an **eastward Kelvin wave** carrying warm water along the equatorial Pacific
  [7.5] **IMD, 13 April 2026** | SW monsoon forecast **92% of LPA (±5%)** – below normal – as **El Nino** was expected to develop (LPA 87 cm)
  [7.6] **IMD, May 2026** | A developing **super El Nino** could **weaken** India's 2026 SW monsoon
