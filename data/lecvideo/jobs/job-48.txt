## topic group2 3-38 | Poverty – Concepts (absolute, Relative, Chronic), Poverty Line Debates
### part 0: Poverty Line in India – Basis and Calorie Norms
[0] h: Poverty line – meaning
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] **Poverty line** | The minimum income / consumption level needed for basic needs; a person is poor if income or consumption falls **below that minimum**
  [1.1] Variation | It **varies with time and place** (basic needs and prices differ); the Census does not estimate it
  [1.2] **Basis** | **Household consumption expenditure** – **monthly per capita consumption expenditure (MPCE)** rather than income
  [1.3] Why not income | Incomes of the self-employed and daily-wage workers are hard to judge and change with seasons
  [1.4] Defined in terms of | **Calorie intake**; BPL = whether a person can obtain a **minimum prescribed food basket**
  [1.5] **Poverty Line Basket (PLB)** | Socially accepted minimum basket of basic needs bought in the market
  [1.6] Methods used | **Head Count Ratio, calorie intake, household consumption expenditure**; literacy is not used
[2] h: Calorie norms and data
[3] table: Fact | Key detail
  [3.0] **Calorie norm** | **2,400 kcal rural / 2,100 kcal urban** per person per day (ICMR; **Alagh 1979**, Lakdawala 1993)
  [3.1] Dandekar-Rath (1971) | 2,250 kcal for both
  [3.2] **Survey** | **NSSO** household consumption expenditure survey (now **HCES**, MoSPI), about every 5 years
  [3.3] Estimates | Planning Commission / **NITI Aayog**
  [3.4] **BPL census** | Ministry of Rural Development
  [3.5] Official poverty 1999-2000 (MRP) | About **26% (26.1)**
### part 1: Types and Concepts of Poverty
[0] h: Types of poverty
[1] table: Type | Meaning
  [1.0] **Absolute** | Below a fixed **minimum subsistence / calorie norm**; India's concern; UN definition adopted at **Copenhagen (World Summit for Social Development), 1995**
  [1.1] **Relative** | Income **well below the median** of the society – comparison of income groups (top vs bottom fractiles) rather than a calorie norm; persists in the **USA, UK**; measured by Lorenz curve / Gini
  [1.2] **Subjective** | A person's own perception of his position
  [1.3] **Chronic** | **Always poor + usually poor** – long periods or across generations
  [1.4] **Transient** | **Churning poor** (move in and out of poverty – small farmers, seasonal workers) + **occasionally poor**
  [1.5] **Culture of poverty** | **Oscar Lewis**, American anthropologist – poverty passed on as a way of life
[2] table: Fact | Key detail
  [2.0] **Cyclic / churning poor** | Those who **keep moving between poor and non-poor**
  [2.1] Recognised types | **Absolute, relative, subjective**
  [2.2] **Capability approach** | **Amartya Sen**
  [2.3] **Engel's Law** | As income rises, the **share** spent on food **falls**
### part 2: Causes and Social Indicators of Poverty
[0] h: Causes of poverty
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] Causes | **Slow growth, rapid population growth, unemployment** (unemployment raises the number below the line)
  [1.1] Investment | A **rising rate of investment reduces** poverty
  [1.2] Economic insecurity | From **poverty, unemployment and falling real wages**
  [1.3] Roots of urban poverty | The roots of **urban poverty lie in rural areas**: low rural education → migrants get poor urban jobs
  [1.4] Solution | **A complex mix of methods**; economic and social issues are both relevant
[2] h: Indicators
[3] table: Indicator | Key detail
  [3.0] **Social indicators** | Illiteracy, lack of **safe drinking water** and sanitation, lack of health care, lack of **job opportunities**, malnutrition
  [3.1] Not counted | **Size of the house**, **number of means of transport**
  [3.2] **MPCE** | The poverty line itself – an economic indicator
  [3.3] **MPI** (UNDP-OPHI, Alkire-Santos 2010) | **Health, education, living standards**

### part 5: Poverty Measures – HCR, Poverty Gap, FGT
[0] h: Poverty measures

### part 2: Calorie Norms and the Method of Each Committee
[0] h: Method of each committee
[1] table: Committee | Method / basis
  [1.0] Dandekar-Rath | **2,250 kcal** per day, **same for rural and urban**
  [1.1] **Alagh** | Poverty line = per capita consumption expenditure meeting **2,400 kcal (rural)** and **2,100 kcal (urban)** – purely nutritional
  [1.2] **Lakdawala** | Kept the calorie anchor (₹49.09 rural / ₹56.64 urban per month at 1973-74 prices); **State-specific lines**; update by **CPI-AL (rural)** and **CPI-IW (urban)**; stopped scaling to National Accounts
  [1.3] **Tendulkar** | Moved **away from the calorie anchor**; basket covers spending on **food, education, health, electricity, clothing and footwear**; **MRP** data; **one urban poverty line basket (25.7% urban HCR) used for both rural and urban**
  [1.4] **Rangarajan** | Separate **food and non-food** baskets; **2,155 kcal rural / 2,090 kcal urban** plus protein and fat norms; **MMRP** data
[2] table: Fact | Key detail
  [2.0] **Why State poverty lines differ** | **Price levels vary from State to State** (inter-state price differentials)
  [2.1] Planning Commission calorie norm | **2,400 kcal rural, 2,100 kcal urban** – the rural figure is the higher one
  [2.2] Price index for urban lines (Lakdawala) | **CPI-IW**; rural = **CPI-AL**
[3] x: State lines differ because of prices; the calorie pair is 2,400/2,100; the single urban basket belongs to Tendulkar.
### part 3: Recall Periods, NSS Data and Price Indices

### part 1: Lorenz Curve and Gini Coefficient
[0] h: Origin

[2] h: Lorenz curve and Gini
[3] table: Fact | Key detail
  [3.0] **Lorenz curve** axes | X-axis = cumulative % of population (poorest first); Y-axis = cumulative % of income
  [3.1] Equality line | **45° line = perfect equality**; the farther the curve bows away, the **greater the inequality**
  [3.2] What it is | A graph, not a number – it **measures inequality of income / distribution of income**
  [3.3] **Gini coefficient** | **Derived from the Lorenz curve** = area between the curve and the equality line ÷ whole area under the equality line; measures **income inequality**
  [3.4] Range | **0 (perfect equality) to 1 (one person has all income)**
  [3.5] Gini index | Coefficient × 100 (0.40 → 40); **higher Gini = more inequality**
  [3.6] **Relative poverty** | Measured with the **Lorenz curve and Gini coefficient**; absolute poverty with the **poverty line**
  [3.7] India – consumption Gini (World Bank, Spring 2025) | **28.8 (2011-12) → 25.5 (2022-23)**
  [3.8] India – income Gini (World Inequality Database) | 52 (2004) → 62 (2023)

## topic group2 3-42 | Unemployment - Types (structural, Frictional, Cyclical, Disguised, Seasonal, Educated)
### part 0: Disguised Unemployment – Meaning, Test and Where Found
[0] h: Disguised unemployment
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] **Meaning** | **More persons are employed on a job than are actually needed**; the extra workers appear employed but add nothing to output
  [1.1] **Test** | The **marginal productivity of labour is zero** (or negative)
  [1.2] Effect of withdrawal | **Withdrawing some workers leaves total output unchanged** – a shift out of agriculture **need not reduce production**
  [1.3] **Where** | Mainly in **agriculture / the primary sector**, on family farms in **rural areas** (also the unorganised sector)
  [1.4] **NCERT example** | Five family members work a two-hectare plot that needs fewer hands; NCERT calls it hidden **underemployment**
  [1.5] **Hidden, not open** | The persons appear employed, so it is **not captured in PLFS headline unemployment rates**
  [1.6] **Nurkse** | Disguised unemployment is a **potential source of saving** – surplus farm labour can be withdrawn for capital formation (roads, irrigation, railways, housing)
[2] b: Open unemployment (persons with no jobs) is a different type.
[3] x: Disguised unemployment: more workers than needed; MPL = zero; found in agriculture and rural areas. **Seasonal** unemployment is the other agricultural type.
### part 1: Types of Unemployment – Cause and Where Predominant
[0] h: Types of unemployment
[1] table: Type | Cause / feature | Example | Where predominant
  [1.0] **Seasonal** | Work only at certain times of the year | Farm labour idle in off-season months | **Agriculture**, tourism, agro-industries
  [1.1] **Disguised** | Surplus labour, **MPL = 0** | Five family members on a plot needing two | Agriculture, family farms, rural
  [1.2] **Structural** | Demand for labour short of supply; skill / technology mismatch; long-run | Labour-intensive economy turning capital-intensive | **India's basic problem**
  [1.3] **Frictional** (search) | Time between jobs; **workers moving from one job to another** | Graduate seeking a first job | Normal; **exists even at full employment**
  [1.4] **Cyclical** (Keynesian, demand-deficient) | **Fall in aggregate demand – recession / depression** | Lay-offs in a recession | Developed / capitalist economies
  [1.5] **Technological** | Change in technology, automation; a **subtype of structural** | Robots replace car workers | Manufacturing and routine services
  [1.6] **Educated** | Qualified persons cannot find matching jobs | – | **Urban**
  [1.7] Institutional | Labour laws, high minimum wages, union rules | Hiring halted due to rigid layoff rules | –
  [1.8] Vulnerable | No formal contract or social security | Construction labour, street vending | Informal sector
  [1.9] Casual / Classical | Day-to-day contract ends / wages too high (real-wage unemployment) | – | –
[2] table: Fact | Key detail
  [2.0] Rural vs urban (NCERT) | **Rural = seasonal + disguised; urban = educated**
  [2.1] PLFS | Open unemployment higher in **urban** areas (AP 2023-24: rural 3.4%, urban 5.9%)
  [2.2] India | Has all types – structural, seasonal, disguised and underemployment
  [2.3] **At full employment** | **Frictional** (and structural) unemployment still exist
  [2.4] Industry | **Capital-intensive industries cause unemployment**; a labour-intensive industry **employs more hands**
  [2.5] Automation and AI | Reduce demand mainly for **low-skilled, routine jobs**
  [2.6] **Structural unemployment** in a developing economy | Arises from **inadequate productive capacity** (too little capital to employ the growing labour force)
[3] x: Depression → cyclical; job-to-job → frictional; agriculture → disguised and seasonal.
### part 2: Nature and Causes of Unemployment in India
[0] h: Nature of unemployment in India
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] **India's problem** | **Chronic unemployment and under-employment** of a **structural** kind (demand for labour falls short of supply), with seasonal and disguised forms
  [1.1] **Cyclical** unemployment | Negligible in India; typical of developed capitalist economies
  [1.2] **Cause in a developing country** | **Lack of complementary factors of production – deficiency of capital**; the rate of capital formation limits jobs for a fast-growing population
  [1.3] Developed economies | Keynes's "lack of effective demand"
  [1.4] Labour | India is a **labour-surplus economy**; Indian agriculture is **land-scarce and labour-surplus**
  [1.5] Skilled-labour surplus | About **245.3 million by 2030** (projected)
  [1.6] Informal workforce | About **90%**
[2] h: Causes
[3] b: Rapid **population growth**, **labour immobility**, shift to **capital-intensive** production, **stagnation of the industrial sector**, neglected secondary sector and **MSMEs**, stringent labour laws, **delay in official paperwork**, **slow infrastructure**, import-oriented economy, **jobless growth** led by IT and finance.
[4] b: High growth of labour-intensive manufacturing and government aid to the unemployed reduce unemployment; disguised unemployment, poverty and low productivity hold villages back – the **Panchayat system** does not.

### part 3: Theories – Keynes, Classical, Phillips, Friedman, Okun
[0] h: Theories of unemployment
[1] table: Economist / school | Idea
  [1.0] **J.M. Keynes** | Unemployment in developed economies = **deficiency of effective demand**; macro equilibrium is an **under-employment, short-run** equilibrium
  [1.1] **Classical** | Assumes **full employment**; **wage cuts reduce unemployment**; full employment is consistent with seasonal and frictional unemployment
  [1.2] **A.W. Phillips (1958)** | **Inverse relation between inflation and unemployment** (Phillips curve; Economica 1958, UK data 1861-1957)
  [1.3] **Milton Friedman** | **Natural rate of unemployment** hypothesis
  [1.4] **Friedman-Phelps / monetarists** | **Long-run Phillips curve is vertical** at the natural rate (NAIRU)
  [1.5] **Arthur Okun** | 1 point of unemployment above the natural rate ≈ **2% fall in output** below potential GDP
[2] table: Fact | Key detail
  [2.0] **Natural rate** | **Frictional + structural** unemployment (excludes cyclical)
  [2.1] **Stagflation** | High inflation + high unemployment (1970s USA) – broke the Phillips trade-off
[3] x: Phillips = inflation; Okun = output; Friedman = natural rate; Keynes = effective demand.

[0] h: Other classifications
