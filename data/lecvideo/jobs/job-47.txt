### part 3: Structural Change and Services-led Growth
[0] h: Structural change
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] **Structural change** | Changing shares of the primary, secondary and tertiary sectors in national income – occurred **because the process of economic growth was initiated**
  [1.1] **Shifting of workers from agriculture to other sectors** | An indication of **economic growth**, not backwardness
  [1.2] **Post-1991 growth** | **Service-led** – not import-, export- or agriculture-led
  [1.3] **1992-2001** | **Services** grew fastest and contributed most to GDP
[2] table: Model | Author | Prediction
  [2.0] Three-sector | **Colin Clark and Allan Fisher** | As income rises, labour and output shift **primary → secondary → tertiary**
  [2.1] Dual-sector | **W. Arthur Lewis (1954)** | Surplus farm labour moves to modern industry until it runs out – the **Lewis turning point**
[3] table: Term | Meaning
  [3.0] Missing middle | Agriculture → services **without a manufacturing-led stage**
  [3.1] Premature deindustrialisation | Services **generally require high skills and may not absorb semi-skilled farm workers** → jobless growth
  [3.2] Premature leapfrogging | **Services absorb value while manufacturing fails to absorb surplus farm labour**
  [3.3] Nominal vs real share | Fall in manufacturing's nominal share = **relative-price effect** (Economic Survey **2025-26**)
  [3.4] Share logic | A sector growing slower than GDP loses share even if its output rises
[4] table: Fact | Key detail
  [4.0] **EAC-PM Chairman** (Nov **2025**) | Services-led growth cannot be sustained without a manufacturing base
  [4.1] India's global position | **2.9%** of global manufacturing value added; **1.8%** of world merchandise exports; **44th** on the Economic Complexity Index

### part 2: Status Approaches – UPS, UPSS, CWS, CDS
[0] h: Status approaches
[1] table: Approach | Reference period / unit | Counted employed if | Captures
  [1.0] **UPS** (Usual Principal Status) | **365 days** | Worked the major time (**183 days or more**) | **Chronic** (long-term) unemployment
  [1.1] **UPSS** (Principal + Subsidiary) | 365 days | Also subsidiary work for at least **30 days** | Broadest worker count
  [1.2] **CWS** (Current Weekly Status) | **7 days** | Worked at least **1 hour on any day** | **Chronic + seasonal**
  [1.3] **CDS** (Current Daily Status) | Each day of the reference week; unit = **half day** | **4 hours or more** = full day; **1-4** hours = half day | Daily / intermittent; **highest rate**
[2] table: Fact | Key detail
  [2.0] Unemployed for a major part of the year | **Usual status** unemployment
  [2.1] Chronic unemployment | Measured with **usual status** data; the weekly status also captures it
  [2.2] **CDS gives the highest rate** | It also counts the days of unemployment of persons recorded as employed under CWS
  [2.3] NSS reference periods | **Three** (year, week, day); there is no "Usual / Current Monthly Status" approach
  [2.4] **Census** | **Main worker = worked 183 days or more** in the year; marginal worker = less than 183 days (a Census line, not an NSS approach)
[3] x: Chronic unemployment = **usual status**; NSS has year, week and day approaches – no monthly one.
### part 3: Labour Force Indicators – LFPR, WPR, UR
[0] h: Definitions
[1] table: Indicator | Definition
  [1.0] **Labour force** | **Employed + unemployed** (seeking or available for work); workforce = employed only
  [1.1] **LFPR** | Labour force (employed + those willing and available) ÷ **working-age** population (per **100** / **1000**)
  [1.2] **WPR** (Worker Population Ratio) | **Employed persons as a percentage of the total population**
  [1.3] **UR** (Unemployment Rate) | Unemployed ÷ **labour force** × 100
  [1.4] **PU** (Proportion Unemployed) | Unemployed ÷ population
  [1.5] Identity | **WPR = LFPR × (1 − UR)**
[2] table: Fact | Key detail
  [2.0] Outside the labour force | **Students, homemakers** and others not seeking work

### part 4: Micro Basics – Production, Costs and Factor Pricing
[0] h: Production
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] **Law of diminishing returns** | More units of a variable factor added to a fixed factor → **marginal product eventually diminishes**

[2] h: Costs

## topic group2 3-37 | Environmental Degradation As A Development Challenge; Green Accounting and Green Gdp
### part 0: Resource Depletion and Causes of Environmental Degradation
[0] h: Resource depletion
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] **Resource depletion** | Use faster than regeneration; **overuse of natural resources → resource depletion**
  [1.1] Best illustration | **Overfishing in a coastal area** harming the whole marine ecosystem and livelihoods
  [1.2] Not depletion | A forest managed **sustainably for timber while keeping biodiversity**
[2] table: Resource | Cause of depletion
  [2.0] Groundwater | Deep bore tubewells for irrigation
  [2.1] Topsoil | Brick production
  [2.2] Biodiversity habitats | Expansion of agriculture
[3] h: Causes vs conservation practices
[4] table: Human-driven causes | Conservation practices
  [4.0] **Deforestation** (primary cause) | Sustainable agriculture
  [4.1] Chemical-intensive farming | Renewable energy production
  [4.2] Groundwater over-extraction, dams | Wildlife conservation
  [4.3] Road and urban expansion, industrial emissions, overfishing | –
[5] table: Fact | Key detail
  [5.0] **Deforestation** | Driven by urbanisation, agricultural expansion and timber extraction; **reduces** biodiversity
  [5.1] India's **two threats** | **Poverty-induced degradation** + **pollution from affluence** and a rapidly growing industrial sector
  [5.2] Green Revolution | HYV monoculture and chemical fertilisers cut soil fertility; **HYV irrigation needs** depleted the water table
  [5.3] Fertiliser | **Urea ≈ 56%** of fertiliser use; low-carbon alternative **green urea** (green hydrogen + captured CO2)
  [5.4] India's low EPI score | **Urban particulate pollution and coal-based power**
  [5.5] Largest polluter overall | **Industry** (factories, thermal power, mining) is the **largest contributor**; transport is a major urban air polluter but smaller overall
### part 1: Functions of the Environment, Absorptive and Carrying Capacity
[0] h: Functions of the environment
[1] table: Function | Meaning
  [1.0] **Supplies resources** (first function) | **Both renewable** (trees, fish – replaced naturally or inexhaustible) and **non-renewable** (fossil fuels – exhausted with use)
  [1.1] Assimilates waste | Absorbs and neutralises wastes
  [1.2] Sustains life | Genetic and biodiversity
  [1.3] Aesthetic services | Scenery and amenity
[2] b: Recycling is a human activity, not one of the environment's functions.
[3] h: Key concepts
[4] table: Fact | Key detail
  [4.0] **Renewable resources** | Those that **can be replaced naturally over time or are inexhaustible** – used without the resource becoming depleted
  [4.1] **Absorptive capacity** | Ability of the environment to absorb degradation = the **maximum amount of pollutants an ecosystem can process and neutralise without harmful effects**
  [4.2] **Carrying capacity** (NCERT) | The environment works without interruption as long as **resource extraction is not above the rate of regeneration** and **wastes are within the assimilating capacity**
  [4.3] **Crisis** | When **extraction > regeneration** and waste > absorptive capacity, the environment **cannot sustain life**
[5] x: "We are today at the threshold of environmental crisis."
