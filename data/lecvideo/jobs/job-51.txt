## topic group2 3-68 | Financial Instabilities – Crises, Systemic Risk, Shadow Banking, Macroprudential Regulation
### part 0: Global Financial Crisis 2007-09 – Sub-prime, Lehman, Teaser Loans
[0] h: Sub-prime crisis
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] **Origin** | The **US sub-prime mortgage** market (surfaced July 2007) – loans to borrowers with poor credit records
  [1.1] Cause | The **US housing (real-estate) bust** – not oil prices
  [1.2] **Lehman Brothers** | US investment bank; filed for **Chapter 11 bankruptcy on 15 Sep 2008**; sub-prime lending was a main cause of the crisis
[2] b: **Chain:** loans to **weak borrowers** → sold on and **securitised** into **mortgage-backed securities** sold worldwide → rates rise, **home-loan defaults** → **failure of US banks** → FPI outflows and a **crash in the Indian stock market**.
[3] h: Related terms
[4] table: Term | Key detail
  [4.0] **Teaser loans** | **Low fixed rate in the first years, higher later** (e.g. SBI home loans, 2008-09) – an aspect of **sub-prime lending**; banks risk **future defaults**; they were home loans, not loans to new entrepreneurs for factories or exports
  [4.1] **Credit Default Swap** | Insurance-like derivative on bonds; seller pays on default; **counterparty risk**
  [4.2] **Response in India** | FSDC set up **Dec 2010** (G-20 initiative after the crisis); shadow banking came under scrutiny worldwide

[0] table: Fact | Key detail
  [0.0] **Shadow banking** | Credit creation by **financial institutions outside the regular banking regulatory framework** (hedge funds, NBFCs; also unregulated activities of regulated entities) – they escape CRR/SLR and capital rules

  [1.2] CPI-AL / CPI-RL (Agricultural / Rural Labourers) | Labour Bureau | 2019 (from June 2025; earlier 1986-87) | Farm and rural labourers' wages
  [1.3] **CPI-UNME** (Urban Non-Manual Employees) | **CSO** – the only one of the four labour/worker CPIs not compiled by the Labour Bureau | 1984-85 | Discontinued from **January 2011** after CPI-Urban began

[2] table: Fact | Key detail

  [2.1] **Labour Bureau** | Set up **1920**, at **Chandigarh**, under the **Ministry of Labour and Employment**
  [2.2] **Dearness allowance (DA)** | Fixed on the **Consumer Price Index – CPI-IW**; linking pay to a price index is **indexation** ("fixing of wage compensation")
  [2.3] **IIP** | Measures **industrial output, not prices** (NSO)
  [2.4] **National income and GDP estimates** | **NSO** under MoSPI (earlier CSO)
  [2.5] **Retail price index** | Change in the **average cost of living** of households (a basket of retail prices)
[3] h: Headline measure – from WPI to CPI
[4] b: WPI was India's **headline / "primary" / most common** inflation measure **till April 2014**.
[5] b: **April 2014:** RBI adopted **CPI (Combined)** as its key measure (Urjit Patel Committee); **headline inflation is now based on CPI-C**, while CPI, WPI and the labour CPIs are all still published.
[6] b: Inflation rate = **year-on-year % change in CPI** (current month's CPI over the CPI of the same month last year).
[7] h: WPI
[8] table: Fact | Key detail
  [8.0] Coverage | Prices of goods traded in bulk (first point); **no services** – CPI covers goods **and services**
  [8.1] **2011-12 series** | **697 items**; **manufactured products = largest weight, 64.23%** (primary articles 22.62%, fuel and power 13.15%; 2004-05 series: manufactured 64.97%)
  [8.2] **New WPI (base 2022-23, 2026)** | **957 items**; weights on **Gross Value of Output**; runs with a new **Producer Price Index (PPI)** for **5 years**, then WPI is phased out
  [8.3] **PPI** | **Average change in prices received by producers** (output PPI: goods leaving production; input PPI: inputs entering it)
  [8.4] Release | **Weekly** (primary articles, fuel) until 2012, so in 2010 it was not only monthly
  [8.5] Food weight | WPI gives **less weight to food** than CPI-IW
  [8.6] Base 2004-05 series | Introduced on **14 September 2010**
[9] h: CPI structure
[10] b: New CPI (base 2024): **12 divisions** (COICOP 2018), **358 items**; **food and beverages 36.75%** (about 46% in the 2012 series); 12 online platforms and rural house rent added. **Food has a bigger weight in CPI than in WPI**.
[11] h: Committees on price indices
[12] table: Committee | Subject
  [12.0] **Abhijit Sen** (2007; report May 2008) | WPI revision – base **2004-05**
  [12.1] Saumitra Chaudhuri (2012) | WPI revision – base 2011-12
  [12.2] Ramesh Chand (NITI Aayog, 2025) | WPI base 2022-23
  [12.3] **Urjit Patel** (2014) | CPI as nominal anchor; flexible inflation targeting
[13] h: GDP deflator
[14] b: **GDP deflator = (Nominal GDP / Real GDP) × 100** – the **broadest** measure (all goods and services produced).
[15] b: A **rise in the GDP/GNP deflator** = rising prices → **fall in the purchasing power of money**.
### part 1: Causes and Types of Inflation by Cause
[0] h: By cause
[1] table: Type | Cause
  [1.0] **Demand-pull** | **Aggregate demand > aggregate supply**: expansionary fiscal and monetary policy, **fiscal stimulus**, higher purchasing power, rising money supply – "too much money chasing too few goods"
  [1.1] **Cost-push** | **Rise in prices of factors of production** (wages, raw materials, fuel) → **supply falls** while demand is constant
  [1.2] **Structural / bottleneck** | Deficiencies in the economy – poor farm productivity, **supply bottlenecks**, **inefficient distribution** and storage; persists long and is **hard for government to tackle**
  [1.3] Export-led | Strong export demand drains domestic supply
[2] table: Fact | Key detail
  [2.0] **Inflationary gap** | Excess of aggregate demand (spending) over aggregate supply at **full-employment** output
  [2.1] Not demand-pull causes | **Inflation-indexing of wages** (cost side) and **rising interest rates** (they cut demand)
  [2.2] Input costs | A **permanent fall in input costs** reduces cost-push pressure
  [2.3] **Inflation expectations** | Also feed inflation (wage-price spiral)
  [2.4] **Decrease in supply** | Supply curve shifts left – the **same quantity is supplied only at a higher price**; extension/contraction are movements along the curve
[3] h: Causes of price rise in India
[4] table: Side | Factors
  [4.0] Demand | **Increase in money supply**, rise in non-developmental public expenditure, **deficit financing / deficit budget**, loose monetary policy, rapid GDP growth, population growth
  [4.1] Supply | **Erratic / less agricultural production**, **hoarding**, higher MSPs, **rise in crude oil and fertiliser prices** (oil shocks), inefficient storage and marketing, rupee depreciation, cartelisation
[5] b: General price rise may come from **more money supply**, **lower aggregate output** and **higher effective demand**.
[6] b: **Food inflation:** changing **consumption pattern with rising incomes** and **structural constraints in the food supply chain**.
[7] b: **Supply shocks** (erratic monsoon, El Nino, costlier crude, rupee depreciation) are the **dominant cause** of inflation in India; **better storage and distribution efficiency** eases them.
[8] b: An **unstable currency** works **against** inflation management; stability, sound monetary policy and balanced supply-demand help.
[9] h: Crude oil benchmarks
[10] b: **Brent** (North Sea), **West Texas Intermediate (WTI)** (US) and **Dubai** – grades of **crude oil** used as price benchmarks; oil-price shocks feed cost-push inflation.
### part 2: Meaning of Inflation and Its Kinds by Speed
[0] h: Definition
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] **Inflation** | A sustained **rise in the general price level** and a **fall in the purchasing power (value) of money**; the essential attribute is **increase in prices**
  [1.1] During inflation | Prices **increase**; when all prices rise, the purchasing power of money **decreases**
  [1.2] **Rise in a commodity's price** | **Fall in the value of currency and rise in the value of the commodity** (in money terms)
  [1.3] **Economic growth** | Usually coupled with **(moderate) inflation** – rising income and demand push prices up
[2] h: Related terms
[3] table: Term | Meaning
  [3.0] **Deflation** | **Persistent fall in the general price level** (negative inflation) – not a currency fall or a fall in the inflation rate
  [3.1] **Disinflation** | **Fall in the rate of inflation**, still positive (e.g. 3% → 2%)
  [3.2] **Reflation** | Inflation returning after deflation – sign of recovery
  [3.3] **Stagflation** | High inflation + **slow growth / stagnant output** + high unemployment
  [3.4] **Skewflation** | Inflation in some items (e.g. food) while others are stable or falling
[4] b: A **fall in the inflation rate** means only that **prices are rising more slowly**.
[5] h: Stagflation factors
[6] b: Inflationary pressure (e.g. from **higher government spending**) **plus** **declining industrial production and sluggish growth** → stagflation.
[7] b: A **fall in aggregate demand** and **currency appreciation** pull prices **down** – they slow growth but do not create the inflation half of stagflation.
[8] h: By speed
[9] table: Type | Rate
  [9.0] **Creeping** | Gradual, below about **3%**
  [9.1] **Walking / trotting** | **3-10%**
  [9.2] **Galloping** | Double or triple digit (20%, 100%, 200% a year)
  [9.3] **Hyperinflation** | Large and accelerating; prices shoot up overnight
### part 3: Controlling Inflation
[0] h: Tools
[1] table: Type | Measures
  [1.0] **Monetary (RBI)** | **Decrease money circulation**: raise repo, reverse repo, Bank Rate, CRR, SLR; **sell government securities (OMO)** – reducing interest rates works the other way
  [1.1] **Fiscal (Government)** | Cut non-developmental spending, avoid deficit financing, raise direct taxes, cut indirect taxes on essentials
  [1.2] **Supply / administrative** | Control demand, **rationing**, prevent hoarding and black marketing, ban exports of scarce goods, suspend futures trading, buffer stocks
[2] b: Control needs a **combination of monetary, fiscal and exchange-rate policies** – monetary policy alone cannot fix supply-side inflation; the **RBI** has the key role alongside the Government.
[3] table: Measure | Key detail
  [3.0] **Dual price policy** | **Sugar** – a share of output (**levy sugar**) bought at a controlled price for the PDS, the rest sold in the open market (levy abolished 2013)
  [3.1] **Price Stabilisation Fund (PSF)** | Central Sector Scheme, **2014-15 (launched March 2015)**; began with **onion and potato**; pulses added later
  [3.2] **Bharat Atta** | Subsidised wheat flour – helped contain **wheat inflation**
  [3.3] **Bharat Dal** | Chana dal (July 2023), extended to **moong dal**; sold via NAFED, NCCF, Kendriya Bhandar; **Bharat Chana Dal** at **₹60/kg** from July 2023
  [3.4] OMSS (Domestic) | FCI sells **wheat and rice**
  [3.5] **Onion exports** | Ban lifted **4 May 2024** with **40% duty** and US$550 MEP; MEP removed and duty cut to **20%** on **13 Sep 2024**; NCCF and NAFED released 47,786 MT of PSF onion (to 8 Oct 2025)
  [3.6] **RBI's biggest concern** | **Inflation**
  [3.7] Repo rate (7 Oct 2026) | **5.50%** (5.25% in Aug 2026); repo acts on demand and **cannot directly fix supply shocks** (onion, crude oil)
[4] h: Inflation-Indexed Bonds (IIBs)
[5] table: Fact | Key detail
  [5.0] Design | **Principal and/or interest linked to an inflation index** → protect investors from **uncertainty about inflation** (real return)
  [5.1] For government | Can **reduce coupon rates** on its borrowing (no inflation-risk premium)
  [5.2] Tax | Interest and capital gains are **taxable**
  [5.3] Issued | By the RBI in **2013**
[6] h: AP measures
[7] b: **State-level Price Monitoring Committee** headed by the **Chief Secretary**; **DES monitors 8 essential commodities daily** (groundnut and palm oil added Feb 2025).
### part 4: Effects of Inflation and Deflation
[0] h: Gainers and losers of inflation
[1] table: Gain | Lose
  [1.0] **Debtors (borrowers)** – repay money of lower real value | **Creditors (lenders)** – hurt most
  [1.1] Businessmen, producers, big farmers (profits rise faster than costs) | **Fixed-income groups** – pensioners, salary and wage earners
  [1.2] Holders of real assets, equity investors | **Bond / debenture holders**, savings-account holders
  [1.3] Exporters (if the rupee depreciates) | Consumers, the poor
[2] b: Rule: **inflation benefits debtors**, not bond-holders, consumers or fixed-income groups. With a price hike, businessmen generally gain (though not all) and consumers must curtail needs.
[3] b: Other effects: falling saving (MPS falls), shift to gold, rising inequality, wage-price spiral, fewer exports; **food inflation is regressive** (the poor spend more of income on food); **menu costs** of re-pricing.
[4] h: Deflation
[5] b: Purchasing power of money **rises** → **fixed-income groups (pensioners, salary earners)** and **creditors gain**; **debtors lose** (heavier real debt).
[6] b: **Deflationary spiral:** postponed purchases → lower profits → higher unemployment → heavier real debt burden.

### part 1: Deficit Definitions and Formulas
[0] h: Definitions
[1] table: Deficit | Formula | Shows
  [1.0] Revenue (RD) | **Revenue expenditure − revenue receipts** | Borrowing for day-to-day consumption (dissaving)
  [1.1] Effective RD (ERD) | **RD − grants for creation of capital assets** | True consumption deficit (C. Rangarajan concept)
  [1.2] Fiscal (FD / GFD) | **Total expenditure − (revenue receipts + non-debt capital receipts, i.e. recovery of loans + disinvestment)** | **Total borrowing requirement**
  [1.3] Primary (PD) | **FD − interest payments** | Current imbalance, excluding past debt
  [1.4] Budget deficit (old) | **Total expenditure − total receipts** (incl. borrowings) | Ad hoc T-bill era measure
  [1.5] Monetised | Part of FD financed by the **RBI** (high-powered money) | Inflationary
  [1.6] Net FD | **GFD − net lending of the Centre** | e.g. 1,200 − 400 = **₹800 crore**
[2] h: Fiscal deficit
[3] table: Fact | Key detail
  [3.0] Other forms | **FD** = total expenditure minus total receipts **excluding borrowings** = **budget deficit + borrowings and other liabilities** (market borrowings)
  [3.1] Nature | Closest to the **interest-bearing borrowings during a year** – a flow, not accumulated debt
  [3.2] Loose wording | "Excess of total expenditure over revenue receipts" = **fiscal deficit**; strictly, non-debt capital receipts are also deducted
  [3.3] **GFD (financing side)** | **Net borrowing at home + borrowing from RBI + borrowing from abroad**; net borrowing at home includes small savings and, indirectly, bank SLR holdings
  [3.4] Build-up | **FD = RD + capital expenditure − non-debt capital receipts**; RD is a part of FD
  [3.5] **Headline marker** | The **fiscal deficit** is the headline marker of fiscal health; RD and ERD show what the borrowing is used for
[4] h: Primary and effective revenue deficit
[5] table: Fact | Key detail
  [5.0] PD | **PD = FD − interest** (= **gross primary deficit** when taken from GFD); **PD + interest = FD**; it is not revenue deficit minus interest
  [5.1] **ERD** | Introduced in **Union Budget 2011-12**; added to FRBM by the **2012 amendment**; dropped as a target in 2018; shows capital receipts used for consumption
  [5.2] Official terms | RD, FD, PD adopted from **1997-98**; budget deficit stopped being the headline measure after ad hoc T-bills ended (1997)

### part 0: Meaning and Purpose of Deficit Financing
[0] h: Meaning
[1] table: Usage | Meaning
  [1.0] Indian | Budget gap met by **borrowing from the RBI**; RBI creates new money – the government **"buys money" from the RBI**
  [1.1] Western | Any financing of a budget deficit, mainly **borrowing from the public/market**
  [1.2] Effect | **Increase in money supply**; creates **extra paper currency**
[2] table: Fact | Key detail
  [2.0] **Deficit financing** | Financing a **deficit budget** – **spending in excess of revenue** (total expenditure > total receipts) and sustaining that gap
  [2.1] **Fiscal deficit** (Union Budget) | Budgetary deficit + net increase in internal and external borrowings and other liabilities
  [2.2] **Deficit unit** | An economic unit that **spends more than it receives** in a period
[3] h: Purpose in Indian planning
[4] table: Fact | Key detail
  [4.0] Purpose | **Raising resources for economic development** – a "pragmatic tool" to finance the **Five Year Plans** (not redemption of public debt, BoP adjustment or cutting foreign debt)
  [4.1] Inflation | **Small doses** for productive investment need not be inflationary; an **often-used tool** for financing budget deficits
  [4.2] Why needed after 1947 | **Low tax base**, poor compliance, weak private banking and **low domestic savings**
  [4.3] Objectives | Remedy for depression, raise aggregate demand, subsidies, anti-poverty programmes, PSU losses
  [4.4] Eighth Plan | Largest source of financing public-sector Plan outlay – ahead of external borrowing
[5] table: Period | Use
  [5.0] Independence to **late 1960s** | Little use, to fund plans
  [5.1] **Second Plan** | About **₹1,100 crore** against ₹1,200 crore planned
  [5.2] **Third Plan** | Reliance to be **strictly limited**

### part 1: Methods and Their Inflationary Effect
[0] h: Methods
[1] b: Running down **cash balances** with the RBI; **issue of new currency**; **borrowing from the RBI** (RBI prints notes); also internal and external borrowing, external aid and grants.
[2] b: **Order of preference:** external aid/grants → external borrowing → internal borrowing → **printing currency (last resort, most inflationary)**.
[3] b: **Monetised deficit:** borrowing from the RBI through fresh currency (**high-powered money**); the FRBM Act disallows it in normal times.

[6] b: Deficit financing is **inflationary in character**; it **increases the money supply**; if it fails to raise output it **generates inflation**.

### part 4: Progressive, Regressive and Degressive Taxes; Laffer Effect
[0] table: Type | Feature | Example
  [0.0] **Progressive** | **Tax rate rises as income rises** – average burden rises with income ("ability to pay") | **Income tax**
  [0.1] **Regressive** | Takes a **higher proportion of income from low incomes** than from high | Indirect taxes, GST on essentials
  [0.2] **Proportional** | **One rate for all** incomes | Flat-rate tax
  [0.3] **Degressive** | Rate rises at first, then becomes flat | Progressive slabs with a top flat rate
[1] h: Indian tax system
[2] table: Fact | Key detail
  [2.0] **Indirect taxes** | Regressive – charged at the **same rate for all income groups**
  [2.1] **Income tax in India** | **Progressive and direct**; collected by the Centre; GST, sales tax and motor vehicles tax lack this progressivity
  [2.2] **India's tax system** | **Progressive in nature but overall degressive** (progressive slabs up to a flat top rate; regressive indirect taxes)
  [2.3] **Redistribution** of income | Best achieved by **progressive taxation combined with progressive expenditure**
  [2.4] **Widening the tax base** | Lower exemption threshold, fewer exemptions – raises revenue without changing rates; the **Chelliah approach: lower rates, broader base**
[3] h: Laffer effect
[4] table: Fact | Key detail

  [4.2] Origin | **Arthur Laffer** (1974); term coined by **Jude Wanniski**
