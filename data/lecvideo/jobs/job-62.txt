### part 2: Climate and Rainfall – Two Monsoons
[0] h: Climate and seasons
[1] b: Climate: **tropical (monsoon)**, **hot and humid** – hot summers and rain from both the SW and NE monsoons; summer **March to June**, when the **moisture level is quite high**; Rayalaseema is **semi-arid**.
[2] table: Season | Period | Key detail
  [2.0] Winter | January–February | 13–30 °C
  [2.1] Summer | March to mid-June | Mango showers in April–May (pre-monsoon)
  [2.2] SW monsoon | Mid-June to September | Onset in the 2nd week of June
  [2.3] Retreating / NE monsoon | October–December | October = transition from rains to winter
[3] b: Order: **Winter → Summer → SW monsoon → Retreating monsoon**.
[4] b: **Coldest place:** **Lambasingi** – "Kashmir of AP" (erstwhile **Visakhapatnam**, now **Alluri Sitharama Raju** district). **Hottest:** Rentachintala (Palnadu); heat-wave belt Palnadu, Kurnool, Anantapur.
[5] h: Two monsoons
[6] table: Basis | SW monsoon | NE / retreating monsoon
  [6.0] Share of AP rain | About **two-thirds** (IMD 1989–2018: 57%) | About **one-third (≈33%)**
  [6.1] Wettest months | IMD 1989–2018 means: September (146 mm) > August (143) > **July** (128) > June (96) | October–November
  [6.2] Key region | Most of AP, esp. the north coast | **Nellore** and the southern coastal districts – the **NE-monsoon maximum district** is **Nellore**; Rayalaseema depends on it
[7] b: Most of AP's rain falls in **August–September**, the two wettest months by IMD means.
[8] b: Normal annual rainfall about **860 mm** (574.7 SW + 285.3 NE); river floods mainly in the **SW monsoon (Jul–Sep)**, cyclones in Oct–Dec.
[9] b: India gets about **75%** of its rain from the SW monsoon; the main exception is the **Coromandel coast of Tamil Nadu** (maximum NE-monsoon rain); the Gujarat coast gets none.
[10] b: 2026, an El Niño year: SW-monsoon deficit **51.2%** (1 Jun–25 Aug), all 28 districts deficient.

### part 3: Drought-prone Areas and Drought Management
[0] h: Drought-prone Rayalaseema
[1] b: Rayalaseema lies in the **rain shadow** of the Western Ghats and the Karnataka–Telangana highlands → little SW-monsoon rain → it depends on the **NE monsoon**, whose failure causes **chronic drought** in Rayalaseema and southern Prakasam.
[2] table: Fact | Key detail
  [2.0] **Most frequently drought-affected district** | **Anantapur** (lowest rainfall)
  [2.1] Greatest rural distress | **Prakasam, Anantapur, Kurnool, Kadapa**
  [2.2] Rain-shadow areas | Interior Maharashtra, Karnataka, Telangana, AP and Tamil Nadu
  [2.3] **Chronically drought prone** | Areas under **750 mm** a year
  [2.4] Meteorological drought | Seasonal rain < **75%** of normal
  [2.5] **First artificial-rainfall (cloud-seeding) experiment in AP** | **Atmakur, Anantapur** district
[3] h: Drought proofing
[4] b: **Watersheds in arid areas** = prevention, i.e. stopping a hazard turning into a disaster (relief and assistance are response).
[5] b: A watershed project that raises groundwater but leaves **yields flat** shows that the community **ignored crop diversification and soil management**.
[6] b: Drought prevention: **check dams, desilting village tanks, farm ponds**, **artificial recharge of groundwater**; **planting Eucalyptus** does not help (water-hungry). **Ralegan Siddhi** (Maharashtra, Anna Hazare) overcame its water crisis with **check dams and tanks**.
[7] table: Scheme / project | Key detail
  [7.0] **WDC-PMKSY 2.0** | Degraded and rain-fed land via **integrated natural-resource management**
  [7.1] Pre-Monsoon Dry Sowing | Kurnool, Jul 2026
  [7.2] 100-day water plan | Launched at **Yadiki (Anantapur)**, 6 Apr 2026
  [7.3] **Gandikota** reservoir | On the **Pennar** (Telugu Ganga, YSR Kadapa)

[5] table: Fact | Key detail

[7] b: **Perennial:** fed by **rain + snowmelt** (rainfall and melting ice); Himalayan rivers are **not dry in winter** – SW-monsoon rain alone does not explain this, the snowmelt does.

[9] b: Himalayan rivers show **all three stages** of the erosion cycle (youth, maturity, old age); **Peninsular rivers have reached maturity**.

### part 1: East-flowing vs West-flowing Rivers – Deltas, Estuaries and Direction
[0] h: Why most Peninsular rivers flow east
[1] b: The **Western Ghats** are the main water divide; the Deccan Plateau is **higher in the west and slopes east** to the Bay of Bengal, so most large rivers flow **east**.
[2] b: The Peninsular block was **tilted north-west → south-east** (Himalayan upheaval), giving the whole drainage a Bay of Bengal orientation – about **77%** of India's drainage area drains to the **Bay of Bengal** and about **23%** to the Arabian Sea.
[3] b: **Narmada and Tapi** are the only major Peninsular rivers flowing **west** – they occupy **trough faults (rift valleys)**.
[4] h: Comparison
[5] table: Feature | East-flowing (Bay of Bengal) | West-flowing (Arabian Sea)
  [5.0] Rivers | **Subarnarekha, Brahmani, Baitarani, Mahanadi, Vamsadhara, Nagavali, Godavari, Krishna, Pennar, Palar, Kaveri, Vaigai, Tamraparni** | **Narmada, Tapi, Mahi, Sabarmati, Vaitarna, Mandovi, Kali, Sharavati, Netravati, Periyar, Bharathapuzha, Pamba**
  [5.1] Size | Long and large, dendritic tributaries | Short and swift (except Narmada, Tapi); about **600** such rivers drain the west coast
  [5.2] Mouth | **Extensive deltas** | **No deltas – long, deep estuaries** (natural harbours)
  [5.3] Use | Irrigation | Hydropower
[6] b: **Why no deltas in the west:** steep Western Ghats slope (rapid flow), **short courses** at high speed, **hard rocky terrain**; the Narmada and Tapi filled their fault troughs, so there is little alluvium – sediment is carried away by waves.
[7] b: East-flowing Deccan rivers build deltas although the Deccan is hard rock: **Mahanadi** (delta near **Paradeep**, Odisha), Godavari, Krishna, Kaveri. The **Narmada, Tapi, Mandovi** end in **estuaries** (Narmada estuary ~27 km, south of **Bharuch**).
[8] b: **Subarnarekha** forms an **estuary** between the Ganga and Mahanadi deltas.

### part 1: Soil Conservation – Methods and Practices
[0] h: Meaning
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] **Soil conservation** | Protecting soil from harm – **controlling erosion and keeping fertility high** (NCERT); it prevents **soil erosion**
  [1.1] **Best single method** against erosion | **Afforestation**
  [1.2] Causes of erosion | Deforestation, overgrazing, heavy rain
[2] h: Methods
[3] table: Method | How it works / where
  [3.0] **Contour ploughing / contour bunding** | Ploughing and bunds along contour lines on **hilly, sloping, rainfed land** slow runoff and reduce erosion by water flow
  [3.1] **Terrace cultivation** | Steps on **steep slopes and mountain areas** – the best check on steep slopes
  [3.2] **Shelter belts** | **Rows of trees** that cut wind speed and protect soil (arid western India, coasts); CAZRI dune fixing in Rajasthan
  [3.3] **Check dams, gully plugging** | Trap sediment and stop gully growth
  [3.4] **Strip cropping** | Erosion-resistant strips (grass) alternate with row-crop strips
  [3.5] **Mulching** | Cover of residue / straw on the soil – **retains soil moisture**, reduces erosion
  [3.6] **Crop rotation** | Different crops in sequence on the same land – **increases soil fertility**, cuts pests, prevents degradation; a form of **sustainable agriculture**
  [3.7] Afforestation, regulated forestry, controlled grazing, cover cropping, mixed farming | NCERT remedial measures
[4] table: Fact | Key detail

[0] h: Saline and alkaline soils

## topic group2 4-43 | Hydrological Disasters – Floods, Flash Floods, Cloudbursts, Droughts, Tsunamis, Storm Surges
### part 0: Tsunamis – Meaning, Causes, Behaviour and the 2004 Tsunami
[0] h: Meaning
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] **Tsunami** | Japanese word: **"tsu" = harbour + "nami" = wave** ("harbour wave")
  [1.1] Nature | A **series of waves** of very long wavelength (over 500 km) and long period (10 minutes to 2 hours); the **first wave is often not the largest** – later waves can be bigger
  [1.2] Timing | A **natural disaster**; can strike at **any time of day or night**
  [1.3] Tides | Play **no role** in generating it – it is not a "tidal wave"
[2] h: Causes
[3] table: Cause | Key detail
  [3.0] **Undersea earthquakes** | **Main / most common cause** – sudden vertical displacement of the sea floor at **subduction zones** (shocks above about 7.5)
  [3.1] Submarine or coastal **landslides** | Displace water
  [3.2] **Volcanic eruptions** | E.g. Krakatoa 1883 (about 40 m wave), Anak Krakatau 2018
  [3.3] Explosions, meteorite (bolide) impacts | Rare
[4] b: **Hurricanes / cyclones** raise **storm surges**, not tsunamis.
[5] b: Most tsunamis occur in the **Pacific Ocean** (Ring of Fire – Alaska, Japan, Philippines, Indonesia); India's **coastal States/UTs** are vulnerable to **tsunamis**.
[6] h: Behaviour
[7] table: Deep ocean | Near the coast
  [7.0] Very long wavelength; height under 1 m (a "slight swell" of about 30 cm); speed **hundreds of km/h** (about **700–850 km/h** at 4,000–6,000 m depth) | **Slows down** (speed depends on depth); wavelength shortens, **height rises sharply** (shoaling) – 15 m or more; on coastal plains roughly **50 km/h**
[8] table: Fact | Key detail
  [8.0] Impact | **Less over the open ocean, more near the coast**; ships at sea hardly notice it
  [8.1] **Warning sign: drawdown** | The sea **suddenly recedes** (sea level falls quickly) before the wave hits; also strong quake shaking
  [8.2] **Ocean-crossing speed** | About jet-airliner speed (~800 km/h) – from Los Angeles to Tokyo in about **10 hours**, about a flight's time
[9] h: 2004 Indian Ocean Tsunami
[10] table: Fact | Key detail
  [10.0] Date and cause | **26 December 2004**; **Sumatra-Andaman earthquake, Mw about 9.1** (Indian plate under the Burma plate)
  [10.1] Speed | About 800 km/h, falling to under 70 km/h near shore
  [10.2] Areas hit | South-east coast and islands: **Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh, Puducherry, Kerala, Andaman & Nicobar**; **Odisha**, West Bengal and **Gujarat** unaffected
  [10.3] Worst hit | State **Tamil Nadu**, district **Nagapattinam**; India over **12,000 deaths**; Indira Point submerged
  [10.4] **Mangroves** | A safety hedge: **extensive prop roots** (and pneumatophores) are **not uprooted by storms and tides** and absorb wave energy
  [10.5] Other natural barriers | Sand dunes and coastal cliffs block waves; **open water bodies give no protection**
  [10.6] **Fukushima (Japan), 11 March 2011** | Mw 9.0 Tohoku earthquake + tsunami → nuclear radiation
[11] h: Risk zones and warning
[12] table: Risk | States/UTs
  [12.0] Very high | **Andaman & Nicobar** (near the Sunda subduction zone)
  [12.1] High | Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh, Puducherry, Odisha
  [12.2] Moderate | Kerala, Karnataka, Goa, Maharashtra, Gujarat
[13] table: Fact | Key detail
  [13.0] Sources for India | **Sunda-Andaman** subduction zone (east) and the **Makran** zone (west coast)
  [13.1] **Indian Tsunami Early Warning Centre (ITEWC)** | At **INCOIS, Hyderabad** (MoES); detects tsunamigenic quakes within **10 minutes**; can warn **25 Indian Ocean countries**
  [13.2] **NIOT (Chennai)** | OMNI buoys feed ITEWC
  [13.3] International Tsunami Information Centre | **Honolulu**
  [13.4] World Tsunami Awareness Day | **5 November**
### part 1: Floods – Extent, Causes and Management
[0] h: Nature and extent
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] Flood | Overflow of water onto normally dry land when rivers or other water bodies cannot contain it; **rapid-onset**; can be **mitigated through early warning**
  [1.1] Frequency | The **most frequent / most common natural disaster in India**
  [1.2] **Rashtriya Barh Ayog (National Flood Commission)** | Set up **1976**, report **1980**: of India's **329 mha**, **more than 40 mha** (about **12%** of the area) is flood-prone; a recent estimate is about **49 mha**
  [1.3] Actually flood-affected | About **10%** of India's area
  [1.4] Worst-affected states | **Assam and Bihar**, then **Uttar Pradesh and West Bengal**; **most flood-prone state: Bihar**
  [1.5] Tamil Nadu | Floods in Nov–Jan (retreating monsoon)
  [1.6] People exposed | About **200 million** every year
  [1.7] **Flood control** | A **State subject**; the Centre helps through **FMBAP** (Flood Management and Border Areas Programme, 2021–26)
[2] h: Causes
[3] table: Cause | Effect
  [3.0] **Heavy / prolonged rainfall** | **Primary cause of riverine flooding** in low-lying areas
  [3.1] Cloudburst | Very intense rain over a small area
  [3.2] Snowmelt | Sudden rise in river flow
  [3.3] Siltation (from soil erosion) | Reduces channel depth and capacity
  [3.4] Embankment breach | Water escapes the channel
  [3.5] **Deforestation** | **Human factor**: increases runoff
  [3.6] Floodplain encroachment | Raises exposure
  [3.7] **Poor drainage** | Water accumulates
  [3.8] **Dam failure** | Sudden downstream discharge; **overtopping** = water spills over the crest
  [3.9] Landslide lake | **Pareechu river (Tibet)**, August **2004** – flood threat on the **Sutlej** in Himachal Pradesh
[4] b: Major causes in India: **heavy rainfall, poor drainage, deforestation and dam failure**.
[5] h: Management
[6] table: Structural | Non-structural
  [6.0] Embankments, dams and reservoirs, channel and drainage improvement, diversion channels | **Flood-plain zoning**, flood insurance, flood-proofing, **forecasting and warning**, watershed management
[7] table: Measure | Key detail
  [7.0] **Afforestation** of catchments | **Flood control (mitigation)** – forests **increase infiltration and interception**; wetlands and floodplains store water; mangroves buffer coasts
  [7.1] **Watershed structures** (check dams, percolation tanks) | Mitigate both **drought and flood**
  [7.2] **Forecasting** | **Central Water Commission (CWC)** (Jal Shakti), using **GIS, radar** (Doppler) and **remote sensing / satellite** data
  [7.3] Satellite imagery and GIS | Map the **inundated area**, from which the **number of people affected** and damage are estimated
  [7.4] **Odisha Flood Hazard Atlas** | Built from satellite images of historic flood inundation **2001–2018**
  [7.5] **Assam** | People near rivers and embankments build **houses on stilts** (*chang ghar*) above flood level
[8] h: Committees and programmes
[9] table: Body | Year | Role
  [9.0] **National Flood Control Programme / National Flood Policy** | **1954** | Launched after the 1954 floods
  [9.1] **Rashtriya Barh Ayog** | 1976 (report 1980) | Assessed about **40 mha** flood-prone
  [9.2] **Expert Committee for Scientific Assessment of Flood-Prone Areas** | **July 2012** | Set up by the **Ministry of Water Resources**, under the CWC Chairman
  [9.3] NDMA Guidelines | 2008 (floods), 2010 (urban floods) | Policy

### part 3: Flash Floods, Cloudbursts and GLOFs
[0] h: Flash floods
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] Definition | A flood that **occurs suddenly, unexpectedly and for a short duration**
  [1.1] Causes | **Local precipitation of high intensity** (intense rain, cloudbursts), or dam / glacial-lake / landslide-lake bursts; heavy rain, urban settings and blocked drains are possible causes
  [1.2] Dangers | Little warning time; **debris**; landslides
  [1.3] Vulnerable areas | **Mountains, steep catchments and urban areas**
  [1.4] **IMD** | Flash-flood guidance for India + **Bangladesh, Bhutan, Nepal**
[2] h: Cloudburst
[3] table: Fact | Key detail
  [3.0] Definition | **Sudden, extremely heavy rainfall for a short period over a small area** – **IMD: at least 100 mm (10 cm) in one hour over about 20–30 sq km**
  [3.1] Features | Localised, with **thunder and lightning**; **mostly in hilly areas** (1,000–2,500 m; orographic lifting); **250–300 mm in a couple of hours**; occurs **day or night**
  [3.2] Forecasting | Very hard (highly localised) – Doppler radar, nowcasting
  [3.3] **HKH "monsoon paradox"** | Erratic seasonal rain plus more short, intense cloudbursts
[4] table: Region | Areas | Why
  [4.0] Himalaya | Uttarakhand, HP, J&K, Ladakh, Sikkim | Steep slopes, orographic effect
  [4.1] Western Ghats | Kerala, Karnataka, Maharashtra | Intense rain on the **windward** side
  [4.2] AP Eastern Ghats agency areas | Narrow valleys | Cloudburst **flash floods**
[5] h: GLOFs and events
[6] b: **GLOF**: glacier retreat enlarges a **proglacial (moraine-dammed) lake**, whose dam bursts. **NDMA GLOF Guidelines (October 2020)**: population risk highest in **Sikkim and Jammu & Kashmir**.
[7] table: Event | Year | Key detail
  [7.0] Leh cloudburst | 2010 | Ladakh
  [7.1] **Kedarnath** | June 2013 | Cloudburst + GLOF; **Chorabari (Chaurabari) Glacier** in **Uttarakhand**, **north of the temple**; lake **Gandhi Sarovar**
  [7.2] Operation **Phuktal** | 2015 | Army + NDMA blasted a landslide-debris lake on the **Phuktal river** (Zanskar, **Kargil**) to prevent flash floods
  [7.3] Chamoli glacier burst | 2021 | Uttarakhand
  [7.4] South Lhonak GLOF | Oct 2023 | Sikkim (Teesta III dam)
  [7.5] HP flash floods | 2023 | Unregulated slope construction
  [7.6] Mandi (HP) | 2025 | Nearly 20 times normal rain
  [7.7] **Dharali-Harsil** (Uttarkashi) | 5 Aug 2025 | **Flash flood**; ice-patch collapse on the **Srikanta glacier** (ISRO)
  [7.8] Nepal GLOF floods | 26 Aug 2026 | 26 GLOFs since the 1970s (UNDP)
### part 4: Droughts
[0] h: Nature and types
[1] b: Prolonged deficiency of rainfall or moisture → water scarcity; **slow-onset**; in India drought is **coterminous with the monsoon** (its failure or delay) – **irregular or delayed monsoons** raise drought chances; >80% of rain falls in under 100 days of the SW monsoon.
[2] table: Type | Indicator | Time lag
  [2.0] **Meteorological** | **Prolonged inadequate rainfall, maldistributed over time and space**; rain ≥ 25% below LPA (degree of dryness + length of dry period) | Immediate
  [2.1] Agricultural | Soil-moisture deficit → crop failure | Intermediate
  [2.2] Hydrological | Fall in reservoirs, rivers, aquifers | Weeks to months
  [2.3] Socio-economic | Demand exceeds supply; water / food shortage hits livelihoods and markets | Long-term
  [2.4] Ecological | Ecosystem productivity collapses | –
[3] b: **IMD**: deficiency **26–50%** of LPA = **moderate**; **over 50%** = **severe** drought (no separate "extreme" category).
[4] h: Local names of drought (NCERT)
[5] table: Scarcity | Name
  [5.0] Food grains | **Akal**
  [5.1] Fodder | **Trinkal**
  [5.2] Water | **Jalkal**
  [5.3] All three | **Trikal** (most devastating)
[6] h: Triggers, consequences, regions
[7] table: Fact | Key detail
  [7.0] Triggers | Delayed / erratic monsoon, breaks, heat and evaporation; **El Nino**; also deforestation and over-use of groundwater
  [7.1] Normal SW-monsoon rain | **868.6 mm** (1971–2020)
  [7.2] Long-term consequences | **Groundwater depletion, land degradation, desertification, distress (rural) migration**, debt, lower farm output
  [7.3] Spread | A **recurrent** hazard that can **regularly affect all regions** of India; 68% of cultivable area vulnerable
  [7.4] Regions | **Western Rajasthan, Kutch-Saurashtra, Marathwada-Vidarbha, interior Karnataka, Rayalaseema (AP)**
  [7.5] **Rayalaseema** | **Deep borewells** worsen groundwater loss
  [7.6] Management | NDMA Guidelines 2010; Manual for Drought Management 2016; nodal ministry **Agriculture**
  [7.7] High soil salt concentration | A soil-salinity problem, not a cause of drought

### part 1: Landslides and Avalanches – Causes, Prone Areas, Zonation, Mitigation
[0] h: Where and why
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] Extent | **About 12.6% of India's area (~0.42 million sq km)** (GSI 12.6–15%): **Himalayas, North-East, Western Ghats (incl. Nilgiris)**; parts of the Eastern Ghats
  [1.1] Terrain | **Mostly hilly terrain**, on **steep slopes of rocky mountains**
  [1.2] **Natural causes** | Heavy rain and cloudbursts, earthquakes, fragile young fold mountains, steep slopes
  [1.3] **Man-made causes** | **Deforestation**, **road building**, **shifting cultivation**, mining and quarrying, changes in **land-use pattern**, hill cutting; **forestation is a remedy**
  [1.4] India's vulnerability | **Deforestation + heavy rainfall in mountains** (India has little volcanic activity)
  [1.5] **Himalayas** | Landslide frequency has **increased** with large-scale **mining**, roads and dams
  [1.6] Western Ghats / Nilgiris | Steep escarpments and orographic rain
[2] h: Major events

  [3.0] **Malpa** (Pithoragarh, Uttarakhand) | Landslide – Kailash-Mansarovar pilgrims killed | **18 Aug 1998**
