## topic group2 4-5 | Interior of the Earth – Crust, Mantle, Core; Discontinuities and Seismic Evidence
### part 0: Layers of the Earth and Their Composition
[0] h: Crust, mantle, core
[1] table: Layer | Depth | State | Density (g/cm³) | Made of
  [1.0] **Crust** – outermost layer | Oceanic ~5 km, continental ~30 km (~70 km under mountains) | Solid | 2.8–3.0 | Continental: granite (Si, Al); oceanic: **basalt** (Fe, Mg)
  [1.1] **Mantle** – largest layer | Moho → **2,900 km** | Solid, flows plastically (convection) | ~4.6 | **Silicate minerals** of iron and magnesium (upper mantle mostly silicates)
  [1.2] **Outer core** | 2,900–5,150 km (~2,260 km thick) | **Liquid** iron–nickel | ~10–12 | Fe + Ni
  [1.3] **Inner core** | 5,150–6,371 km (radius ~1,220 km) | **Solid** – immense pressure; P-waves at 11.23 km/s | 13.3–13.6 | Mostly **iron**
[2] table: Fact | Key detail
  [2.0] Mantle | **83% of volume, 68% of mass** → the **largest** layer; "**mixed metals and silicates**" (iron–magnesium silicates)
  [2.1] Core | **16% of volume, 32% of mass**; **mainly iron** (with nickel)
  [2.2] Core's lighter elements | **Silicon, sulphur, oxygen**
  [2.3] **Lithosphere** | **Crust + uppermost solid mantle** (~100 km)
  [2.4] Continental crust | Thicker, older, granitic, lighter
  [2.5] Oceanic crust | Thinner, younger, **basaltic**, denser
  [2.6] **Compositional layering** | Crust, mantle, core
  [2.7] **Mechanical layering** | Lithosphere, asthenosphere, mesosphere, outer core, inner core
[3] h: Suess's chemical layers
[4] table: Suess term | Elements | Feature
  [4.0] **SIAL** | **Si**lica + **Al**uminium | Granite; forms the continents; density ~2.9
  [4.1] **SIMA** | **Si**lica + **Ma**gnesium | Basalt; source of magma and lava
  [4.2] **NIFE** | **Ni**ckel + **Fe**rrum (iron) | Core; very dense (~11); explains Earth's magnetism
[5] b: **Eduard Suess** coined SIAL, SIMA and NIFE; Sial is only silica + aluminium, Nife only metal.
[6] b: **Whole Earth by mass:** **iron 32.1%** > oxygen 30.1% > **silicon 15.1%** > magnesium 13.9% > sulphur 2.9% > nickel 1.8%.
[7] x: Crust: **oxygen** most abundant element, **aluminium** most abundant metal; whole Earth: **iron**.
### part 1: Seismic Waves, Shadow Zone and the Seismograph
[0] h: Seismic waves
[1] table: Basis | P (primary) | S (secondary) | Surface (L)
  [1.0] Type | **Longitudinal**, push–pull, compressional; vibration parallel | Transverse, shear; vibration perpendicular | Along the surface (Love, Rayleigh)
  [1.1] Speed | **Fastest**, arrive first | Slower | Slowest
  [1.2] Medium | Solid, liquid, gas | **Solids only** | –
  [1.3] Damage | Lower | – | **Highest**
[2] b: An earthquake generates **P, S and L (surface) waves** – "P.S.L". P and S are **body waves**.
[3] b: **P-waves are sound (acoustic) waves:** compressional like sound, and they become audible sound when they reach the air.
[4] b: Wave speed changes with density → **reflection and refraction** at boundaries reveal the layers. The **S-wave** behaviour (vanishing in the outer core) is the evidence of a **liquid outer core**.
[5] table: Fact | Key detail
  [5.0] **Shadow zone** | P-waves strongly refracted at the mantle–core boundary → no direct P **~105°–145°**
  [5.1] S-waves | **Absent everywhere beyond ~105°**
  [5.2] Belt **105°–145°** | Gets **neither P nor S**
  [5.3] Each earthquake | Has its **own** shadow zone, set by its epicentre
[6] h: Recording
[7] b: **Seismograph** records seismic waves; it is a **vibrating rod** or pendulum that starts vibrating with the tremors.
### part 2: Earth's Magnetism, Heat and Pressure
[0] h: Magnetic field
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] **Cause** | Electric **currents in the liquid outer core** – convection of molten iron = **geodynamo** (not a giant magnet at the centre)
  [1.1] Role | Shields life from **solar wind and cosmic radiation**; flow changes are linked to **reversals**
  [1.2] **Magnetic axis** | **~11.5°** to the geographic axis
  [1.3] **Magnetic equator** | Field lines **horizontal**, dip = 0; passes near **Thumba** (Kerala) – hence TERLS
  [1.4] Magnetic poles | Field vertical
  [1.5] **North magnetic pole** | First located (1831) on the **Boothia Peninsula, northern Canada**; has since drifted into the Arctic Ocean towards Siberia (~86°N as of 2025, World Magnetic Model 2025)
  [1.6] **Direction of the field** | **Declination** (angle from true north) **and inclination/dip** (angle from horizontal)
  [1.7] **Isogonic lines** | Join points of **equal magnetic declination** (agonic line = zero declination); isoclinic lines join points of equal dip
  [1.8] ESA **Swarm** mission | Outer-core flow under the Pacific switched from westward to eastward
[2] h: Heat, pressure, density
[3] table: Fact | Key detail
  [3.0] With depth | Temperature, pressure and density **all rise**; ~**1°C per 32 m** in the upper crust
  [3.1] Earth's mean density | **5.5 g/cm³** (densest planet)
  [3.2] Geothermal sites | **Puga Valley (Ladakh)**, Manikaran (Himachal), Tattapani (Chhattisgarh), Cambay (Gujarat), **Bakreshwar (West Bengal)**
  [3.3] Diamonds | Rise from the mantle via **kimberlite pipes**; **Madhya Pradesh** ~90% of India's reserves

[0] h: Igneous rocks
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] Formation | **Cooling and solidification of magma or lava**
  [1.1] Name | **Primary rocks**
  [1.2] Features | **No layers, no fossils**; both **crystalline and non-crystalline**

  [1.4] Not igneous | **Quartzite** (metamorphic), **dolomite**

  [2.0] **Intrusive (plutonic)** | Cools **slowly at depth** → **large grains / crystals** | **Granite**, gabbro, diorite, pegmatite

[4] b: **Felsic:** silica-rich, light-coloured, rich in quartz and feldspar, poor in Fe, Mg, Ca. **Mafic (basic):** rich in Fe, Mg, Ca; dark, dense – **olivine and pyroxene** are mafic minerals and **dark** in colour.
[5] h: Deccan Trap basalt
[6] table: Fact | Key detail
  [6.0] Rock | The **Deccan Plateau / Deccan Traps** are made mainly of **basalt** – igneous lava flows
  [6.1] Origin | Fissure eruptions at the end of the Cretaceous, **~65–66 million years ago**, as India passed over the Réunion hotspot

## topic group2 4-7 | Earth Movements – Continental Drift, Sea-floor Spreading, Plate Tectonics and Plate Boundaries
### part 0: Continental Drift – Pangaea and Gondwanaland
[0] h: Wegener's theory
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] **Continental drift** | **Alfred Wegener** (German), **1912**
  [1.1] Drift forces | **Pole-fleeing force** (Earth's rotation) and **tidal force** (Sun and Moon) – **too weak**, so the theory was rejected
  [1.2] Original landmass | **Pangaea**, surrounded by the mega-ocean **Panthalassa**
  [1.3] Split | Into **Laurasia** (north) and **Gondwanaland** (south), separated by the **Tethys Sea**
  [1.4] Date of split | Began **about 200 million years ago**; older books round Pangaea's break-up to **~225 million years** (also given for Gondwanaland's break-up)
  [1.5] Other dates | "Pangaea split 150 million years ago" and "Gondwanaland broke up 65 million years ago" also appear; dates differ between textbooks
[2] table: Name | Present landmasses
  [2.0] Laurasia | North America, Greenland, Europe, Asia (minus India)
  [2.1] **Gondwanaland** | **South America, Africa, India, Australia, Antarctica, Madagascar, Arabia**
[3] b: **India (Peninsular)** was part of **Gondwanaland**, not Laurasia; **North America was not** part of Gondwanaland.
[4] h: Evidence of drift
[5] table: Evidence | Example
  [5.0] Jig-saw fit | South America and Africa; **Bullard's 1964** computer fit
  [5.1] Rock belts | ~**2,000-million-year** belt of the Brazil coast matches **West Africa**
  [5.2] Placer deposits | **Ghana coast gold** has no local source; its source is the gold-bearing veins in **Brazil** – the continents lay side by side
  [5.3] Tillite | **Gondwana** glacial sediments of India have counterparts in **six** southern landmasses
  [5.4] Fossils | **Mesosaurus** (South Africa and Brazil only); **Glossopteris**; lemurs

  [1.2] Basis | **Based on sea-floor spreading**

### part 2: The Indian Plate and the Himalayas
[0] h: The Indian Plate
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] **Palaeomagnetic** data | The Indian landmass has moved **northward** from south of the equator
  [1.1] Link with Africa | The Peninsula was joined to Africa until the **Cretaceous**
  [1.2] **Deccan Traps** | Erupted as India crossed near the equator over the **Réunion hotspot**

### part 3: Sea-floor Spreading and Palaeomagnetism
[0] h: Sea-floor spreading
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] **Sea-floor spreading** | Harry Hess, 1960–62: magma rises at mid-ocean ridges → **new crust is continually added**
  [1.1] Balance | Old crust is destroyed by **subduction** – the two roughly balance
  [1.2] Away from the ridge | The **age, density and thickness** of oceanic crust and its sediments **increase**
  [1.3] Age of ocean floor | Rocks are young; the oldest are ~**200 million years**
  [1.4] **Palaeomagnetism** | Rocks record the field of their time
  [1.5] **Vine and Matthews (1963)** | Normal and reversed bands lie **symmetrically** on either side of the ridge
  [1.6] Bursts | One observed episode – seafloor sank 4 m, spread over 1 m, 160 million m³ of lava in 16 days
[2] b: The Earth's magnetic field **has reversed** every few hundred thousand years; the early atmosphere had no 54% oxygen; living organisms **modified** the early atmosphere.

## topic group2 4-8 | Earthquakes – Causes, Seismic Waves, Measurement, Distribution, Effects
### part 0: Measurement – Magnitude, Intensity, Scales and Instruments
[0] h: Magnitude and intensity
[1] table: Feature | Magnitude | Intensity
  [1.0] Measures | **Energy released at the focus** (from the **amplitude of seismic waves**) | **Degree of shaking and damage** observed at a place
  [1.1] Nature | Quantitative; **one value** per earthquake | Qualitative; **varies from place to place**, falls with distance from the epicentre
  [1.2] Scale | **Richter** (Charles F. Richter, **1935**); now **Moment Magnitude (Mw)** | **Modified Mercalli (MMI)**, **MSK-64**, old Rossi-Forel – all **I to XII**
[2] table: Scale | Key detail
  [2.0] **Richter scale** | Logarithmic, based on the **amplitude of seismic waves**; each whole number = **10× amplitude** and about **31–32× energy** (two steps ≈ 1,000×)
  [2.1] **Moment magnitude (Mw)** | Based on the **rupture area of the fault** and the size of the waves; preferred for large quakes (Richter works best for regional quakes up to ~M5)
  [2.2] **Mercalli scale** | Measures **earthquakes** (intensity, from observed effects) – not cyclones, floods or tornadoes
  [2.3] **MSK-64** (Medvedev–Sponheuer–Karnik) | Intensity scale recommended in **1964** at the UNESCO intergovernmental meeting; used in India (BIS zones are defined in MSK)
  [2.4] **Mercator** | A map projection, not a seismic scale
[3] b: Richter measures magnitude and **Mercalli** measures intensity; in loose usage the Richter scale is also called the scale of earthquake intensity.
[4] table: Magnitude | Effect
  [4.0] below 3.0 | Micro/minor – recorded, rarely felt, **no damage**
  [4.1] 3.0–3.9 | Often felt, very rarely damage
  [4.2] 4.0–4.9 | Light – felt by most, none to minimal damage
  [4.3] 5.0–5.9 | Moderate – damages poorly built houses
  [4.4] 6.0–6.9 | Strong (Latur 1993 6.2–6.4)
  [4.5] 7.0–7.9 | Major (Bhuj 2001 Mw 7.7)
  [4.6] 8.0 + | Great (Assam–Tibet 1950 8.6; Chile 1960 9.5)
[5] h: Instruments
[6] table: Instrument | Use
  [6.0] **Seismograph** | **Detects and records seismic waves**; its readings give magnitude (books also call it the device for intensity)
  [6.1] Seismometer | The sensor (pendulum / spring mass) inside a seismograph
  [6.2] Seismogram | The recording itself
  [6.3] Seismoscope | Only shows that a tremor occurred and its time
  [6.4] Tiltmeter / Creepmeter | Ground tilt / slow slip (creep) along a fault
[7] x: Richter = magnitude; Mercalli/MSK = intensity; seismograph = recording device.

## topic group2 4-11 | Weathering, Mass Wasting and Soil Formation; Denudation and the Cycle of Erosion
### part 0: Weathering – Physical, Chemical and Biological
[0] h: Weathering
[1] table: Term | Meaning
  [1.0] **Weathering** | Natural **breaking down and decay of rocks in situ** (at or near the surface) into fragments, clays and dissolved matter; **no transport** is involved
  [1.1] Erosion | Carries the loosened material away
  [1.2] **Geomorphic processes** | Shape the Earth's surface – weathering, mass wasting, erosion, deposition (endogenic forces build relief, exogenic forces wear it down)
[2] table: Type | Process | What happens / example
  [2.0] **Physical (mechanical)** | **Exfoliation**, frost wedging (freeze-thaw), salt-crystal growth, block disintegration | Breaks rock **without chemical change**; driven mainly by **temperature variation** – typical of hot deserts and cold mountains
  [2.1] Chemical | **Solution** | Soluble minerals (rock salt, gypsum) dissolve
  [2.2] Chemical | **Carbonation** | CO₂ in rain forms **carbonic acid**, dissolves limestone (karst) and stores carbon as bicarbonate
  [2.3] Chemical | **Oxidation** | Oxygen dissolved in water reacts with iron minerals – **rusting** → red/yellow soils
  [2.4] Chemical | **Hydration** | Minerals absorb water and **swell**: anhydrite → gypsum
  [2.5] Chemical | **Hydrolysis** | Water (H⁺/hydronium) reacts with silicates: feldspar → clay; **muscovite → vermiculite** when interlayer **potassium is replaced by hydronium**
  [2.6] **Biological** | Living things | Plant roots widen cracks; burrowing animals (earthworms, termites, rodents); lichens release acids
[3] table: Fact | Key detail
  [3.0] **Climate control** | Chemical weathering is **fastest in hot and humid (wet)** climates – heat and abundant water speed up reactions (tropics); slowest in cold or dry climates
  [3.1] Rain as an agent | Rainwater holds **CO₂ in solution** (→ carbonic acid, carbonation) **and atmospheric oxygen** (→ oxidation) – **both** explain why rainfall weathers rocks
  [3.2] Mechanical weathering | Mainly caused by temperature variation; this does not explain the definition of weathering as in-situ decay
  [3.3] Exfoliation | **Physical**; hydration, oxidation, solution, carbonation, hydrolysis are chemical
  [3.4] Oxidation | A weathering process, not mass wasting
[4] table: Weathering landform | Meaning
  [4.0] Exfoliation dome | Onion-like peeling of granite (unloading or heating-cooling)
  [4.1] Tor | Residual granite blocks standing above the weathered surface where joints are widely spaced
  [4.2] Block disintegration | Physical break-up of jointed rock into blocks
[5] b: **AP:** granite/gneiss weathers to **red soil**; basalt to **black soil**. Glacier-fed Himalayan catchments release CO₂ through **pyrite oxidation** (Sep 2026). Freeze-thaw breaks rocks in the high Himalaya.
[6] x: Hot-humid = fastest chemical weathering; rain = CO₂ + O₂; exfoliation = physical; muscovite → vermiculite = hydrolysis.

  [1.0] **Alluvial fan** | Gently sloping, fan-shaped **coarse alluvium** dropped by (often braided) streams at the **break of slope** (hill foot)

  [1.2] Floodplain | Fine alluvium dropped when the river overflows

## topic group2 4-13 | Major Landforms – Glacial, Arid/aeolian, Karst and Coastal Landforms
### part 0: Karst Landforms – Groundwater Work
[0] h: Karst
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] **Karst** | Landscape made by **groundwater dissolving limestone/dolomite** (carbonation); named after the Karst region of **Slovenia**
  [1.1] Sinkholes | Form in **limestone topography**
[2] table: Landform | Key feature
  [2.0] Lapiaz / karren | Grooved, ridged limestone surface
  [2.1] **Sinkhole (doline)** | Closed funnel depression – the **commonest karst landform**
  [2.2] Uvala | Joined (coalesced) dolines
  [2.3] Polje | Large enclosed karst depression
  [2.4] **Karst lake** | Lake in a polje without surface outlet – covers the floor in wet periods, shrinks by seepage in dry ones, e.g. **Lake Scutari** (Yugoslavia)
  [2.5] Swallow hole | Point where a surface stream disappears underground
  [2.6] **Blind valley** | Valley that **ends abruptly** where its stream sinks (steephead valley) – a **karst** feature
  [2.7] **Stalactite** | Calcium-carbonate deposit **hanging from the cave roof**
  [2.8] **Stalagmite** | Deposit **rising from the cave floor**
  [2.9] Pillar (column) | Stalactite and stalagmite joined
[3] b: Rising size: **sinkhole → doline → uvala → polje**.
[4] b: Stalactites, stalagmites and pillars are **depositional landforms of underground (ground) water** – water dissolves CaCO₃ and re-deposits it as it drips.
[5] table: Landform | Setting
  [5.0] Pot hole | River bed
  [5.1] Blow hole | Coast
  [5.2] Playa | Desert
[6] table: Fact | Key detail
  [6.0] **Springs** | Common in **well-jointed rocks**, **karst (limestone) regions** (resurgences / vauclusian springs) and **tilted strata** where permeable and impermeable beds alternate; rare in arid areas (deep water table)

### part 2: Coastal Landforms
[0] h: Coastal erosion
[1] b: **Main agent of coastal erosion: waves** (also tides, currents, tsunamis, humans). Processes: hydraulic action, abrasion, attrition, solution (saltation is a wind process).
[2] table: Type | Landform | Formation
  [2.0] Erosional | Sea cliff, **notch** | Wave erosion; notch cut at the cliff base
  [2.1] Erosional | Wave-cut platform | Cliff retreat
  [2.2] Erosional | Sea cave → **arch** → **stack** → stump | Weak zones eroded; cave cuts a headland; **arch collapses → stack**
  [2.3] Depositional | Beach | Sand/shingle between tides
  [2.4] Depositional | **Spit** / hook | Ridge joined to land at one end (longshore drift); a hooked spit = hook
  [2.5] Depositional | **Bar** | Ridge across a bay; closes it into a lagoon
  [2.6] Depositional | **Tombolo** | Bar joining an island to the mainland
  [2.7] Depositional | Lagoon | Shallow water cut off by a bar (Chilika, Pulicat)
[3] table: Fact | Key detail
  [3.0] Defences | Soft = beach nourishment, dunes, **mangroves**; hard = seawalls, **groynes** (across longshore drift), breakwaters
  [3.1] Worsen erosion | Dams, harbours and sand mining cut sediment
  [3.2] East coast | Erodes more: soft low alluvium, no rocky cliffs
  [3.3] AP | Severe at **Uppada (Kakinada)** and the Krishna coast (Jul 2026); Pulicat = saltwater, Kolleru = freshwater
[4] h: Coast types
[5] table: Coast | Formed by | Example
  [5.0] Fiord | Drowned glacial valley | Norway
  [5.1] Ria | Drowned river valley; ranges **at right angles** to the coast | NW Spain
  [5.2] Dalmatian | Ranges **parallel** to the coast; long islands | Adriatic (Croatia)
[6] b: **Emergent coast**: **east coast** (Coromandel) – broad plain, few natural ports. West: **Konkan** submerged by faulting; Kerala emergent.
[7] b: Geo-heritage: **Erra Matti Dibbalu** (red dunes, Visakhapatnam) and **Tirumala Silathoranam** (quartzite arch) on the **UNESCO Tentative List (Sep 2025)**.
### part 3: Glacial Landforms
[0] h: Glacial landforms
[1] table: Landform | Meaning
  [1.0] **Cirque (corrie)** | Bowl/armchair hollow at the valley head
  [1.1] Arête | Knife-edged ridge between two cirques
  [1.2] Horn | Sharp pyramidal peak (Matterhorn)
  [1.3] U-shaped valley | Glacier deepens and widens a river valley
  [1.4] **Hanging valley** | Tributary glacier valley left high above the deepened main valley; joins it by a **waterfall** – agent: **glacier** (Yosemite)
  [1.5] Fjord | Drowned glacial U-valley (Norway, Greenland, New Zealand)
  [1.6] **Moraine / till / boulder clay** | **Unsorted glacial debris** deposited by ice (depositional landforms of **glacial activity**): lateral, terminal, medial, ground
  [1.7] **Till / drift plains** | Glacial deposits in gently undulating sheets; some boulder clay plains, e.g. **East Anglia** and the northern **Mid-West of the USA**, form rich arable lands
  [1.8] Drumlin | Egg-shaped till hill; a field = "basket of eggs"
  [1.9] Esker | Winding ridge of **stratified** meltwater sediment from a sub-glacial tunnel
[2] h: Glacial hazards
[3] table: Fact | Key detail
  [3.0] **Hanging glaciers** | Stranded on steep slopes as the main glacier **thins**; collapse → ice-rock avalanches, GLOFs (Nepal, 1 Sep 2026)
  [3.1] Monitoring | From 2025 the **CWC** monitors **2,843** glacial lakes/water bodies (up from 902)
### part 4: Arid (Aeolian) Landforms
[0] h: Wind as the agent
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] Desert landforms | Made **mainly by wind** (deflation, abrasion, deposition), helped by sheet floods
  [1.1] Wind transport | Deflation, **saltation**, surface creep
[2] table: Landform | Process | Key point
  [2.0] **Barchan** | Deposition | **Crescent** dune, horns downwind – e.g. **Thar (Jaisalmer)**
  [2.1] Seif | Deposition | Long narrow (longitudinal) dunes where sand is scarce
  [2.2] Sand dune | Deposition | Accumulation of wind-blown sand
  [2.3] Loess | Deposition | Fine wind-blown silt (Loess Plateau, China)
  [2.4] **Yardang** | **Erosion** | Streamlined ridge parallel to the wind
  [2.5] Zeugen | Erosion | Table-like ridge, hard cap over soft rock
  [2.6] Mushroom rock | Erosion (abrasion) | Base abraded more than top
  [2.7] **Ventifact** | Erosion | Wind-faceted pebble

  [2.9] **Inselberg** | Erosion (residual) | Isolated residual hill (Uluru)
  [2.10] Mesa / butte | Erosion | Flat-topped table hill / smaller remnant
  [2.11] Playa | Evaporation | Dry salt-lake bed
[3] b: **Barchans are depositional**; yardang, inselberg and ventifact are erosional.
[4] b: **Pediment:** gently inclined **rock-cut (erosional)** surface at the foot of mountains in deserts, sometimes with a thin debris cover; an **alluvial fan** is its **constructional** counterpart; pediments extend into **pediplains**.

### part 1: Relief Divisions of the Ocean Floor and Ocean Deposits
[0] h: Four major divisions
[1] table: Division | Depth | Key feature
  [1.0] **Continental shelf** | To about **200 m** (100-fathom isobath); average width about 80 km | "Submerged prolongation of land"; gradient 1° or less; **massive sedimentary deposits** → source of **fossil fuels (most offshore oil)**; richest fishing
  [1.1] **Continental slope** | **200–3,000 m** | Gradient 2°–5°; **canyons and trenches**; marks the end of the continent
  [1.2] Continental rise | Foot of the slope | Apron of sediment
  [1.3] **Deep-sea (abyssal) plain** | **3,000–6,000 m** | **Most extensive** (over 76% of ocean basins); **flattest, smoothest**; **fine-grained sediments** (clay, silt); polymetallic nodules
  [1.4] **Oceanic deeps / trenches** | Deepest | **Active volcanoes and strong earthquakes**; 3–5 km deeper than the surrounding floor
[2] b: Depth order: deeps > deep-sea plains > slope > shelf. The deep sea below 200 m is the **oldest and largest biome**.
[3] table: Fact | Key detail
  [3.0] Shelf | **Submerged**; average depth about **100 fathoms**
  [3.1] Most shelf | **Atlantic** (more than the Pacific); widest single shelf = **Siberian shelf**, about 1,500 km
  [3.2] Narrow shelf | Where high mountains run close and parallel to the coast (Chile, west Sumatra)
  [3.3] **Marine sediments by volume** | About **90%** lie on the continental margins (shelf, slope, rise), which cover only about a quarter of the floor; the deep-ocean floor holds the least
  [3.4] Increasing sediment | Deep-ocean floor, shelves, rises, slopes
[4] h: Ocean deposits
[5] b: **Terrigenous** deposits come **from land** (gravel, sand, silt, **mud**) and lie mainly on **shelves and slopes**; they get **finer away from the shore**; **muds are the finest** terrigenous material; mud **colour depends on chemical content** (blue, red, green muds). **Coastal gravel** is terrigenous.
[6] table: Pelagic deposit | Make-up
  [6.0] Calcareous ooze | Lime shells (**globigerina, pteropods**)
  [6.1] Siliceous ooze | Silica skeletons (**diatoms, radiolarians**); colder, deeper water
  [6.2] **Red clay** | Fine inorganic clay + volcanic dust; **most widespread** deep-sea deposit, largest in the deep **Pacific**; not from lime shells

  [8.0] **West** | **Wide**; broadest off **Gujarat** (and part of Maharashtra); **Mumbai High** oilfield on it, about **160 km** off Mumbai (ONGC)
  [8.1] **East** | **Narrow**; **fewer ports** – **shallow** near-shore water / shelf choked with delta silt, a straight coastline, and frequent **cyclones and storm surges**

### part 2: Measuring Depth and Ocean Zones
[0] h: Measuring depth
[1] table: Term | Meaning
  [1.0] **Bathymetry** | Measuring ocean depth and **mapping the sea floor**; narrower than oceanography
  [1.1] **Fathom** | Unit of **depth** = **6 feet** (about 1.83 m)
