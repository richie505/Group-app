### part 1: Globalisation – Meaning and Impact
[0] h: Meaning
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] Globalisation | **Dismantling barriers** in international trade; interdependence through trade, technology, investment, people and information; **minimum possible restrictions** across borders
  [1.1] Includes | Lower import duties (incl. on capital goods), abolition of import licensing, free FDI flow, MNC entry
  [1.2] Separate measures | **Disinvestment of public-sector equity** (privatisation); greater state control and reduced foreign trade run against it
  [1.3] **Free trade** | Reduction or removal of trade barriers (tariffs, quotas)
  [1.4] Macroeconomy | A stable macroeconomy helps by withstanding shocks
  [1.5] Lower import duty on capital goods | A liberalisation tool that helps firms upgrade technology
[2] table: Liberalisation | Globalisation
  [2.0] Removes domestic controls | Integrates with the world economy
  [2.1] De-licensing, deregulation, import liberalisation | Trade, foreign investment, MNC participation
  [2.2] Greater market orientation | Greater global integration; technology flows
[3] h: Impact
[4] table: Fact | Key detail
  [4.0] Trade | Exports and imports both grew fast – **imports grew faster than exports** (persistent trade deficit)
  [4.1] Domestic and foreign trade | Liberalisation since 1991 → **rapid expansion of domestic and foreign trade**
  [4.2] India's world trade share | 1.78% (1950) → 0.6% (1995); later rose
  [4.3] **Trade/GDP** | Merchandise trade 14.2% of GDP (1990-91) → ~43% (2011-12) – about **three times**
  [4.4] **Largest FDI sources 1991-95** | **USA and Japan**
  [4.5] Export growth 1994-95 | About **18%**
  [4.6] **FII inflows began only after 1992** | FIIs allowed Sept 1992
  [4.7] Net FDI | $44.0 bn (2020-21) → under $1 bn (2024-25) → $7.6 bn (2025-26); gross $94.6 bn
  [4.8] **Services** | IT, outsourcing benefited most; IT exports cushioned the CAD; 1,800+ GCCs earned $38.25 bn of services exports in July 2026
  [4.9] Indian MNCs | Tata, Reliance, Infosys, Sun Pharma
  [4.10] Major impact | Poverty fell but **economic disparities rose**
  [4.11] **ISO 9001/9002** | Adopted for many industries (BIS)
  [4.12] Industrial licensing | Liberalised on 24 July 1991

### part 5: Causes and Indicators of the 1991 Crisis
[0] h: 1980s groundwork (Indira and Rajiv Gandhi, 1984-89)
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] Measures | **Broad-banding of licences**; easier **capital-goods imports**; higher MRTP limit; **FERA relaxed**
  [1.1] Growth | Broke the **Hindu rate**, touched **5.5%** – but **debt-fuelled**
  [1.2] **Rajiv Gandhi** | Some liberalisation began; the 1991 reforms were **far more comprehensive**
  [1.3] Rajiv Gandhi government's legacy | Background research on **economic liberalisation** in the PMO, Commerce and Finance Ministries
[2] h: Long-term causes
[3] table: Cause | Effect / data
  [3.0] **Fiscal profligacy** – the long-term cause | Gross fiscal deficit **5.4%** (1981-82) → **8.0%** (1989-90); average **6.3%** (Sixth Plan) → **8.2%** (Seventh)
  [3.1] **Licence-Permit-Quota Raj** | Heavy controls; **MRTP Act 1970**
  [3.2] PSU losses | Low returns on public investment
  [3.3] Weak exports | World export share **2.2%** (1948) → **0.5%** (1983)
  [3.4] Structural | CADs financed by foreign borrowing since the 1980s, incl. short-term ECBs
[4] h: Immediate triggers
[5] table: Trigger | Effect
  [5.0] **Gulf War** (Aug 1990) | Oil prices doubled; **Gulf remittances dried up**
  [5.1] Oil import bill | **Sharp rise in the oil and petroleum import bill** – the immediate cause of the forex loss
  [5.2] Political instability | Two changes of government, Dec 1989 – June 1991; fall of the **Chandra Shekhar** government
  [5.3] Capital flight | NRIs withdrew deposits; foreign banks stopped lending
[6] b: Road to crisis: 1980s fiscal profligacy → CAD on foreign borrowing → Gulf War shock → **BoP crisis 1991**.
[7] h: Crisis indicators (1990-91 / 1991)
[8] table: Indicator | Value
  [8.0] Forex reserves (June 1991) | **~$975 million** – about **two weeks** of imports
  [8.1] Fiscal deficit | **9.4%** of GDP (Centre + States); Centre's GFD **8.4%** (6.1% in 1980-81)
  [8.2] Current account deficit | **3.1-3.2%** of GDP
  [8.3] Trade deficit | **₹10,644 crore** (3.2% of GDP)
  [8.4] Internal debt of the government | **35%** (1980-81) → **53%** of GDP (1990-91)
  [8.5] GDP growth | **1.1%** (1990-91); below 1% in 1991-92
[9] x: The crisis = a **Balance of Payments crisis** (**high inflation, very low forex reserves, large fiscal deficit**); response = **NEP 1991** and a **Structural Adjustment Programme** with IMF-World Bank loans (~**$7 bn**).

### part 7: Stabilisation vs Structural Adjustment
[0] h: Stabilisation vs structural adjustment
[1] table: Basis | Stabilisation | Structural adjustment
  [1.0] Term | **Short-term** | **Long-term** (medium / long-term efficiency and growth)
  [1.1] Nature | **Demand-side** (demand management) | **Supply-side**
  [1.2] Targets | Inflation, fiscal deficit, BoP | Industrial, trade, financial, investment structures
  [1.3] Fiscal tools | Cut the fiscal deficit: less wasteful spending, subsidy cuts, better tax collection | Tax-sector reform
  [1.4] Monetary tools | **Tight monetary policy** by the RBI to control inflation | Financial-sector reform
  [1.5] Trade / exchange | **Rupee devaluation** to correct the BoP | Tariff cuts, QR removal, export promotion
  [1.6] Industrial | – | **Delicensing**, MRTP relaxation, FDI opening, **disinvestment**
  [1.7] Lead institution | **IMF** | **World Bank** (IBRD)
[2] b: Sequencing: stabilisation first; structural adjustment alongside and after.
[3] h: IMF-World Bank SAPs
[4] table: Fact | Key detail
  [4.0] **Structural Adjustment Programmes** | IMF and World Bank loan packages to **reduce fiscal imbalances and adjust the economy for long-term growth**
  [4.1] Conditions | Austerity, privatisation, deregulation, **currency devaluation**, better tax collection
  [4.2] **Eighth Plan (1992-97)** | Began just after stabilisation and structural adjustment
  [4.3] **Washington Consensus** (ten rules) | Fiscal discipline, tax reform, trade and FDI liberalisation, privatisation, deregulation, property rights; it sought fewer, not more, import quotas
  [4.4] Washington Consensus today | Seen as **obsolete**; blamed for the **1997** and **2008** crises (14 Mar 2026)
### part 8: New Industrial Policy 1991, PSU Reform and National Renewal Fund
[0] h: Industrial Policy (24 July 1991)
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] **Industrial licensing** | **Abolished** for all but a few industries (hazardous and strategic, e.g. hazardous chemicals, aerospace); now only **5-6** industries need a licence
  [1.1] Delicensed firms | **Pricing freedom**, no expansion curbs
  [1.2] Public-sector reserved industries | Cut from **17** (1956 policy) to **8**; later pruned further – now only **atomic energy and railways** (+ specified minerals)
  [1.3] **MRTP** | **Asset limits, pre-entry restrictions and merger / expansion controls abolished**
  [1.4] **FDI** | Automatic approval up to **51%** in high-priority industries (now **100%** in most sectors); NRIs / OCBs up to 100%; **FIIs** allowed into stock markets
  [1.5] **BIFR** | Set up to revive **sick industrial units**
[2] h: National Renewal Fund (February 1992)
[3] table: Fact | Key detail
  [3.0] Purpose | A **safety net** for workers hit by industrial restructuring under the new policy
  [3.1] Objectives | **Retraining and redeployment** of employees affected by modernisation and restructuring; **compensation** of affected employees; counselling
  [3.2] In practice | Mostly paid for **Voluntary Retirement Schemes (VRS)** in central PSUs; later limited to public sector units only

## topic group2 3-57 | Money Supply – M0 to M4, High-powered Money, Money Multiplier, Velocity
### part 0: Money – Functions, Kinds and Key Terms
[0] h: Functions of money
[1] table: Class | Functions of money
  [1.0] Primary | **Medium of exchange**; measure of value (**unit of account**)
  [1.1] Secondary | **Store of value**; standard of deferred payments; transfer of value
  [1.2] Contingent | Basis of credit; distribution of national income; **giving liquidity**
[2] b: **Production of goods** is no function of money.
[3] table: Fact | Key detail
  [3.0] **Desirable attributes of money** | General acceptability, legal-tender status, portability, durability, stable value (a fall in purchasing power is undesirable)
[4] h: Kinds of money
[5] table: Term | Meaning
  [5.0] Fiat money | Notes and coins with **no intrinsic value**; money by government order; also legal tender
  [5.1] Legal tender | Money a **creditor is under compulsion to accept** in settlement of claims; RBI notes **unlimited**, coins **limited** legal tender
  [5.2] Commodity money | Money with **intrinsic value** (gold, silver); a paper note is no commodity money
  [5.3] Near money | Liquid assets that cannot directly pay: **bills of exchange**, time deposits, T-bills, bonds near redemption, savings certificates
  [5.4] Plastic money | **Credit cards** (and debit / prepaid cards), **mainly issued by banks**; used in place of cash; no legal tender
  [5.5] Cheque | Bank (credit) money – an **alternative to cash**; can be refused, so no legal tender
  [5.6] Hard / soft money | Hard = backed by **gold or credible assets**; soft = paper backed by **government bonds**
  [5.7] Bank money / cash | Bank money = **secondary money**; cash = high-powered money
  [5.8] Token coin | **Face value higher than metal value** (rupee coin)
  [5.9] **Digital Rupee** | Legal tender under the RBI Act, amended by the **Finance Act 2022**; not every highly liquid asset is legal tender
[6] h: Key terms
[7] table: Term | Meaning
  [7.0] Seigniorage | **Face value minus cost of producing** currency = the issuer's (**central bank's**) **profit** from issuing notes; a ₹500 note costs about **₹3** (₹2.87–3.09 per piece, Lok Sabha reply 10 Mar 2017)
  [7.1] Gresham's Law | **Bad money drives good money out**: good (full-value) coins are **hoarded, melted and exported**; applies to metallic coins
  [7.2] Velocity | Average number of times a unit of money changes hands in a period; ₹10 used twice = **₹20** of transactions
  [7.3] Required Reserve Ratio | Fixed percentage of deposits every bank must keep as reserves
  [7.4] **Money supply** | **Currency + deposit money** (deposits of the public with banks); no part of a country's wealth (mines, dams, livestock are)
[8] table: Fact | Key detail
  [8.0] **Small coins** | Phased out when their **production cost exceeds face value**
  [8.1] **Currency expansion** | Value of money falls → **higher prices**

### part 2: Monetary Aggregates M1 to M4 and Liquidity Aggregates
[0] h: Monetary aggregates
[1] table: Aggregate | Components | Type
  [1.0] **M1** | **Currency with public + demand deposits with banks + other deposits with RBI** (NCERT: **CU + DD**) | **Narrow money**, most liquid
  [1.1] **M2** | M1 + **post office savings bank deposits** | Narrow
  [1.2] **M3** | M1 + **time deposits with banks** | **Broad money** ("aggregate monetary resources")
  [1.3] **M4** | M3 + all post office deposits (**excluding NSCs**) | Broad, least liquid
[2] table: Fact | Key detail
  [2.0] Origin | **Second Working Group on Money Supply (1977)** → RBI publishes M1–M4 besides Reserve Money
  [2.1] **M3** | Most commonly used broad money = **currency with public + demand deposits + time deposits + other deposits with RBI** (all four)
  [2.2] **M3 − M1 = time deposits** | Time deposits are outside M1; M3 = M1 (not M2) + time deposits
  [2.3] **Liquidity order** | M1 > M2 > M3 > M4; assets: **currency > demand deposits > savings deposits > time deposits** (4-1-3-2)
  [2.4] **Highly liquid asset** | Converted into cash in a very short time with little loss of value
  [2.5] **Demand deposits** | Can be withdrawn instantly; **time deposits** are locked for a fixed period and are the least liquid
  [2.6] **Banks' liabilities** | Deposits are the main liability; **time deposits** = the largest component, far above demand deposits
[3] h: New aggregates (1998)
[4] table: Fact | Key detail
  [4.0] **Third Working Group** (1998) | Chaired by **Y. V. Reddy**: residency-based **NM0–NM3** (separate short-term and long-term deposits)
  [4.1] **L1, L2, L3** | **Liquidity aggregates**: add post-office deposits, term money and CDs of FIs, NBFC public deposits
[5] b: **US money supply, June 1933 (Depression trough) to end-1939:** rose steadily (gold inflows after the 1933 devaluation) – **by about 90%**.
### part 3: Money Multiplier and Credit Creation
[0] h: Money multiplier
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] **Money multiplier** | **Money supply ÷ reserve money (M3 ÷ M0)**; m = (1 + cdr) / (cdr + rdr)
  [1.1] Example | **10**% reserves, no leakages: m = 1/0.10 = 10; ₹100 supports about **₹1,000** of deposits
  [1.2] **Credit creation** | Banks lend beyond their cash; **loans exceeding deposits = credit creation**; capacity depends on **cash reserves** (strictly, a bank lends out its **excess reserves**)
  [1.3] **Interbank transfer** | Brings the receiving bank **reserves**, raising its lending potential
[2] table: Money creator | Creates
  [2.0] RBI | Reserve money (notes)
  [2.1] Government | Coins and ₹1 note
  [2.2] Commercial banks | Deposit money by lending beyond CRR / SLR
[3] h: What moves the multiplier and money supply
[4] table: Factor | Effect
  [4.0] Higher CRR, SLR or cash preference (cdr) | **Lower** multiplier, less credit creation
  [4.1] Stronger **banking habit** | **Higher** multiplier
  [4.2] RBI **buys** G-secs from the public; **Government borrows from RBI** | Money supply **rises**
  [4.3] RBI sells G-secs; public deposits currency in banks | Falls / no rise
  [4.4] **Cash withdrawal** of ₹1 lakh from a demand deposit | **Unchanged** (DD −₹1 lakh, currency +₹1 lakh)
  [4.5] **Raising SLR** | Locks more funds in liquid assets (kept by the bank itself; CRR is kept with the RBI) → fewer loanable funds; an SLR cut frees funds
  [4.6] **CRR increase** | Banks have less to lend

### part 6: High-powered Money (Reserve Money, M0)
