## topic group2 1-0 | Pre-harappan and Early Harappan Cultures – Mehrgarh, Amri, Kot Diji, Kalibangan-i
### part 0: Mehrgarh – Earliest Farming and Settled Life
[0] h: Mehrgarh
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] Location | **Kachhi plain**, Baluchistan, near the **Bolan Pass**
  [1.1] Date | c. **7000 BCE**
  [1.2] Significance | **Earliest agriculture** (wheat, barley), domestication and **settled life** in the subcontinent
  [1.3] Excavator | **J.-F. Jarrige** (1974)
  [1.4] Sequence | Seven periods (I–VII), with remains **from the Stone Age (Neolithic) to the Harappan** period – unlike Amri, Kot Diji or Kalibangan
  [1.5] Nature | A **Neolithic** site, not a Harappan city
[2] table: Period | Evidence
  [2.0] I (c. 7000 BCE) | **Aceramic** Neolithic; mud-brick houses; barley, wheat; sheep, goat, cattle
  [2.1] II | **Pottery** and **cotton** (c. 5000 BCE, the oldest cotton) begin
  [2.2] III onward | **Copper** working, wheel-made painted pottery, beads
[3] h: Life in Neolithic Baluchistan
[4] table: Fact | Key detail
  [4.0] **Burials** | Under or near the houses, flexed, with grave goods, sometimes with **goats**
  [4.1] Crops (Baluchistan and Indus plains) | **Wheat, barley, dates, cotton**
  [4.2] Main animals | **Sheep and goats**
[5] h: Other early villages
[6] table: Early village | Area | Excavator
  [6.0] Kili Gul Muhammad | Quetta valley | W. A. Fairservis
  [6.1] Damb Sadaat | Quetta valley (Early Harappan phase) | W. A. Fairservis

[7] x: **Mehrgarh** = the **earliest settled life** and the **earliest agriculture** in the subcontinent.

## topic group2 1-2 | Ivc Town Planning – Grid Layout, Citadel and Lower Town, Drainage, Great Bath, Granaries
### part 0: Hallmarks of the IVC – Town Planning, Burnt Bricks, Bronze Age
[0] h: Hallmarks of the IVC
[1] b: **Hallmark / specialisation:** **town planning** (urban centres, grid, drains) – the single most distinctive feature of the IVC.
[2] b: **Most significant feature:** **burnt-brick buildings** – burnt bricks in almost all constructions (Egypt used mainly dried bricks).
[3] table: Fact | Key detail
  [3.0] Known for | Town planning; Mohenjodaro and Harappa; agriculture and craft industry (bead, shell, metal factories)
  [3.1] Public buildings | **No temples or palaces** clearly found, unlike Mesopotamia and Egypt
  [3.2] Civic authority | Standard bricks, grid, streets and drains point to a **civic authority**; the absence of palaces or temples does not disprove it
  [3.3] Known | Pillars (Mohenjodaro assembly hall, 20 pillars), wells, drains, bows and arrows
  [3.4] Unknown | The **true arch** (only the corbelled arch) and **iron** – a **Bronze Age** culture; iron came c. 1000 BC (Later Vedic)
[4] h: IVC and Vedic culture
[5] table: Point | IVC (Harappan) | Vedic
  [5.0] Life | **Urban**, planned cities | Rural, pastoral
  [5.1] Script | **Pictographic** (undeciphered) | No script
  [5.2] Metal | Copper, bronze; **no iron** | Iron in the Later Vedic age
  [5.3] Pottery | **Red ware painted in black** | **Painted Grey Ware**
[6] b: **Pottery** places the IVC before the Aryans: the stratified sequence (from Mehrgarh to the black-on-red Harappan ware) lies below the Painted Grey Ware of the Vedic people.
[7] x: The IVC was a **Bronze Age** culture: **no iron**.
### part 1: Wells, Drainage and Water Management
[0] h: Wells and water
[1] table: Site | Feature
  [1.0] **Mohenjodaro** | **About 700 brick wells**, many inside houses – the most wells
  [1.1] **Dholavira** | **Water management**: 16 reservoirs, dams, embankments, channels; some **rock-cut**
  [1.2] Kalibangan | Many houses had their own wells; **wooden drains**
  [1.3] Banawali | **No systematic drainage**
  [1.4] Allahdino (Sindh) | Well irrigation
[2] h: Drainage
[3] b: **Drainage** = the most advanced IVC feature: house drains → sump / cesspit → **covered** street drains (bricks or stone slabs) with **manholes** → **soak pits** or main channels; streets sloped for flow; better than in contemporary Mesopotamia and Egypt.
[4] b: **Lothal:** mud-brick houses but burnt-brick drains.
[5] h: Dholavira reservoirs
[6] table: Fact | Key detail
  [6.0] Water source | The **Manhar and Mansar** rivulets
  [6.1] Largest reservoir | Rock-cut, about **73–79 m long, 7 m deep**
  [6.2] Stepwells | Cut in sandstone = Harappan **rock-cut architecture**
[7] x: **Dholavira** = reservoirs (water harvesting); **Mohenjodaro** = wells.

### part 3: Bricks, Streets and Houses
[0] h: Bricks
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] Material | **Burnt bricks** common; **mud bricks** for fillings and platforms and at **Kalibangan**
  [1.1] Standard ratio | **1:2:4** (thickness : width : length)
  [1.2] Sizes | **Same ratio, different sizes**: house bricks 7.5 × 30 cm, city-wall bricks 10 × 40 cm
  [1.3] **Dholavira** | **Sandstone and limestone** blocks, not baked brick
  [1.4] **Ornamental bricks** | Kalibangan
  [1.5] **Curved bricks** | Chanhudaro
[2] h: Streets
[3] table: Fact | Key detail
  [3.0] **Grid layout** | Streets ran north–south and east–west, crossing at **right angles** and splitting the city into blocks; **broad and straight**
  [3.1] Width | Main streets about **10 m (30 feet)**
  [3.2] **Banawali** | The only city with **radial streets**
[4] h: Houses
[5] table: Fact | Key detail
  [5.0] Plan | Rooms round a **central courtyard**; brick-paved **bathrooms** and toilets; no ground-level windows (privacy)
  [5.1] Entrances | On **side lanes**, not main streets; Lothal is the exception (entrances on the main street)
  [5.2] **Storeys** | Most houses single-storeyed, but **staircases** show many had an upper storey
  [5.3] **Arch** | Only the corbelled arch, never the true arch

[1] table: Fact | Key detail

  [1.3] **Perforated jars** | Large hole at the base, small holes in the wall – probably for **straining liquor**
  [1.4] **Kalibangan** | Six types of pottery
  [1.5] **Lothal** | Painted jar (cunning fox story)
  [1.6] Export | Big storage jars reached Oman
  [1.7] Painting | Found only on pottery (no wall paintings)
### part 2: Lost-wax (Cire Perdue) and Metal Crafts
[0] h: Lost-wax (cire perdue) casting
[1] b: 1. Wax model coated with clay. 2. Heated: the wax melts out. 3. Molten metal poured into the hollow mould. 4. Clay removed.
[2] b: Lost-wax objects: the **Dancing Girl**, the **copper dog and bird of Lothal**, the **bronze bull of Kalibangan**, buffalo and goat figures; the method continued in Late Harappan and Chalcolithic cultures (Daimabad bronzes) and later in **Chola** bronzes such as the **Nataraja**.
[3] h: Metal objects
[4] table: Object | Site | Metal
  [4.0] Dog chasing a deer | Harappa | Bronze
  [4.1] Dog and bird | Lothal | Copper
  [4.2] Bull / ox | Kalibangan | Bronze
  [4.3] Chariot group, buffalo, rhinoceros, elephant | Daimabad | Bronze / copper
  [4.4] Gold foundry, active furnace | Rakhigarhi | Gold
  [4.5] Gold and silver necklaces | Allahdino | Gold, silver
[5] b: Tools: chisels, needles, fishhooks, razors, mirrors, axes; vessels of copper, bronze and **silver**.
### part 3: Terracotta Figurines
[0] h: Terracotta figurines
[1] table: Type | Examples
  [1.0] Mother Goddess | Heavily jewelled female figurines (Harappa) – the most important terracottas
  [1.1] Toys | Toy carts with seated drivers (Chanhudaro), whistles, rattles, birds, gamesmen
  [1.2] Animals | Bulls (humpless bull prominent), birds, dogs, monkeys
  [1.3] Horned god | Clay mask of a horned god (Gujarat, Kalibangan)
[2] b: Terracottas are crude, used by common people as toys or cult objects; seals and metal images were for the upper class.
### part 4: Beads, Faience and Shell
[0] h: Beads, faience and shell
[1] table: Centre | Evidence
  [1.0] Chanhudaro | Bead-making factory, furnace, shell-working, bangles; lipsticks, ink pot; the only city without a citadel
  [1.1] Lothal | Bead workshops; carnelian beads for export
  [1.2] Rakhigarhi | Gemstone factory – 3,000+ semi-precious stones
[2] table: Fact | Key detail
  [2.0] Materials | **Carnelian** (from **Saurashtra** / Bharuch), agate, jasper, **steatite**, lapis lazuli, **faience**, **gold**
  [2.1] Export | Beads went to **Mesopotamia** and the **Persian Gulf**
  [2.2] **Faience** | Ground sand / silica, glazed blue-green: beads, bangles, tiny vessels – called the **world's first glazed ware**; its tie with **mosaic glass** = an early clue to glass-making
  [2.3] Shell work | Chanhudaro shell-ornament factory; Nageshwar, Balakot, Lothal
  [2.4] New techniques | Seal carving (steatite; unicorn the commonest motif) and carnelian working

## topic group2 1-8 | Ivc Religion – Pashupati Seal, Mother Goddess, Tree and Animal Worship, Fire Altars
### part 0: Mother Goddess, Pashupati and Male Deities
[0] h: Mother Goddess – the chief female deity
[1] b: **Mother Goddess (Devi Mata):** terracotta female figurines, heavily jewelled, with fan-shaped head-dresses → **fertility cult**; probably the chief female deity.
[2] b: **Sites:** Harappa, Mohenjodaro, Kot Diji, Kalibangan, Banawali (with the toy plough) and Gujarat sites; Ropar is known for the dog burial instead.
[3] b: **Continuity:** the Mother Goddess cult survives in the later **Shakti cults**. "Spirit and Brahma", "ritual" and the "sacrificial system" are Vedic ideas; the Indus cult centres on the **Mother Goddess**.
[4] h: Pashupati seal – the chief male deity
[5] table: Feature | Key detail
  [5.0] Site | **Mohenjodaro** (steatite seal)
  [5.1] Figure | **Three-faced**, horned head-dress, seated in a **yogic posture**
  [5.2] Animals around | **Elephant, tiger, rhinoceros, buffalo**; **two deer** below the seat; no lion
  [5.3] Identification | **Proto-Shiva / Pashupati** ("lord of animals") – **John Marshall**
  [5.4] Caution | The script is undeciphered, so the reading is an inference from later Shiva worship
[6] b: The **Indus people worshipped Shiva**; Indra and Varuna are Rig Vedic gods; Brahma and Vishnu are later.
[7] b: **Both male and female deities** were worshipped (Mother Goddess + Pashupati).
[8] h: Idol worship
[9] b: **Idol worship began in the pre-Aryan (Harappan) period:** no temple has been found, yet idolatry was practised through figurines and seals. The Rig Vedic Aryans had **no idols or temples**.
### part 1: Tree, Animal and Bird Worship; Fertility Symbols
[0] h: Sacred trees, animals and birds
[1] b: **Pipal:** the tree most often depicted on Indus seals (a deity standing among pipal branches); the motif goes back to the **Early Harappan** phase, and the pipal is still worshipped.
[2] b: **Sacred animals:** the **unicorn** (mythical one-horned animal, the commonest seal motif), the **humped bull** (next in importance), elephant, tiger, buffalo, rhinoceros; cobras and horned deities also appear. The **animals round Pashupati** were probably worshipped too.
[3] b: The peacock is only a pottery motif.
[4] h: Fertility and phallic symbols
[5] b: **Linga and yoni:** conical stones (lingas) and **round pierced stones** (yoni) – fertility objects, possibly early Shaivite elements; found at Harappa.
[6] b: **Swastika:** a seal motif, later an auspicious Hindu symbol; it has no link with the Vedic yajna.
[7] b: **Seals:** mostly of **steatite**, mostly **square**; some with a hole were used as **amulets**.
### part 2: Fire Altars, Great Bath and Ritual Practices
[0] h: Fire altars
[1] table: Site | Evidence
  [1.0] **Kalibangan** (Rajasthan) | A row of **seven fire altars** on a mud-brick platform; a **triangular terracotta cake** with a **horned deity** on one side and a **human pulling a goat by a rope** (animal sacrifice) on the other
  [1.1] **Lothal** (Gujarat) | Fire altar
  [1.2] Banawali, Rakhigarhi | Small pits with terracotta cakes regarded as fire altars
[2] b: **Horned deity on a terracotta cake** → **Kalibangan**.
[3] b: **⚠ Caution:** Harappan fire altars are **not conclusive proof of the later Vedic yajna**.
[4] h: Other ritual evidence
[5] b: **Great Bath (Mohenjodaro):** probably for **ritual purification** (bathing).
[6] b: **Amulets:** holed seals and amulets suggest belief in **ghosts and evil forces**; deities shown in **yogic poses** → the practice of yoga and meditation.
[7] b: **Burials with grave goods** point to a belief in life after death.
### part 3: Nature of Harappan Religion and Continuities
[0] h: Nature of Harappan religion
[1] b: **Character:** a predominantly **secular** civilisation – religion present but not dominant; nature- and **fertility-oriented** worship.
[2] b: **No temple** has been identified anywhere; worship was domestic or open-air (under trees).
[3] b: **Undeciphered script:** every reading of Harappan religion (proto-Shiva, Mother Goddess) is inferred from later traditions.
[4] b: **Continuities into Hinduism:** Shiva / Pashupati, Shakti (Mother Goddess), linga-yoni, pipal and animal veneration, yoga.
[5] b: **Contrast with the Rig Veda:** Vedic religion = **male nature gods and open-air yajnas**, no idols; Harappan = Mother Goddess, idols, animal and tree worship.
[6] b: **Significance:** the earliest material evidence of a Shiva-like deity, a Mother Goddess and fertility cults in India.

## topic group2 1-9 | Decline of the Ivc – Competing Theories; Late Harappan Cultures
### part 0: Late Harappan Phase – Features and Sites
[0] h: Features (c. 1900–1300 BCE, post-urban phase)
[1] b: **Chalcolithic:** tools of **stone and copper**; **no metal objects requiring complicated casting**; people lived in **villages** (agriculture, stock-raising, hunting, fishing).
[2] b: **Lost:** the extraordinary **uniformity** of the Mature phase (pottery, seals, bricks, weights) and its construction techniques, writing, long-distance trade, large public buildings and craft specialisation; pottery became **regionally varied**.
[3] b: **Bhagwanpura (Kurukshetra, Haryana):** Late Harappan houses of **burnt brick**; Late Harappan pottery overlaps with **PGW**; a 13-roomed house (excavated by **J.P. Joshi**).
[4] b: **Iron** was absent in the Mature phase and came only later (PGW, Later Vedic).
[5] h: Regional Late Harappan cultures
[6] table: Culture / site | Region
  [6.0] **Cemetery H** | Harappa region, Punjab (Pakistan)
  [6.1] **Jhukar**, Jhangar | Sindh
  [6.2] **Rangpur** | Kutch, Saurashtra and mainland Gujarat
  [6.3] Late Siswal, **Bhagwanpura** | Haryana
  [6.4] Bara, **Dadheri** | Punjab (India)
  [6.5] **Alamgirpur** | Uttar Pradesh (Meerut)
  [6.6] **Daimabad** | Maharashtra – the southernmost Late Harappan site
  [6.7] Swat | Khyber Pakhtunkhwa
[7] b: **Movement:** settlements shifted **eastward** to the Ganga–Yamuna Doab and **southward** to Gujarat and the Deccan.
### part 1: OCP, Copper Hoards and Harappan–Vedic Continuity
[0] h: OCP and Copper Hoards
[1] b: **Ochre Coloured Pottery (OCP):** the Late Harappan phase of the Ganga–Yamuna Doab; **Copper Hoards** are often found with OCP in the **Doab**.
[2] h: Harappan–Vedic continuity
[3] b: **Continuity view:** the Ghaggar-Hakra identified with the Rigvedic **Sarasvati**; Bhagwanpura's **Late Harappan + PGW** overlap.
[4] b: **Traits that continued:** crafts, pottery, beads, weights, agriculture, wheat and cotton cultivation, proto-Shiva (Pashupati) worship – **not iron tools**, which are post-Harappan.
[5] b: **Did not continue:** the **Harappan script**, which disappeared with the cities.
[6] b: **Rakhigarhi:** the only Harappan site so far to yield **ancient DNA**.
### part 2: Decline Theories – Proponents
[0] h: Decline theories
[1] table: Theory | Proponent(s)
  [1.0] **Aryan invasion** | **Ramaprasad Chanda** (1926), elaborated by **Mortimer Wheeler** (1947) – Indra "**Purandara**", huddled skeletons
  [1.1] **Floods** | **Marshall, Mackay, S.R. Rao**; floods + tectonic uplift – **M.R. Sahni**
  [1.2] **Shift of the Indus course** | **H.T. Lambrick** – Mohenjodaro abandoned
  [1.3] **Climate change / aridity** | **Aurel Stein**, A.N. Ghosh
  [1.4] **Drying of the Ghaggar-Hakra (Sarasvati)** | **D.P. Agrawal and Sood** – Kalibangan, Banawali abandoned
  [1.5] **Ecological imbalance** | **W.A. Fairservis**
  [1.6] **Decline of trade with Mesopotamia** (c. 1900 BCE) | Trade-decline school
[2] b: **Other stresses:** **soil salinity**, deforestation, malaria at **Mohenjodaro**; **Chanhudaro** was flooded several times.
[3] x: **Consensus:** the decline was **gradual and multi-causal** – urban collapse over about **200 years** (c. 1900–1700 BCE) and a long tail to c. 1300 BCE; climate change and the drying of the Sarasvati are the most widely accepted causes.
### part 3: Climate and the 4.2 ka Event
[0] h: Climate and the decline
[1] b: **4.2 ka event:** a sharp weakening of the monsoon about **4,200 years ago**; the same aridity is linked to the fall of **Akkad** and Old Kingdom Egypt.
[2] b: **Deoria Tal (Uttarakhand):** pollen record – the shift in the **oak-to-pine ratio** shows the monsoon **weakened sharply**.
[3] b: **IIT Kharagpur study:** continuous low rainfall for about **900 years** drove the decline and migration.
[4] b: **Ghaggar-Hakra:** the densest cluster of Harappan sites; its drying ended cities like Kalibangan.
### part 4: Invasion Theory and Its Rejection
[0] h: Invasion theory and its rejection
[1] b: **Wheeler's case:** the Rig Veda's fort-breaking Indra (Purandara) and the huddled skeletons of Mohenjodaro.
[2] table: Reason for rejection | Key detail
  [2.0] Dating | The Rig Veda postdates the decline
  [2.1] Skeletons | They belong to different levels – multiple burials, not a massacre
  [2.2] Conflict | No evidence of large-scale conflict between Late Harappans and Indo-Aryans
[3] b: **Rakhigarhi DNA (female, c. 4,600 years ago):** no **steppe pastoralist** component → challenges invasion theories and supports **indigenous continuity**; migration (not invasion) is still argued from other DNA studies.
