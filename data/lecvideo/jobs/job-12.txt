## topic group2 1-110 | Land Revenue Systems – Permanent Settlement, Ryotwari and Mahalwari; Areas and Effects
### part 0: Ryotwari System – Read, Munro, Baramahal; Features and Areas
[0] h: Origin and first areas
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] **First in Andhra** | The **Ceded Districts (Rayalaseema)** by Munro, after his survey (**1802**-07); also dated 1808
  [1.1] **Whole Madras Presidency** | Enforced by Munro as **Governor (1820)**; approved by Lord Hastings
  [1.2] **Bombay** | Extended by Munro's disciple **Mountstuart Elphinstone** after 1818 (Pringle's Ricardo-based survey; later Wingate and Goldsmid)
  [1.3] **Areas** | **Madras**, **Bombay**, **Assam**, Coorg, Burma – about **51%** of British India; South India first, West India (Bombay) second
[2] b: **Devised by:** **Captain Alexander Read** (first experiment) and **Thomas Munro** – also called the **"Munro system"**.
[3] b: **First experiment:** **Baramahal (1792)** – ceded by Tipu after the Third Mysore War (Treaty of Srirangapatnam, 1792); Read Superintendent of Revenue, Munro his assistant (1792-99).
[4] x: First in Madras Presidency = Baramahal; first in Andhra = Rayalaseema; prevalent in South India (Madras).
[5] h: Features
[6] table: Feature | Key detail
  [6.0] Settlement | **Directly with the ryot**, who paid revenue **directly to the Government** – no intermediary
  [6.1] Ownership | Peasant recognised as **owner (proprietor)**
  [6.2] Records | Ryots **registered and given pattas**; land **surveyed and assessed before being taxed**
  [6.3] Rights | Ryot could **sell, mortgage, gift, lease or sublet**; not evicted while the assessment was paid
  [6.4] Assessment | Fixed on the **land, not the crop**; **revised periodically – about every 30 years** (20-30)
  [6.5] Demand | First **50%** of estimated produce; **no automatic remission** after a bad harvest – only occasional reduction
[7] b: Munro: **"The Ryotwari is a system which has always prevailed in India."**
[8] h: Criticism
[9] b: "Many zamindars replaced by **one giant zamindar**" – the State as **ultimate landlord**; revenue very high, enhanced at will, payable even in drought.
[10] b: Arbitrary **'putcut'** assessment where there was no survey; over-assessment, poor records, unclear boundaries.
### part 1: Permanent Settlement (Zamindari) – Cornwallis 1793
[0] h: Origin
[1] b: Warren Hastings' five-year farming (1772) → annual leases → Cornwallis' **decennial (10-year) settlement, 1789** → declared **permanent in 1793**.
[2] table: Fact | Key detail
  [2.0] Introduced by | **Lord Cornwallis, 1793**
  [2.1] Areas | **Bengal and Bihar**, then Orissa, the northern districts of Madras (Circars, 1802) and Varanasi – about **19%** of British India; Madras and Coorg outside it
  [2.2] Planner | **John Shore** (with James Grant)
[3] h: Features
[4] table: Feature | Key detail
  [4.0] Settled with | **Zamindars**, recognised as **owners (proprietors)**; rights **hereditary, transferable, saleable** (alienable, rentable, heritable)
  [4.1] Revenue | **Fixed for ever**; zamindar paid **10/11** of the rental, kept **1/11**
  [4.2] **Sunset Law** | Revenue due by sunset of the date, else the estate was sold
  [4.3] Cultivators | Reduced to **tenants-at-will**; many zamindars **did not issue pattas** – **no official check** on them
  [4.4] **Regulations of 1799 (Haftam) and 1812 (Panjam)** | Let zamindars seize tenants' property without a court
[5] h: Why a fixed, high demand
[6] table: Fact | Key detail
  [6.0] Aims | Stable income for the Company; cheap collection through few zamindars; a **loyal landlord class (political allies)**; hope that **zamindars would invest in land** so that area and yield rose
  [6.1] Drawback | After **1810 prices rose**; zamindars gained, the Company could not raise its share → **rarely extended beyond Bengal**
[7] h: Results
[8] table: Result | Key detail
  [8.0] Ryots | **At the mercy of zamindars**
  [8.1] Zamindars | **Allies of the Company**, owners of the land; took the profits of agriculture
  [8.2] Sales | By 1815 about half of Bengal's estates sold for arrears → new landlords: **urban merchants and moneylenders**
  [8.3] Evils | **Absentee landlordism, sub-infeudation, rack-renting**
  [8.4] **Litigation rose after 1793** | Because of Cornwallis' **judicial reforms (abolition of court fees, separate civil courts)**
  [8.5] **Bengal Tenancy Act, 1885** | Tenants' (occupancy) rights in **Bengal and Bihar**
[9] b: **Chronology:** Permanent Settlement 1793 → Subsidiary Alliance 1798 → Doctrine of Lapse 1848 → Partition of Bengal 1905.
### part 2: Mahalwari and Taluqdari; the Three Systems Compared
[0] h: Mahalwari
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] Devised by | **Holt Mackenzie, 1822** (Regulation VII of 1822) in the **North-Western Provinces of the Bengal Presidency** – the "father" of Mahalwari
  [1.1] **1833** revision | Under **William Bentinck** by **R.M. Bird** (Regulation IX of 1833, "father of land settlement in north India"); carried on by **James Thomason**
  [1.2] **Areas** | **North-Western Provinces (Agra-Oudh), Punjab**, Gangetic valley, parts of Central India – about **30%**
  [1.3] **Unit** | The **mahal** (village or group of villages)
  [1.4] Ownership | **Jointly by the village community**; common ownership = **"Bhai Chara" / "Mahali"**
  [1.5] Collection | Revenue fixed per village, collected by the **village headman (lambardar)**; each cultivator paid his share; **village collectively liable**
  [1.6] Revision | **Periodically revised**
[2] h: Taluqdari
[3] b: **Taluqdari = Awadh (Oudh)**; taluqdar = landlord-middleman.
[4] h: The three systems compared
[5] table: Basis | Permanent | Ryotwari | Mahalwari
  [5.0] Settled with | Zamindar | Individual ryot | Village (mahal)
  [5.1] Owner | Zamindar | Ryot | Village community
  [5.2] Revenue | Fixed for ever | Revised ~30 years | Revised periodically
  [5.3] Area | Bengal, Bihar, Orissa | Madras, Bombay, Assam | NWP, Punjab
  [5.4] Man | Cornwallis, John Shore | Read, Munro | Holt Mackenzie, Bird
[6] b: All three made land **saleable, mortgageable and alienable** – a commodity; different systems in different regions created **different classes** in the peasantry.

## topic group2 1-111 | Economic Impact of Colonial Rule – Drain of Wealth, Deindustrialisation, Commercialisation of Agriculture
### part 0: Drain of Wealth and the Economic Nationalists
[0] h: Meaning
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] **Drain of wealth** | The part of India's wealth carried to **Britain** without **adequate economic or material return** – peculiar to **British** rule
  [1.1] **Began** | With the **Diwani of Bengal (1765)** – Bengal's revenue bought the Company's export "investments"
  [1.2] Form | India usually had a **favourable balance of trade**; the drain took the form of **unrequited exports** (export surplus paid for Home Charges, British office and war expenses)
  [1.3] Size (18th century) | About **2-3% of Britain's national income** and nearly 9% of India's income – helped finance Britain's industrial revolution
[2] h: Components
[3] table: Component | What it covered
  [3.0] Salaries, pensions | Of British civil and military officials, remitted home
  [3.1] Interest | On the public debt raised in **Britain**
  [3.2] Railway guarantee | **5%** assured return to British investors, paid from Indian revenue
  [3.3] Military | British Indian Army and wars abroad – the largest budget head
  [3.4] **Profits** | **Of British capitalists**, merchants, banks, shipping and insurance (a drain in Naoroji's theory)
[4] b: Naoroji: what was drained was a **"potential surplus"** that could have developed India; imperialists claimed that linking India to the world capitalist market was itself modernisation.
[5] h: Dadabhai Naoroji
[6] table: Fact | Key detail
  [6.0] Title | **"Grand Old Man of India"**
  [6.1] Theory | Propounded and popularised the **drain theory**; first stated in **"England's Debt to India" / "England's Duties to India" (1867)**; coined **"moral drain"**
  [6.2] Books | ***Poverty and Un-British Rule in India* (1901)**; ***Poverty of India: Papers and Statistics*** (from his 1876 paper "The Poverty of India"); the phrase **"Un-British"**
  [6.3] **First estimate of per capita income: Rs 20 (1867-68)** | Naoroji
  [6.4] 1880 | Showed that a **jail convict's food and clothing cost about 50% more** than the average Indian's income
  [6.5] Firsts | First Indian elected to the **House of Commons (1892, Finsbury Central)**; first to make **Swaraj** the national demand (Calcutta Congress, 1906)
[7] h: Other economic critics
[8] table: Writer | Work
  [8.0] **R.C. Dutt** | ***Economic History of India*** (2 vols, 1901-03) – economic record of colonial rule; *Open Letters to Lord Curzon* (1900)
  [8.1] **G. Subramania Iyer** | *Some Economic Aspects of British Rule in India* (1903)
  [8.2] **William Digby** | *'Prosperous' British India* (1901)
  [8.3] **M.G. Ranade** | *Essays on Indian Economics*
  [8.4] **R.P. (Rajani Palme) Dutt** | ***India Today*** – stages of colonialism
  [8.5] Ramkrishna Vishwanath | First Marathi economics book (1843) on the drain
  [8.6] Gajula Lakshmi Narasu Setti | Paper ***The Crescent*** (reported Andhra famines); Chennapatnam Swadeshi Sangam
[9] b: The economic critique was the Moderates' greatest contribution and a base of **Swadeshi**; Moderates demanded cuts in **military spending**, lower land revenue, protective tariffs.
### part 1: Stages of Colonialism; One-way Free Trade and Deindustrialisation
[0] h: Stages of colonialism (R.P. Dutt, India Today)
[1] table: Stage | Period | Key detail
  [1.0] Mercantilist (merchant capital) | 1757-1813 | Company monopoly; **mostly purchase of Indian goods** for export, paid from Bengal revenue
  [1.1] Free trade (industrial capital) | 1813-1858 | India a **market for British goods** and supplier of raw materials
  [1.2] Finance capital | 1858 onwards | British capital in railways, plantations, banks
[2] b: India changed from a **leading exporter of manufactures** to a **supplier of raw materials** – an **agricultural colony of manufacturing Britain**.
[3] h: One-way free trade
[4] table: Fact | Key detail
  [4.0] **Charter Act of 1813** | Ended the Company's trade monopoly → **one-way free trade**
  [4.1] Duties | British goods entered India **duty-free / nominal duty**; Indian textiles paid **heavy duties in Britain** (already from the 1720 laws)
  [4.2] **Cotton excise (1894-96)** | On Indian mill cloth, to offset the import duty on British goods; **Suez Canal (1869)**
[5] b: **Calico Acts:** 1700 – ban on imports of printed calicoes; **1721 – ban on (imports and use of) printed cotton textiles**, to protect British wool and silk makers.
[6] b: **Railways:** Built by private British companies with a **5% guarantee from Indian revenue**; carried British goods inland and raw materials out – **accelerated the decay of handicrafts**.
[7] b: **Imperial preference:** Special privileges (lower duties) for **British imports in India**.
[8] b: Karl Marx: British economic policy in India "disgusting"; the British **"inundated the very mother country of cotton with cottons"**.
[9] h: Deindustrialisation
[10] table: Fact | Key detail
  [10.0] Early steps | Weavers coerced (1769 directive on silk; Bolt 1772); Lancashire cloth in Bengal **1786**; by **1850** India took **one-fourth** of British cotton exports
  [10.1] **Industrial Revolution (first half of the 19th century)** | **Indian handicrafts were ruined**; **raw cotton** exports rose
  [10.2] Timing | Intensified **after 1813**, with the end of the Company's monopoly
  [10.3] Other causes | Loss of princely patrons, export of raw materials, transit duties, British privileges (**B.D. Basu**); shipbuilding crushed by the 1813-14 laws
  [10.4] **Share of world manufactures** | About **24.5% (1750)** → under **2% (1900)** (Paul Bairoch)
  [10.5] **No independent industrial development** | **Absence of heavy (capital-goods) industries**; laissez-faire did not promote indigenous capitalism
[11] b: **Foreign trade:** Foreign trade of British India grew **artificially (fostered)** – raw materials out, British manufactures in.
[12] b: **Results:** Urban handicrafts' sudden collapse; **artisans crowded into agriculture**; **Dhaka** (muslin) declined while Calcutta, Madras, Bombay grew.

## topic group2 1-118 | Government of India Act 1919 – Dyarchy, Montagu-chelmsford Reforms; Rowlatt Act
### part 0: Government of India Act, 1919 – Features
[0] h: Origin
[1] b: **Montagu Declaration, 20 August 1917:** "progressive realisation of **responsible government** in India as an integral part of the British Empire" – the first official promise; pressure mainly from the **Home Rule Leagues**.
[2] b: **Edwin Montagu** = Secretary of State for India; **Lord Chelmsford** = Viceroy; the **Montagu-Chelmsford Report, 1918** (**constitutional reforms**) was the **basis of the GoI Act, 1919**.
[3] b: Passed **1919**, in force **1921**; British PM: **David Lloyd George** (1916-22). The Morley-Minto Act is the separate **Indian Councils Act, 1909**.
[4] h: Main features
[5] table: Feature | Key detail
  [5.0] Subject lists | **Central and provincial subjects separated** ("Devolution Rules") – jurisdiction of Centre and provinces clearly defined; **devolution of legislative authority** to provinces
  [5.1] Structure | Still **centralised and unitary** despite devolution; a large part of authority delegated to provinces
  [5.2] Provinces | **Dyarchy** (reserved / transferred) and extension of provincial government; provincial budgets **separated** from the central budget
  [5.3] Centre | **Bicameral** for the first time: **Council of State** (60) + **Legislative Assembly** (145, 104 elected) – **expanded and reconstituted legislatures**
  [5.4] Elections | **Direct elections** for the first time (1909 = indirect); franchise limited by property, tax, education
  [5.5] Electorates | Communal representation **extended** – separate electorates for **Sikhs**, Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians, Europeans (Muslim separate electorates = **1909**)
  [5.6] Executive Council | 3 of the 6 members (other than the C-in-C) Indian
  [5.7] High Commissioner | New office of **High Commissioner for India**, London; first holder Sir William Meyer
  [5.8] PSC | **Section 96(C)** → Public Service Commission; set up **1926** (Lee Commission)
  [5.9] Review | **Statutory commission after 10 years** → Simon Commission (1927, two years early)
  [5.10] Princes | **Chamber of Princes** (Narendra Mandal), 1921
  [5.11] Governor's tenure | No term fixed by the Act; governors (like the Governor-General) appointed for **five years** by practice
[6] h: Reactions
[7] b: Congress special **Bombay session, Aug 1918 (Hasan Imam)** condemned the scheme; **Amritsar (1919)**: "inadequate, unsatisfactory and disappointing".
[8] b: Moderates under **Surendranath Banerjee** (with Sapru, Srinivasa Sastri, Jayakar) left and formed the **Indian (National) Liberal Federation** (1919).
[9] b: **C.R. Das + Tilak** rejected it; **Gandhi + Malaviya**: give it a fair trial; **Krishna Patrika**: "giving with the right hand, taking with the left".
[10] b: **Andhra:** **Nyapati Subbarao**'s 27-member Andhra delegation met Montagu at Madras for an **Andhra province**; **Kasinathuni Nageswara Rao** presided over the Special Andhra Mahasabha, **Guntur, 17 Aug 1918**.
[11] b: **Muddiman Committee (1924):** the British response to the **Swarajist** demand for a review of the 1919 Act.
### part 1: Provincial Dyarchy – Reserved and Transferred Subjects
[0] h: Meaning
[1] b: **Dyarchy (diarchy):** "double rule" (Greek *di-arche*) – the **subjects delegated to the provinces** were divided into two categories, **reserved** and **transferred**.
[2] b: **Introduced by:** the **Government of India Act, 1919** = **Montagu-Chelmsford (Mont-Ford) Reforms** – in the **provinces**; it gave provincial governments more powers. The earlier Acts of **1861, 1892** and **1909** (Morley-Minto) had no dyarchy.
[3] b: **Aim:** partial **responsible government** in the provinces – the transferred half was run by ministers answerable to the legislature; dyarchy divided the administration into two sections.
[4] h: Reserved vs transferred
[5] table: Subjects | Examples | Run by | Answerable to
  [5.0] **Reserved** | **Administration of justice, police, land revenue**, finance, prisons, irrigation, control of the press | **Governor + Executive Council** | Not the legislature
  [5.1] **Transferred** | **Local self-government**, education, public health, agriculture | **Governor + Ministers** | **Provincial legislative council**
[6] b: **Reserved set:** **justice, land revenue, police**; **local self-government** was a transferred subject. **Flaw:** ministers held transferred subjects but **finance stayed reserved**; the experiment was "largely unsuccessful".
[7] b: **Dyarchy at the Centre** = the **1935 Act** (never worked); dyarchy in the **provinces** = **1919**; the 1935 Act abolished provincial dyarchy.
