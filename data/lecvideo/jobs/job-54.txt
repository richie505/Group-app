## topic group2 3-98 | Public Distribution, Procurement and Buffer-stocking; Food Subsidy and Fci Reform
### part 0: Price Terms, Economic Cost and Food Subsidy
[0] table: Term | Meaning
  [0.0] MSP | Floor price announced before sowing (CACP → CCEA)
  [0.1] **Procurement price** | Price at which the government **buys grain for PDS and buffer stocks** (announced separately at harvest from 1966-67; since 1968-69 MSP itself is the effective procurement price)
  [0.2] **Central Issue Price (CIP)** | Price at which the **Centre supplies grain to States** for distribution through fair price / ration shops
  [0.3] FPS retail price | Fixed by **State Governments** (States' role in PDS is operational)
  [0.4] Ceiling price | Maximum price that may be charged – protects consumers
  [0.5] **Economic cost** | **MSP + bonus + procurement incidentals + distribution cost**
  [0.6] **Food subsidy** | **Economic cost minus CIP**
  [0.7] Hidden subsidy | FCI carrying cost financed by borrowing (NSSF loans) instead of budget releases
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] Pooled grain cost (MSP + bonus) | ≈ **two-thirds** of economic cost
  [1.1] Subsidy components | **FCI subsidy + subsidy to DCP States**
  [1.2] **NSSF** borrowing | Ended; subsidy brought on budget in **Budget 2021-22**
  [1.3] Issue prices | Wheat and rice are issued at **uniform CIPs**
  [1.4] Procurement | **Open-ended** (not target-based); MSP is announced for pulses and oilseeds as well as cereals
  [1.5] **PDS issue prices raised** | To **lessen the subsidy burden**
[2] table: Year | Food subsidy (₹ crore)
  [2.0] 1991-92 | 2,850
  [2.1] 1998-99 | **8,700** (CAG 2000: from ~2,800 in 1992-93 = **more than three times**)
  [2.2] **2000-01** | **12,010** total = buffer carrying cost **4,233** + consumer (PDS) subsidy **≈ 7,777** (BE 8,210)
  [2.3] 2024-25 | 1,99,500 (FCI 1,29,089 + DCP States 70,411)
  [2.4] **Union Budget 2026-27** | **₹2,27,629 crore**
[3] b: **Flaws of the food security system:** subsidy burden, **inefficient FCI operations**, limited benefit to the poor.

### part 6: Issue Price of Foodgrains – CIP History, NFSA Prices, Free Grain and OMSS Prices
[0] h: Central Issue Price
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] **Central Issue Price (CIP)** | Price at which the **Centre (FCI)** issues wheat and rice to **States/UTs** for TPDS; fixed by the **Union Government** (Department of Food and Public Distribution); **uniform** across India
  [1.1] **Gap = subsidy** | Low CIP vs high **economic cost** (MSP + bonus + procurement incidentals + distribution cost)
  [1.2] **Economic cost FY 2025-26** | About **₹41.73/kg rice** and **₹29.80/kg wheat** – the whole cost is now subsidy
  [1.3] **Effects of very low CIP** | Higher subsidy, larger offtake, risk of **diversion** to the open market; higher CIP cuts subsidy but lowers offtake
  [1.4] **Retail price** at fair price shops | CIP + transport, handling and **dealer margin**, fixed by **States**
[2] h: CIP history
[3] table: Period | Issue price
  [3.0] **TPDS (1997)** | Dual prices – **BPL** at about **50% of economic cost**; **APL** at (near) **full economic cost** from 2000
  [3.1] **AAY (25 Dec 2000)** | **Rice ₹3/kg, wheat ₹2/kg** for the poorest families
  [3.2] **Frozen CIPs (from July 2002)** | **BPL rice ₹5.65, wheat ₹4.15**; **APL rice ₹8.30, wheat ₹6.10** per kg
  [3.3] Early 2000s | **Issue prices above market prices** cut APL offtake and swelled stocks (about **63 million tonnes, July 2002**)
  [3.4] **NFSA 2013 (Schedule I)** | **Rice ₹3, wheat ₹2, coarse grains ₹1** per kg; revisable later but **not above the MSP**
  [3.5] **Free grain from 1 January 2023** | CIP made **zero** for NFSA beneficiaries under PMGKAY (about **81 crore** people), extended to **31 December 2028**
[4] table: Fact | Key detail
  [4.0] **Entitlement** | **5 kg per person per month** (priority households); **35 kg per family** (AAY)
  [4.1] **Shanta Kumar HLC (2015)** | AAY may keep ₹3/2; **priority households** should pay about **50% of MSP**
  [4.2] **AP** | Free rice, extended to **State-funded rice cards** outside the NFSA list at its own cost
[5] h: Other prices
[6] table: Fact | Key detail
  [6.0] **OMSS reserve prices 2026-27** | Wheat **₹2,600/quintal (FAQ)** (₹2,585 URS); FCI rice to **ethanol distilleries ₹2,320/quintal** (₹2,390 from 1 Nov 2026) – below economic cost
  [6.1] **Bharat brand** | **Bharat Rice** launched **February 2024** at **₹29/kg** through **NAFED, NCCF, Kendriya Bhandar**; Bharat Atta likewise
  [6.2] **PDS sugar** for AAY families | **₹13.50/kg**, with a Central subsidy of **₹18.50/kg**

### part 1: Disinvestment – Meaning, Methods and Strategic Sales
[0] h: Terms
[1] table: Term | Meaning
  [1.0] **Disinvestment** | Sale of government equity in PSEs – reduces equity; control may stay
  [1.1] Privatisation | Ownership + management control to the private sector
  [1.2] **Strategic sale** | A block of shares (usually **50% or more**) **with transfer of management control** to a strategic partner
  [1.3] Minority disinvestment | Govt keeps **>51%** and control
  [1.4] Liberalisation | Removing licences, quotas, controls (no sale)
  [1.5] Complete privatisation | 100% sold – most comprehensive mode
[2] table: Fact | Key detail
  [2.0] **Methods of disinvestment** | Cross-holding, **warehousing**, minority sale, **OFS**, IPO/FPO, **CPSE ETF**, Bharat 22, buyback, **strategic sale** (SLR, CRR, repo are monetary tools)
  [2.1] **Policy recast (1999-2000)** | From token minority sales to **strategic sale** – **majority holding (51%+) sold to a strategic private partner with change in management**
  [2.2] **Rationale** | Poor PSU returns, **fiscal deficit**, efficiency, listing; an **integral part of liberalisation**; it always reduces government equity
  [2.3] Proceeds | Not mainly for repaying external debt
  [2.4] Control | Government keeps control in many CPSEs
  [2.5] **Criticism** | **"Selling the family silver"** – proceeds bridge the **fiscal deficit** instead of building assets; **undervaluation**; lost dividends

### part 3: Causes of Industrial Sickness – Stage-wise, Sector-wise and Present-day Causes
[0] h: Stages
[1] b: Healthy unit → **tendency towards sickness** → **incipient sickness** → **sickness**; early detection at the incipient stage gives the best chance of revival.
[2] h: Stage-wise and internal causes
[3] table: Cause | Key detail
  [3.0] **Planning stage** | Wrong **location**, wrong **technology**, under-estimated project cost, over-optimistic **demand** estimates → **sickness at birth**
  [3.1] **Implementation stage** | **Time and cost overruns** (delays in land, clearances, machinery) – interest burden rises before production starts
  [3.2] **Production / operational** | **Low capacity utilisation**, **obsolete technology**, poor **quality control**, high-cost raw materials, poor maintenance
  [3.3] **Marketing** | Faulty demand forecasting, dependence on a **single buyer**, weak distribution, failure to adapt products
  [3.4] **Financial** | High **gearing** (debt far above equity), **inadequate working capital**, poor inventory and receivables management, **diversion of funds**
  [3.5] **Managerial and labour** | Incompetent or dishonest management, family disputes, **strikes, lockouts**, overstaffing; **internal mismanagement** = single largest cause
  [3.6] **Induced (wilful) sickness** | Promoters **siphon off funds** and let the unit fail to escape dues – "rich promoter, sick company"
  [3.7] **External** | **Power shortages**, raw-material scarcity, **demand recession**, sudden changes in licensing, price control, tax or **import policy**, natural calamities
  [3.8] **1991 liberalisation** | End of licensing and tariff protection exposed inefficient units to **import competition**
[4] h: Sector-wise causes
[5] table: Sector | Cause
  [5.0] **Cotton textiles** | Old looms, powerloom competition, the **1982 Bombay textile strike** (Datta Samant)
  [5.1] **Jute** | Obsolete mills, **synthetic** substitutes
  [5.2] **Sugar** | Cane prices (**FRP/SAP**) vs controlled sugar prices
  [5.3] **PSUs** | Political interference, overstaffing, pricing controls, a **soft budget constraint** (losses always covered by the State)
[6] h: Present-day causes
[7] table: Fact | Key detail
  [7.0] **Twin Balance Sheet problem** | Economic Survey **2016-17** – over-leveraged firms + bad-loan-laden banks
  [7.1] Its roots | Over-optimistic infrastructure investment in the 2004-08 boom, cost overruns, policy shocks such as the Supreme Court's cancellation of **214 coal blocks (2014)**
  [7.2] **MSME causes** | Inadequate and costly credit, weak bargaining power, **delayed payments**
  [7.3] MSMED Act 2006 | Buyers pay MSEs within **45 days**
  [7.4] **Section 43B(h)**, Income-tax Act | Finance Act **2023**, from FY 2023-24 – deduction disallowed if dues are not paid in time
  [7.5] **COVID-19 shock (2020)** | Collapse of cash flows → **ECLGS** (May 2020) for MSMEs
  [7.6] **Early-warning signals** | Delayed repayments, frequent **overdrawing**, falling capacity use, delays in stock statements
  [7.7] RBI stress tags | **SMA-0 (1-30 days)**, **SMA-1 (31-60)**, **SMA-2 (61-90)**, then NPA
  [7.8] **IBC trigger** | Minimum default raised from ₹1 lakh to **₹1 crore** (March 2020); SICA's net-worth test no longer applies
### part 4: Consequences of Industrial Sickness – Banks, Workers, Fiscal Burden and IBC Outcomes
[0] h: Banks and the economy
[1] table: Affected | Consequence
  [1.0] **Banks** | Loans to sick units become **NPAs** → higher **provisioning**, lower profits, **erosion of capital**
  [1.1] Gross NPA ratio | Peaked at about **11.2% (March 2018)**; **1.8% (March 2026)** – a multi-decadal low (RBI FSR, June 2026)
  [1.2] **Credit squeeze** | Stressed banks turn risk-averse and cut credit even to healthy firms – part of the **Twin Balance Sheet** problem
  [1.3] **Locked-up capital** | Land, plant and bank funds stay idle – a high **opportunity cost** for a capital-scarce economy
  [1.4] **Chain effect** | Sick buyers delay payments to **ancillary MSME suppliers** – sickness spreads down the supply chain
  [1.5] **Investors** | Shareholders lose wealth; repeated failures dent **investor and FDI confidence**
  [1.6] **Consumers** | Shortages and higher prices where sick units are major suppliers; greater **import dependence**
[2] h: Workers, regions and the State
[3] table: Affected | Consequence
  [3.0] **Workers** | **Unemployment**, wage and PF arrears, loss of skills; Mumbai's cotton-mill closures after the 1982 strike left **mill lands** idle (later redeveloped)
  [3.1] **Regional decline** | Sick **jute mills** of West Bengal and old textile centres lost jobs and investment
  [3.2] **Social** | Labour unrest, migration, decline of mill towns → safety nets such as the **National Renewal Fund (1992)** and VRS
  [3.3] **Fiscal burden** | Loss of **excise/GST and income-tax revenue**, bailout and takeover costs, **bank recapitalisation**
  [3.4] Recapitalisation | ₹70,000 crore under **Indradhanush** (2015); **₹2.11 lakh crore** plan (**October 2017**)
  [3.5] **State takeovers** | **National Textile Corporation (1968)** took over sick mills; **Sick Textile Undertakings (Nationalisation) Act, 1974** – protected jobs but lowered PSU returns
[4] h: IBC outcomes (to June 2026)
[5] table: Fact | Key detail
  [5.0] CIRPs admitted | About **9,166**
  [5.1] Ended in | **1,484** **resolution plans**; **3,074** **liquidation** – about **2 liquidations per resolution**
