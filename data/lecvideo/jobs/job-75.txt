## topic group2 5-64 | Renewable Energy Sources and Installed Capacities in India – Overall Targets
### part 0: Renewable vs Non-renewable, Conventional vs Non-conventional
[0] h: Classification
[1] table: Basis | Sources
  [1.0] **Renewable** | Solar, wind, **hydro**, tidal, **geothermal**, **biomass**, biogas
  [1.1] **Non-renewable** | **Coal**, **petroleum**, **natural gas**, **nuclear (uranium)**; minerals such as **iron ore**
  [1.2] Conventional | Coal, petroleum, natural gas, hydel, **firewood**, cattle-dung cake
  [1.3] **Non-conventional** | Solar, wind, tidal, geothermal, biomass – by definition **renewable**
[2] table: Fact | Key detail
  [2.0] **Nuclear** | Non-renewable and conventional – uranium is a finite, non-fossil fuel
  [2.1] **Fossil fuels** | Coal, petroleum, natural gas – formed from buried organisms; ultimate source the **Sun**; all non-renewable
  [2.2] Green energy | Sunlight, wind and algae (biofuel); **natural gas** is not green energy
  [2.3] **Petroleum** | Non-renewable raw material of both **plastics** (e.g. toothbrushes) and **gasoline**
  [2.4] **Hydel** | Conventional yet renewable; **hydroelectricity** suits **sustainable development**
  [2.5] **Gobar gas** | Mainly **methane**
  [2.6] **Gasohol** | **Petrol + alcohol** (ethanol)
  [2.7] Geothermal | Non-conventional
[3] h: Not derived from the Sun
[4] b: Wind, hydro, biomass and fossil fuels are stored solar energy; **nuclear** (fission), **geothermal** (Earth's internal heat) and **tidal** (Moon's pull) are not.
[5] b: India promotes renewables (wind, hydro, solar, geothermal, bio, tidal) because they are **sustainable and clean**, unlike finite, polluting fossil fuels.
### part 1: Solar – India's Most Important Alternative Source
[0] h: Solar energy
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] Rank | **Most important non-conventional source** for India, with the **greatest renewable potential**
  [1.1] **Largest storehouse of alternative energy** | Solar – used most in **organic form** (photosynthesis)
  [1.2] Pollution | **No atmospheric pollution** in converting sunlight to electricity; a **solar cell** is more **eco-friendly** than petroleum, forest products or nuclear fission
  [1.3] **Photovoltaic cells** | Convert **solar energy**
  [1.4] **Source** | Radiant energy from the **Sun**
[2] b: Wind and solar are cleaner and greener; India depends heavily on thermal and hydro power, which harm the environment.

## topic group2 5-65 | Solar Energy – Pv vs Thermal, National Solar Mission, Solar Parks, Rooftop Solar, Isa
### part 0: Photovoltaic vs Solar Thermal; Solar Cells
[0] h: Photovoltaic vs solar thermal
[1] table: Point | Photovoltaic (PV) | Solar thermal / CSP
  [1.0] Principle | **Photovoltaic effect**: light → electricity **directly** (semiconductor) | Sun's rays → **heat** → steam turbine → electricity
  [1.1] Output | **Direct current (DC)**; an **inverter** changes it to AC | Turbine-generator gives AC
  [1.2] Heat storage | No | Possible
  [1.3] Examples | Solar panels, satellites | Solar cooker, water heater, parabolic troughs
[2] h: Solar cells
[3] table: Fact | Key detail
  [3.0] **Photovoltaic cells** | = **solar cells**; convert solar energy into electrical energy (Leclanche, dry and voltaic cells are chemical cells)
  [3.1] **Material** | **Silicon** (semiconductor) – crystalline silicon dominant; thin-film (CdTe, CIGS, a-Si) cheaper, bendable, less efficient
  [3.2] Record | IIT Bombay perovskite-silicon tandem **30.2%**
  [3.3] **Artificial satellites** | Get electricity from **solar cells**
  [3.4] **Solar water pumps** | Run **surface and submersible** pumps, **centrifugal and piston** pumps
  [3.5] **Solar constant** | **≈ 1.4 kW/m²** (**1.361**) – solar energy per unit area at one astronomical unit
  [3.6] **Space-based solar power (SBSP)** | Collected in space; ~**99%** uninterrupted supply – suggested as a national goal
  [3.7] **CUF** | Solar about **21%**, wind **36-40%**, coal about **85%**

## topic group2 5-68 | Environment, Ecology and Ecosystem – Components, Food Chains, Energy Flow and Ecological Pyramids
### part 0: Ecosystem – Concept, Biotic and Abiotic Components, Producers and Decomposers
[0] h: Concept
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] **Ecosystem** | A community of organisms **together with the non-living environment** in which they live, interacting as one functional unit (pond, ocean, forest, even an **aquarium**)
  [1.1] Term coined by | British botanist **Sir Arthur G. Tansley in 1935**
  [1.2] **Two components** | **Biotic** (producers, consumers, decomposers) and **abiotic** (energy, water, air, soil, nutrients, climate); evolution is a process, not a component
  [1.3] Nature | A **natural, open system** – energy and matter enter and leave; it has its own **productivity**; it has no fixed, closed boundaries
  [1.4] **Biosphere** | Narrow zone where the **lithosphere, hydrosphere and atmosphere** meet; treated as an **open system** – energy enters as sunlight and leaves as heat; strictly, the Earth is open for energy but nearly **closed for matter**
  [1.5] Interdependence | All components are **interdependent** – a change in one population affects the rest; human activities affect the environment
  [1.6] **Functions** | Energy flow (one-way), nutrient cycling, production, **self-regulation (homeostasis)**
  [1.7] Structure vs dynamics | Producers, consumers and decomposers form the structure; the **food chain** is the **dynamic part**
  [1.8] **Biogeochemical (nutrient) cycle** | Movement of **chemical elements** (C, N, P) through **biotic and abiotic** components – energy does not cycle; **photosynthesis removes CO₂**; the **phosphorus cycle begins with rock weathering**
[2] h: Abiotic components
[3] table: Type | Factors
  [3.0] Energy | Sunlight – primary source and driving force
  [3.1] Climatic | Light, temperature, rainfall, humidity, wind
  [3.2] **Edaphic** | **Soil** – texture, pH, minerals, drainage
  [3.3] Topographic | Altitude, slope, aspect
  [3.4] Materials | CO₂, water, nitrates, phosphates; humus and other organic compounds
[4] table: Fact | Key detail
  [4.0] **Abiotic factor** | Any **non-living** component that affects organisms or their environment – air, water, light (plants, bacteria and animals are biotic)
  [4.1] **Frost** | An **abiotic** stressor of forests (freezes soil moisture, injures tissue); oomycetes, parasites and pathogens are biotic
  [4.2] **Liebig's Law of the Minimum** | Growth is limited by the nutrient in shortest supply
  [4.3] **Shelford's Law of Tolerance** | Both too little and too much limit
  [4.4] **Stenothermal** | Tolerates only a **narrow** temperature range; **eurythermal** a wide range (likewise stenohaline / euryhaline for salinity)
  [4.5] **Cold-blooded (poikilothermic)** | **Frogs**, snakes, lizards, fish, crocodiles – take the temperature of their surroundings
  [4.6] **Water balance** | Organisms must maintain body water; **terrestrial** life needs **fresh water** because it cannot tolerate the high salt of saline water
[5] h: Producers (autotrophs) – trophic level I
[6] table: Fact | Key detail
  [6.0] **Autotrophs** | Make their own food; their **carbon and energy needs are met by photosynthesis** – they take in **CO₂ and water** and, with **sunlight and chlorophyll**, make **carbohydrates**
  [6.1] **Photosynthesis** | Turns **light energy into chemical energy**; needs light – normally daytime sunlight, but it also runs under moonlight or artificial light
  [6.2] Major producers | On land **herbaceous and woody plants**; in water **phytoplankton** / microscopic **algae** and seaweeds
  [6.3] **Chemoautotrophs** | Bacteria of deep-sea vents / active volcanoes oxidise **hydrogen sulphide**; many **archaea** (methanogens) use energy from chemical compounds – also producers
  [6.4] Infrared photosynthesis | Some **bacteria** (green sulphur bacteria) use **invisible infrared** light
  [6.5] Link between biotic and abiotic | **Producers act as intermediaries**; **decomposers** are also called the link, as they return nutrients to the abiotic pool
  [6.6] **Chlorella** | Green alga with the highest efficiency of converting solar energy into chemical energy
  [6.7] **Oxygen** | Most comes from **algae** / marine phytoplankton – ocean phytoplankton and photosynthetic bacteria give **about 50%** of the world's oxygen, more than rainforests; surface water holds far **less** oxygen than air
  [6.8] **Cactus** | Photosynthesises in its green **stem** (leaves reduced to spines)
[7] h: Consumers and decomposers
[8] table: Component | Examples
  [8.0] Primary consumers (herbivores) | Cow, deer, goat, rabbit, caterpillar, **zooplankton**
  [8.1] Secondary consumers | Wolves, frog, fox
  [8.2] Tertiary / top carnivores | Lion, tiger, hawk, eagle
  [8.3] Omnivores | **Man** (primary and secondary consumer; **top-order heterotroph**), bear
  [8.4] **Decomposers** (saprotrophs) | **Bacteria and fungi** (viruses are not decomposers); **recycle nutrients**
  [8.5] **Detritivores** | **Earthworms, millipedes, woodlice**, nematodes
[9] table: Fact | Key detail
  [9.0] **Fungi** | **Heterotrophs** – mostly saprotrophs absorbing food from dead matter; some parasitic or symbiotic
  [9.1] **Parasitic nutrition** | Food from another living organism **without killing it**
  [9.2] **Decomposition** | Breakdown of dead organic matter by **microorganisms (bacteria, fungi)** into simple inorganic nutrients; **bacteria** turn biodegradable waste into compost
  [9.3] Steps | **Fragmentation → leaching → catabolism → humification → mineralisation**; fully decomposed litter = **humus**
  [9.4] Speed | Fast when detritus is rich in nitrogen and sugars and the climate warm and moist; slow with lignin, chitin, cold or anaerobic conditions
  [9.5] Without **decomposers** | **Mineral (nutrient) movement is blocked** – energy flow continues for a while, but nutrients are not recycled
  [9.6] Consumer types | Macroconsumers = phagotrophs; microconsumers = saprotrophs (osmotrophs)
  [9.7] **Azotobacter** | Free-living nitrogen fixer
  [9.8] **Protists** | Unicellular **eukaryotes**; cyanobacteria are photosynthetic prokaryotes
  [9.9] Other adaptations | Reptiles – **stenothermal**; camel – **passes little urine**
[10] h: Types of ecosystem
[11] table: Type | Examples
  [11.0] Natural terrestrial | Forest, grassland, desert, tundra, mountain
  [11.1] Natural aquatic | Pond, **lake**, river, wetland, estuary, ocean
  [11.2] **Artificial (man-modified)** | **Crop field**, aquarium, park, village; **city** = extreme example
[12] x: A **lake / pond** is the classic **self-sufficient (complete, self-regulating) natural ecosystem**; an aquarium, farmland or polyhouse depends on human inputs.
### part 1: Food Chains, Trophic Levels, Food Webs and Biomagnification
[0] h: Food chain
[1] b: **Food chain:** the order in which organisms **feed upon each other** – producer → herbivore → carnivore; energy passes along it. It runs **across species** (not within one species' population) and does not show the **numbers** eaten – that is shown by a pyramid or food web.
[2] table: Chain | Starts with | Example
  [2.0] **Grazing** | **Living green plants** (autotrophs capture energy and pass it to herbivores) | **Grass → grasshopper → frog → snake** (→ eagle); grass → goat → human
  [2.1] **Detritus (DFC)** | **Dead organic matter** (dead leaves) | Leaf litter → earthworm → chicken → hawk
  [2.2] Pond / aquatic | Phytoplankton | → zooplankton → small fish → large fish
  [2.3] Marine | **Diatoms** | → Crustaceans → **herrings**
  [2.4] Grassland | Grass | → grasshopper or rabbit → snake or hawk
[3] b: In water the grazing chain is the main energy conduit; on land more energy flows through the detritus chain.
[4] h: Trophic levels
[5] table: Fact | Key detail
  [5.0] **Trophic level** | The functional position (feeding step) an organism occupies in a food chain
[6] table: Level | Organisms
  [6.0] I | **Producers** – green plants, **phytoplankton, seaweeds, chemosynthetic bacteria** (vents, active volcanoes), algae
  [6.1] II | **Primary consumers / herbivores** – deer, ants, caterpillar, goat, **zooplankton**
  [6.2] III | Secondary consumers – frog, fox; a man eating goat flesh
  [6.3] IV | Tertiary / top carnivores – snake (in grass → grasshopper → frog → snake), hawk, lion
  [6.4] No single level | Decomposers and detritivores recycle all levels
[7] table: Fact | Key detail
  [7.0] **Highest trophic level** | **Carnivores** (top carnivores)
  [7.1] **Man** | Omnivore – **primary as well as secondary consumer**; eating goat flesh makes him a **secondary consumer**
  [7.2] Numbers | **Producers outnumber primary consumers** (energy and numbers fall at each level)
  [7.3] Chain length | A species can occupy more than one level; chains rarely exceed **4–5 levels**
  [7.4] Missing level | "Tiger, grass, snake, frog" has **no herbivore – the second level is missing**
[8] h: Food web and stability
[9] b: **Food web:** a network of interlinked food chains; **more complex → more stable** (alternative feeding paths).
[10] table: Term | Meaning
  [10.0] Resistance | Withstand a disturbance
  [10.1] **Resilience** | **Recover** after a disturbance
  [10.2] Homeostasis | Self-regulation up to a limit through feedback
[11] b: Diverse systems (rainforest, coral reef) are more stable than tundra, desert or monoculture; the **ocean (marine ecosystem) is the most stable ecosystem** – it stays unchanged over long periods. Warming and ocean acidification weaken calcifying plankton and corals, disrupting marine food webs.
[12] h: Bioaccumulation vs biomagnification
[13] table: Bioaccumulation | Biomagnification (biological magnification)
  [13.0] Build-up of a pollutant inside one organism over its life | **Rising concentration of toxicants at each higher trophic level** – maximum in the topmost level
[14] table: Fact | Key detail
  [14.0] What biomagnifies | Only **persistent, fat-soluble, non-biodegradable** pollutants (DDT, mercury, PCBs) – an increase in **toxicants**
  [14.1] DDT (a **chlorinated hydrocarbon**) | Food crop → rat → snake → **hawk** – highest in the hawk; grasshopper → toad → **snake** – highest in the snake; causes **eggshell thinning** and seabird crashes
  [14.2] **Methylmercury** | Minamata disease (Japan)
  [14.3] Pollutant types | Biodegradable = sewage; non-biodegradable = DDT, plastic, asbestos; primary = CO, SO₂, NOx; secondary = O₃, **PAN**, smog
  [14.4] **Carrion** | Dead, often decaying, animal flesh – food of scavengers (vultures, hyenas)
### part 2: Biotic Interactions and Species Roles
[0] h: Biotic interactions
[1] table: Interaction | Sign | Example
  [1.0] Mutualism | +/+ | Lichen, fig–fig wasp, mycorrhiza; bees and flowers
  [1.1] Commensalism | +/0 | Epiphytic orchid on a tree; cattle egret near cattle; suckerfish on shark
  [1.2] Parasitism | +/− | Cuscuta, ticks, lice, tapeworm
  [1.3] Predation | +/− | Tiger–deer; Pisaster eating mussels

  [1.6] Neutralism | 0/0 | Neither affected
[2] table: Fact | Key detail
  [2.0] **Mycorrhiza** | Fungi + roots (mutualism)
  [2.1] **Lichen** | Fungus + alga; **litmus** is a dye extracted from **lichens** (e.g. Roccella)
  [2.2] Coevolved pollinators | Fig–fig wasp, **yucca–yucca moth**, **Ophrys orchid–bee** (sexual deceit)
  [2.3] **Bees and flowers** | **Symbiotic** (mutualistic) – bees get nectar and pollen, flowers get pollinated
  [2.4] **Epiphytes** | Use the host only for **mechanical support**; take moisture and nutrients from air and rain
  [2.5] **Mistletoe** | **Partial (hemi-) parasite** – green and photosynthetic, but draws water and minerals from the host (green plants are **autotrophs**)
[3] h: Species roles
[4] table: Role | Meaning | Example
  [4.0] Keystone | Effect far larger than its numbers | Pisaster sea star (controls mussels), sea otter
  [4.1] Foundation | Creates or shapes habitat | Corals, kelp
  [4.2] Flagship | Charismatic, draws support | Tiger, giant panda
  [4.3] Indicator | Signals environmental health | Lichens (SO₂)

### part 3: Environment, Ecology and Levels of Organisation
[0] h: Environment
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] **Environment** | Sum total of living and non-living components, influences and events surrounding an organism; **biotic and abiotic factors interact**; never static
  [1.1] Composite | **Physical** (land, water, air, nutrients), **biological** (organisms) and **cultural** elements
  [1.2] Term first used in English | **Thomas Carlyle (1828)**
[2] table: Category | Includes
  [2.0] Physical | Lithosphere, hydrosphere, atmosphere; nutrients, water, air
  [2.1] Biotic | Plants, animals, microbes
  [2.2] Cultural | Man-made – social, economic, political institutions
[3] table: Fact | Key detail
  [3.0] Renewable resources | Come from **both** biotic and abiotic parts (sunlight, wind, water)
  [3.1] **Primary functions** | Provide resources, **assimilate wastes**, habitat for biodiversity
  [3.2] Habitat and environment | Every **habitat is an environment**, but an environment may exist without life
  [3.3] **Natural resources** | Coal, petrol (from crude oil), cow dung; **concrete** is man-made
[4] h: Ecology
[5] table: Fact | Key detail

  [6.3] **Ecological transition** | Concept of **John W. Bennett** (*The Ecological Transition*, **1976**)

[7] h: Levels of organisation
[8] b: Smallest to largest: **organism → population → community → ecosystem → (landscape) → biome → biosphere**.
[9] table: Level | Meaning
  [9.0] Population | Individuals of one species in an area
  [9.1] Community | All populations of different species in an area
  [9.2] Ecosystem | Community + its non-living environment; basic functional unit of ecology
  [9.3] Biome | Large region defined by climate + dominant vegetation (rainforest, tundra, desert)
  [9.4] **Biosphere** | Narrow **zone of contact of lithosphere, hydrosphere and atmosphere** where life exists; all ecosystems together

### part 4: Ecotones, Ecogeographic Rules and Plant Adaptations
[0] h: Ecotones
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] **Ecotone** | Transition zone where two communities meet – mangroves (marine–terrestrial), estuary, **grassland (forest–desert)**, river bank; a uniform forest stand has none
  [1.1] **Edge effect** | More species and higher density of some species in the ecotone; they are **edge species**
[2] h: Ecogeographic rules
[3] table: Rule | Trait
  [3.0] Bergmann | Body size – colder = larger
  [3.1] Allen | Colder = shorter ears and limbs
  [3.2] Jordan | Fish vertebrae – colder = more
  [3.3] Gloger | Colouration – warm-humid = darker
  [3.4] **Rensch's rule** | Sexual size dimorphism increases with body size (tested in shorebirds)
[4] h: Plant groups by water and substrate
[5] table: Group | Habitat | Adaptation – example
  [5.0] **Xerophytes** | Dry | Thick cuticle, sunken stomata, spines, CAM, **green photosynthetic stem** – Opuntia (cactus); generally **succulent**; **long, deep roots** to search for **water**; no broad thin leaves
  [5.1] **Mesophytes** | **Moderate** water supply | Neither very dry nor very wet
  [5.2] **Hydrophytes** | Water | Aerenchyma, reduced roots – Hydrilla, lotus
  [5.3] Halophytes | Saline | **Pneumatophores, vivipary** – Rhizophora; salt **filtered out at the roots** (ultrafiltration), salt glands, succulence
  [5.4] Phreatophytes | Groundwater | Deep roots
  [5.5] Lithophytes | Rocks | –
  [5.6] **Cryophytes** | **Snow and ice** | Mostly algae, mosses, lichens – e.g. red snow (Chlamydomonas nivalis)
[6] b: **Cryptophytes** are Raunkiaer's life form with buds below ground or water. Hot desert soil does not promote root growth.
[7] b: **Aerial roots:** **mangroves** have **pneumatophores** (breathing roots) and **stilt roots**; epiphytic **orchids** have aerial (velamen) roots.
[8] h: Parasitic and insectivorous plants
[9] table: Plant | Key detail
  [9.0] **Cuscuta (dodder)** | **Parasitic angiosperm**, stem parasite: leafless, without chlorophyll; haustoria tap the host's **stem** (xylem and phloem)
  [9.1] **Nepenthes** | Pitcher plant
  [9.2] **Utricularia (bladderwort)** | Aquatic insectivorous plant
  [9.3] Drosera | Sundew
  [9.4] **Aldrovanda** | **Free-floating, rootless aquatic insectivorous plant**; the only species in India in the **salt marshes of the Sunderbans**, also in fresh-water ponds and lakes; sensitive **trigger hairs** on the leaf midrib
  [9.5] **Pinguicula (Butterwort)** | **Alpine Himalayas, Kashmir to Sikkim**; stream-sides in **cool boggy places**
  [9.6] Sequoia / Nostoc | Giant conifer / a cyanobacterium
[10] b: Insectivorous plants trap insects for **nitrogen**.
[11] h: Acclimation and adaptation
[12] table: Fact | Key detail
  [12.0] **Acclimation** | **Short-term, individual-level** physiological, anatomical or morphological adjustment to environmental change
  [12.1] **Adaptation** | **Genetic**, multi-generation change in a population
  [12.2] **Acclimatization** | Compensatory adjustment in **natural (outdoor) conditions**, e.g. to **seasonal temperature**; plants acclimatize to rising **CO₂**, so the **CO₂-fertilization effect diminishes** over time
### part 5: Energy Flow, Productivity and the Ten Percent Law
[0] h: Energy flow
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] Direction | **Unidirectional**: **sunlight → producers → herbivores → carnivores**; energy is not cycled
  [1.1] **Solar energy** | Primary source and **driving force** of an ecosystem
  [1.2] **Law of conservation of energy** (first law of thermodynamics) | Energy can be neither created nor destroyed but **can be transformed** – light → chemical energy in photosynthesis
  [1.3] Second law | Each transfer loses energy as **heat**
  [1.4] **PAR** | Under **50%** of sunlight; plants fix about **2–10% of PAR**
  [1.5] Capture by leaves | Green plants capture only about **1% of the sunlight** falling on their leaves
  [1.6] **Total primary production efficiency** | Share of incoming **solar energy captured and used in photosynthesis** – about **1–2%**
[2] h: Ten percent law
[3] b: **Raymond Lindeman (1942)**: only about **10%** of the energy (as food) passes to the next trophic level; about **90%** is lost (respiration, excretion, locomotion, heat) – energy **decreases** at each transfer, hence **4–5** levels.
[4] x: **1%** = solar energy captured by plants; **10%** = energy passed between trophic levels.
[5] h: Productivity
[6] table: Term | Meaning
  [6.0] **GPP** (**Gross Primary Production**) | Total rate of organic matter (solar energy) fixed by producers in photosynthesis
  [6.1] NPP | GPP − producer respiration; available to consumers
  [6.2] **Primary productivity** | Rate at which **solar energy is stored in green plants** as food (autotroph level)
  [6.3] **Secondary productivity** | Rate of **formation of new organic matter by consumers** (heterotroph level)
[7] table: Fact | Key detail
  [7.0] Aquatic primary production | Depends on **light penetration, nutrients and temperature**; phytoplankton are the producers
  [7.1] Highest NPP per unit area | **Tropical rainforest**; estuaries, coral reefs, swamps, mangroves also high
  [7.2] Lowest | **Deserts and open ocean** (ocean total still large by area)
  [7.3] Decreasing productivity | **Mangroves > grasslands > lakes > oceans**; ecotones (mangroves, estuaries) beat their surroundings
  [7.4] **Upwelling** | Brings **nutrient-rich** deep water to the surface – high phytoplankton productivity
  [7.5] NPP (Whittaker & Likens **1975**, g/m²/yr) | Algal beds and reefs ~**2,500**, **tropical rainforest ~2,200**, swamps and marshes ~**2,000**, estuaries ~**1,500**; tropical forests are usually given as highest

### part 7: Habitat, Niche and Population
[0] h: Habitat vs niche
[1] table: Habitat | Niche
  [1.0] Physical place where an organism lives – its "address" | **Functional role** of a species in its habitat – its "profession"
  [1.1] One habitat holds many species | **Unique** to each species – no two species have identical niches
[2] table: Fact | Key detail
  [2.0] **Ecological niche** | Physical space plus the **functional role** in the community – range of conditions tolerated, resources used, role played; concept of **Grinnell**
  [2.1] **Fundamental niche** | Full range of biotic and abiotic conditions and resources a species could use **free from interference of other species**
  [2.2] **Realised niche** | Actual range used under competition
  [2.3] **Niche breadth** | Specialists (**koala** – only eucalyptus) vs generalists
  [2.4] **Resource partitioning** | Competing species coexist by using different parts of a shared resource
  [2.5] **Terrestrial** vs **hydrophytes** | Terrestrial organisms live on land; hydrophytes are aquatic plants (not necessarily rootless)
[3] h: Population attributes
[4] table: Term | Meaning
  [4.0] **Natality** | **Birth** of individuals; rate per unit population per unit time, or **individuals produced per female per unit time**
  [4.1] Mortality | Deaths per unit time
  [4.2] **Density** | **Individuals per unit area** (or volume)
  [4.3] Others | Age structure, sex ratio, dispersal (dominant vegetation is a community trait)
  [4.4] J-curve | Exponential growth, unlimited resources
  [4.5] S-curve | Logistic growth levelling at carrying capacity K
[5] table: Fact | Key detail
  [5.0] Age pyramids | **Triangular = expanding; bell = stable; urn = declining**
  [5.1] **Carrying capacity** | The **maximum population an environment can sustain** for ever **without being degraded**; **dynamic** – changes with resources, climate and technology
  [5.2] Conditions | Resource extraction stays **below** regeneration; **waste within the absorption capacity**
  [5.3] **Bird migration** | Mainly in search of **food and suitable breeding grounds** as seasons change
[6] table: r-selected | K-selected
  [6.0] Many small offspring, little care, short life, unstable habitats – insects, weeds | Few large offspring, much care, long life, near K – elephants, humans

  [2.0] Numbers | Grassland, pond | **Tree ecosystem**; parasitic chain

### part 0: Nitrogen Cycle
[0] h: Reservoir and processes
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] Main reservoir | **Atmosphere (N₂ about 78% of air)**
  [1.1] Plant uptake | As **nitrate**; atmospheric N₂ is not directly usable
  [1.2] **Uptake forms** | **Inorganic nitrates/nitrites** (also ammonium) and some **organic** compounds – **never directly from the air**
  [1.3] **Rain** | Raises soil **nitrogen** – lightning fixes N₂ into nitrogen oxides that rain carries down as nitrates
[2] table: Process | What happens | Agents
  [2.0] **Fixation** | N₂ → ammonia/nitrate (enzyme **nitrogenase**) | Rhizobium, Azotobacter, cyanobacteria (Anabaena, Nostoc), **lightning**, industrial Haber process
  [2.1] **Ammonification** | Organic N in dead matter → ammonia | Decomposer bacteria and fungi
  [2.2] **Nitrification** | Ammonia → nitrite → nitrate | **Nitrosomonas** (to nitrite), **Nitrobacter** (to nitrate)
  [2.3] **Denitrification** | Nitrate → N₂ gas, returned to air | **Pseudomonas, Thiobacillus**
[3] b: Fixation, ammonification and nitrification **add** usable nitrogen to the soil; denitrification **removes** it.
[4] h: Legumes and root nodules
[5] table: Fact | Key detail
  [5.0] **Rhizobium** | Symbiotic, in **legume root nodules** (pulses, pea); **Rhizobium japonicum** – soybean
  [5.1] **Leghaemoglobin** | **Pink/red** pigment in nodules (alfalfa, soybean); binds oxygen to keep it low, since **nitrogenase is destroyed by oxygen**
  [5.2] **Boron** | Necessary for nodule formation
  [5.3] Pea family (Fabaceae/Leguminosae) | Groundnut, horse-gram, soybean; nitrogen-fixing plants: alfalfa, chickpea, clover
  [5.4] **Insectivorous plants** | Grow in **nitrogen-deficient** (often waterlogged) soils and trap insects for nitrogen
[6] h: Biofertilisers
[7] table: Organism | Nature | Crop / role
  [7.0] Rhizobium | Symbiotic, legume nodules | Pulses, pea
  [7.1] **Azotobacter**, Azospirillum | **Free-living** nitrogen fixers | Cereals, millets, non-leguminous upland crops
  [7.2] Blue-green algae (cyanobacteria) | Photosynthetic nitrogen fixers; **heterocysts** | **Rice / paddy**
  [7.3] **Azolla–Anabaena** | Water fern Azolla + Anabaena in its leaves | Water-logged rice fields (Azolla pinnata)
  [7.4] **Frankia** | Actinomycete, **non-legumes** | **Alnus, Casuarina**

[0] h: Terms

### part 5: Succession – Phases, Sequences and Theories
[0] h: Clements' phases (1916)
[1] table: Phase | What happens
  [1.0] 1. **Nudation** | Bare area forms (erosion, landslide, volcano, fire)
  [1.1] 2. **Invasion** | Seeds/spores arrive; **ecesis** (successful establishment), aggregation
  [1.2] 3. Competition and co-action | Unfit species eliminated
  [1.3] 4. **Reaction** | Organisms modify soil, water, light, temperature – **most important phase**
  [1.4] 5. **Stabilisation** | Climax community
[2] h: Sequences
[3] table: Fact | Key detail
  [3.0] **Xerosere** | Crustose lichens → foliose lichens → mosses → herbs and grasses → shrubs → forest; **lichens** (algae + fungi) start succession on bare rock

### part 2: Meaning, Definition and Levels of Biodiversity
[0] h: Meaning
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] Term **"biodiversity"** | Coined by **Walter G. Rosen (1985)**; **popularised by E.O. Wilson** (Tansley coined "ecosystem")
  [1.1] **Biodiversity** | Range / variety of **different species** in an environment; includes **genetic diversity** within populations and the diversity of **species and ecosystems**
  [1.2] **CBD (1992) definition** | Variability among living organisms from **all sources** (terrestrial, marine, other aquatic) and the **ecological complexes** they are part of – **within species, between species and of ecosystems**
[2] h: Levels
[3] table: Level | Meaning / example
  [3.0] **Genetic** | Variation **within a species** – the basic source; all rice varieties are *Oryza sativa* but differ in size, aroma, nutrients; lets a population **adapt and respond to natural selection**
  [3.1] **Species** | Variety of species in an area – **richness + abundance**
  [3.2] **Ecosystem (ecological)** | Variety of habitats / ecosystems in a region; one ecosystem **cannot replace** another
[4] h: Examples
[5] table: Fact | Key detail
  [5.0] **Narrow genetic base** | Cultivated **banana** (seedless, vegetatively propagated Cavendish clones) may be wiped out by disease – a classic case for genetic diversity
  [5.1] **Crop diversity in space and time** | **Polyculture** (mixed / inter-cropping and rotation), not monoculture
  [5.2] Chinese, Americans, Indians, Black Africans | **One species, *Homo sapiens*** (tigers–lions–cats or pigeons–doves–partridges are different species)
  [5.3] **Biomes** | No two are exactly alike: **climate and geographic location** give unique combinations of plants, animals and soils
### part 3: Measuring Biodiversity – Alpha, Beta, Gamma
[0] h: Alpha, beta, gamma
[1] table: Measure | Scale
  [1.0] **Alpha (α)** | **Within one habitat / area** at a local scale – mean species diversity / **species richness** there
  [1.1] **Beta (β)** | **Comparison between ecosystems** – change (turnover) in species composition along a gradient
  [1.2] **Gamma (γ)** | **Overall diversity across the ecosystems of a whole region** (total geographical area)
  [1.3] **Endemism** | Species **restricted to a specific region**
  [1.4] Species richness / evenness | **Number of species** / their **relative abundance** (low evenness = few species dominate)
[2] table: Fact | Key detail
  [2.0] Terms alpha, beta, gamma | **R.H. Whittaker**
  [2.1] Indices | **Shannon-Wiener** and **Simpson**
  [2.2] Forest A (2 tigers, 6 deer, 7 rabbits) vs B (1, 6, 8) | **Same richness; A more even**
[3] h: Other diversity measures
[4] table: Measure | Key characteristic
  [4.0] Phylogenetic diversity | **Evolutionary relationships** between species
  [4.1] Ecological diversity | Diversity of **habitats / ecosystems** in a region
  [4.2] Genetic diversity | Variety of **genetic traits within species** populations
  [4.3] Species diversity | Species **richness and evenness** in a community
  [4.4] **Functional diversity** | Range of **ecological roles** performed by organisms
[5] h: Sampling
[6] table: Fact | Key detail
  [6.0] Field sampling | **Mist nets** capture **birds and bats**; dipnets, creel surveys and seines are aquatic methods
  [6.1] **Sampling terms** | Population value of a characteristic = **parameter**; value from a sample = **statistic**
  [6.2] **Quadrat sampling** | 20 ha = 2,00,000 m²; 1% intensity = 2,000 m²; 2,000 ÷ (20 × 20 = 400 m²) = **5 quadrats**
### part 4: Richness Patterns – Latitude, Altitude, Himalaya
[0] h: Latitude and altitude
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] **Latitudinal gradient** | Biodiversity **increases towards the equator**, falls towards the poles – greatest in **lower latitudes** and, along mountains, **lower altitudes**
  [1.1] Ants | ~**10 species near the North Pole vs ~2,000 at the equator**
  [1.2] Richest ecosystems | **Tropical rainforests** (land), **coral reefs** (sea)
  [1.3] **Tropical rainforests** | Only ~7% of the land but about **half (50%)** of the world's species
  [1.4] **Butterflies** | Maximum species in **tropical countries**
[2] b: Tropics are richest: **long undisturbed evolutionary time (no glaciation)**, stable climate, more solar energy → productivity.
[3] b: **Himalaya** is rich because it is a **confluence of different biogeographical zones** (Palearctic + Indo-Malayan).
[4] h: Species estimates
[5] table: Fact | Key detail

  [5.2] Share | **Over 70%** of recorded species are animals; plants ~**22%**

### part 2: Causes of Biodiversity Loss – Habitat Destruction First

### part 10: Soil Pollution – Sources, Effects and Control in India
[0] h: Meaning and sources
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] **Soil pollution** | **Toxic substances** in soil above natural levels that harm **soil organisms, crops, animals and people**
  [1.1] FAO | A "**hidden reality**" (2018 symposium; Global Assessment of Soil Pollution, 2021)
  [1.2] **Sources** | **Agro-chemicals** (fertilisers, pesticides), **industrial effluents and solid waste**, **mining and smelting**, urban waste and **sewage sludge**, **e-waste**, plastics, **acid rain**, oil spills, radioactive waste
  [1.3] **Main pollutants** | **Heavy metals** (lead, cadmium, mercury, arsenic, chromium), **persistent organic pollutants** (DDT, endosulfan, PCBs), PAHs, excess **nitrates and phosphates**, **microplastics** (plastic mulch)
  [1.4] **Biomagnification** | Persistent pesticides like **DDT** build up along the food chain
[2] h: Diseases
[3] table: Disease | Pollutant
  [3.0] **Itai-itai** (Japan) | **Cadmium**
  [3.1] **Minamata** (Japan, 1956) | **Mercury**
  [3.2] Blackfoot disease, **arsenicosis** (West Bengal) | Arsenic
  [3.3] **Fluorosis** | Fluoride
  [3.4] **Blue baby syndrome** | **Nitrate**
  [3.5] Nerve damage in children | **Lead**
[4] h: India's problems
[5] table: Fact | Key detail
  [5.0] **Fertiliser imbalance** | Recommended **N:P:K = 4:2:1**; heavy urea use in **Punjab–Haryana** distorts it; surplus **nitrate leaches** into groundwater
  [5.1] **Pesticides** | About **67,000 tonnes** used (2024-25); **Insecticides Act, 1968**, through the **Central Insecticides Board and Registration Committee**
  [5.2] **Endosulfan** | Aerial spraying on cashew in **Kasaragod (Kerala)**; **Supreme Court ban, 2011**; listed under the **Stockholm Convention** on POPs (2011)
  [5.3] **Industrial hot spots** | Chromium – **Ranipet (TN)** and Kanpur tanneries; informal **e-waste** recycling (Seelampur, Moradabad) leaks lead and cadmium
  [5.4] **Bhopal Union Carbide** site | **337 tonnes** of toxic waste moved to **Pithampur** for incineration (2025)
[6] h: Remedies
[7] table: Fact | Key detail
  [7.0] **Reclamation of problem soils** | **Acidic soils → lime**; **alkaline / sodic soils → gypsum**
  [7.1] **Remediation methods** | Bioremediation, **phytoremediation**, soil washing, immobilisation, organic amendments (compost, biochar)
  [7.2] Law | **India has no separate soil-pollution law** – covered by the **Environment (Protection) Act, 1986** and waste rules (Hazardous Waste Rules 2016, SWM Rules)
  [7.3] **Soil Health Card Scheme** | **19 Feb 2015, Suratgarh (Rajasthan)**; **12 parameters** (N, P, K, S, Zn, Fe, Cu, Mn, B, pH, EC, organic carbon); **over 25 crore cards**; about **8,272 soil-testing labs**; since **2022-23** '**Soil Health and Fertility**' under **RKVY**
  [7.4] **Neem-coated urea** | 100% from 2015; slows nitrogen release
  [7.5] **IFFCO Nano Urea** (2021) | The **world's first**
  [7.6] **PM-PRANAM (2023)** | States that cut chemical-fertiliser use get **50% of the subsidy saved** as a grant
  [7.7] **National Mission on Natural Farming** (Cabinet, Nov 2024) | **₹2,481 crore**, **1 crore farmers**, 7.5 lakh hectares
  [7.8] **AP: APCNF** | Community-managed Natural Farming by **Rythu Sadhikara Samstha** since **2016**; **Gulbenkian Prize for Humanity 2024**
  [7.9] **World Soil Day** | **5 December** (FAO; first observed 2014); **International Year of Soils: 2015**
