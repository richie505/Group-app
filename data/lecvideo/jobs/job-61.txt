## topic group2 4-25 | Atmosphere – Structure (layers) and Composition
### part 0: Ozone Layer – Location, Formation and UV Shield
[0] h: Where the ozone layer lies
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] **Ozone (O₃)** | **Three oxygen atoms**
  [1.1] Location | Mainly the **stratosphere** – maximum concentration there
  [1.2] Height | **Lower stratosphere**: main layer **~15–30 km**, densest **~15–35 km**; commonly given as **15–20 km above the surface**
  [1.3] Share | About **90%** of all ozone is **stratospheric**, ~10% tropospheric
  [1.4] Formation (**Chapman cycle**) | **UV + O₂ → O + O; O + O₂ → O₃**; O₃ absorbs UV and splits again
  [1.5] Measurement | Column ozone in **Dobson Units (DU)**; 1 DU = 0.01 mm of pure ozone at 0°C, 1 atm
[2] h: What it does
[3] table: Fact | Key detail
  [3.0] Role | **Absorbs harmful ultraviolet (UV) radiation** of the Sun – **Earth's shield**
  [3.1] What it does not absorb | Cosmic rays, infrared, visible light, microwaves – only **UV**
  [3.2] Excess UV | **Skin (dermal) cancer**, **cataract**, weaker immunity, lower crop yield
  [3.3] Why vital | Ozone is **essential for biotic life** – it screens high-energy radiation
  [3.4] Stratosphere | **Warms with height** – ozone turns absorbed UV into heat
[4] table: UV band | Wavelength | Ozone layer
  [4.0] UV-A | 315–400 nm | Mostly reaches the surface
  [4.1] UV-B | 280–315 nm | Mostly absorbed, part reaches the ground
  [4.2] **UV-C** | 100–280 nm (shortest, most harmful) | **Completely absorbed**
[5] table: Ozone | Role
  [5.0] Stratospheric | Protective shield – good
  [5.1] **Ground-level (tropospheric)** | Secondary pollutant from **NOx + VOCs** in sunlight (photochemical smog); heatwaves raise it (north-west India, Indo-Gangetic Plain) – bad
### part 1: Ozone Depletion, the Ozone Hole and Treaties
[0] h: Cause of depletion
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] Where | In the **stratosphere**
  [1.1] **Main cause** | **Halocarbons** – **CFCs** (most damaging), HCFCs, halons – that release **chlorine and bromine**
  [1.2] **Carbon dioxide** | **Has no direct role** in ozone depletion – nor do CO, nitrogen, acetylene, ethylene, SO₂ or O₂
  [1.3] **HFCs** | Spare ozone but have high global-warming potential
  [1.4] CFCs | Destroy ozone **and** are strong GHGs
[2] table: ODS | Use
  [2.0] **CFCs** | Refrigeration, ACs, aerosols, foam
  [2.1] HCFCs | CFC substitute – **also deplete ozone**
  [2.2] **Halons** | Fire extinguishers
  [2.3] Methyl bromide | Fumigant
  [2.4] **Carbon tetrachloride**, methyl chloroform | Solvents
[3] h: The Antarctic ozone hole
[4] table: Fact | Key detail
  [4.0] Where | Maximum over **Antarctica** – the **poles**; forms in the southern spring **Aug–Nov**
  [4.1] Reason | **A prominent polar front (polar vortex) and polar stratospheric clouds (PSCs), plus the inflow of CFCs**
  [4.2] PSCs | 12–22 km, nitric acid ice; turn inactive chlorine into reactive forms that destroy ozone when sunlight returns
  [4.3] Discovery | Reported in **1985** by a **British team** (British Antarctic Survey: **Farman, Gardiner, Shanklin**)
  [4.4] 2025 hole | Peaked at just over **21 million sq km** and closed early (record ~29 million sq km, 2006)
[5] table: Treaty | Year | Point
  [5.0] Vienna Convention | 1985 | Framework: research, monitoring
  [5.1] **Montreal Protocol** | **1987** | Legally binding; phases out ~100 ODS; universally ratified (with Vienna, the first UN treaties ratified by all)
  [5.2] Kigali Amendment | 2016 (Kigali, **Rwanda**) | **HFC** phase-down 80–85%; in force 1 Jan 2019
[6] table: Fact | Key detail
  [6.0] **World Ozone Day** | **16 September** (Montreal Protocol signing)
  [6.1] India | **First developing country** to phase out **HCFC-141b** in rigid foam; HCFCs cut **44%** vs a 35% target by Jan 2020
  [6.2] AP – **Mission SANA** (Red Balloon Aerospace) | India's first home-made **stratospheric super-pressure balloon**, launched from **Vijayawada** (May 2026)
### part 2: Composition – Gases of the Atmosphere
[0] h: Gases of dry air
[1] table: Gas | Share by volume | Type
  [1.0] **Nitrogen** | **78.08% (~78%)** – most abundant | Permanent
  [1.1] **Oxygen** | – | Permanent
  [1.2] **Argon** | 0.93% – third, inert | Permanent
  [1.3] Carbon dioxide | ~0.04% (0.036% in NCERT); **423.9 ppm in 2024** (WMO Greenhouse Gas Bulletin, Oct 2025); **0.03% CO₂** = the optimum for natural balance | Variable
  [1.4] Water vapour | 0–4% | Variable
  [1.5] Neon, helium, krypton, xenon, hydrogen, ozone | Traces | –
[2] table: Fact | Key detail
  [2.0] Order | **N₂ > O₂ > Ar > CO₂**; **nitrogen + oxygen = 99%** – air near the surface is mainly nitrogen and oxygen
  [2.1] **Permanent gases** | Constant proportion: **nitrogen**, oxygen, argon (and the noble gases); **variable:** CO₂, water vapour, ozone
  [2.2] **Chlorine** | Not a normal constituent of air (neon, helium, CO₂ are)
  [2.3] **Xenon = "Stranger Gas"** | Greek *xenos*; found by **Ramsay and Travers, 1898**
  [2.4] Weather balloons | Filled with **helium** (light, inert)
  [2.5] Air held by | **Gravity**
[3] h: Change with height and with time
[4] table: Fact | Key detail
  [4.0] Mass | 99% within 32 km
  [4.1] Disappearance with height | **CO₂ and water vapour vanish first (~90 km)**, then **oxygen (negligible by ~120 km)**, nitrogen continues → **CO₂ → O₂ → N₂**
  [4.2] **Exosphere** | Light gases rise highest – mostly **hydrogen** (with helium)
  [4.3] **Early atmosphere** | **Reducing** – methane, ammonia, hydrocarbons, little free oxygen; these were oxidised and replaced by **carbon dioxide**; photosynthesis later made it oxygen-rich
  [4.4] **Hadean Eon (4,540–4,000 mya)** | First atmosphere = solar-nebula gases (mainly hydrogen); **volcanic outgassing** (plus gases of the Late Heavy Bombardment) produced the primordial atmosphere
  [4.5] **Oxygen in the atmosphere** | No free oxygen until about **2.4 billion years ago** (Proterozoic Eon, 2,500–541 mya, cyanobacteria); the **Phanerozoic Eon (541 mya – present)** has the present atmosphere
### part 3: Layers – Basis, Sequence and Temperature Trend
[0] h: Sequence of layers
[1] b: Layers are divided on the **vertical profile of air temperature** – not wind, humidity or pressure.
[2] table: Order | Layer | Height | Temperature | Identity
  [2.0] 1 | **Troposphere** – lowest | 0 to 8 km (poles) / 18 km (equator); avg **13 km** | Falls | Weather
  [2.1] 2 | **Stratosphere** – second layer | To **50 km** | Rises | Ozone
  [2.2] 3 | Mesosphere | 50–80 km | Falls to **−100°C – coldest** | Meteors burn
  [2.3] 4 | Thermosphere (ionosphere **80–400/640 km**) | From 80 km | Rises sharply | Ions, radio waves, aurora
  [2.4] 5 | Exosphere | Outermost | Rarefied | Merges into space
[3] table: Fact | Key detail
  [3.0] Upward order | **Troposphere → Stratosphere → Mesosphere → Thermosphere/Ionosphere**
  [3.1] Smallest height | **The troposphere**
  [3.2] The **atmosphere** | The thin layer of gases around the Earth; **no definite upper limit**; **thins gradually** until imperceptible
  [3.3] Temperature trend upward | **Falls, rises, falls, rises**
  [3.4] Reversal layers | **Stratosphere and thermosphere**
[4] table: Boundary | Height
  [4.0] Tropopause | 8–18 km
  [4.1] Stratopause | ~50 km
  [4.2] Mesopause | ~80 km
[5] h: Layer and feature
[6] table: Layer | Feature
  [6.0] Troposphere | Weather
  [6.1] Stratosphere | Ozone; warms because **ozone absorbs UV**
  [6.2] Ionosphere | Radio waves reflected to Earth
  [6.3] Aurora | Ionosphere / thermosphere, not the mesosphere
  [6.4] **Thermosphere** | Warms with height – its thin gases absorb **high-energy solar radiation** (X-rays, extreme UV)
### part 4: Troposphere and Tropopause
[0] h: Troposphere
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] Height | **Average 13 km**; ~8 km at the poles, ~**18 km at the equator**; the lowest layer, temperature falls with height
  [1.1] **Thickest at the equator** | **High insolation and strong convection** carry heat to great heights
  [1.2] Weather | **Almost all weather** – clouds, rain, fog, hail, storms; the **cloud mass of a cyclone** rises only within the troposphere
  [1.3] Contents | Nearly all **water vapour and dust**; most important for **biological activity**
  [1.4] **Normal lapse rate** | **~6.5°C per km** (≈ **1°C per 165 m**) – temperature **decreases** with height
  [1.5] **Agra and Darjeeling** | About the same latitude; Darjeeling much colder in January – temperature falls with **height**
[2] h: Tropopause
[3] table: Fact | Key detail
  [3.0] Nature | **Transition zone between troposphere and stratosphere**, **below the stratosphere**; a **zone of mixing**
  [3.1] Height | **Varies with season** (higher in summer) and **latitude** (higher at the equator); ~18 km over the equator
  [3.2] Temperature | About **−80°C over the equator**, **−45°C over the poles**
### part 5: Stratosphere, Mesosphere, Ionosphere and Exosphere
[0] h: Phenomenon and layer
[1] table: Phenomenon | Layer
  [1.0] Jet aircraft fly | Lower **stratosphere** – **no clouds or weather**, dry, stable
  [1.1] **Meteors burn up** | **Mesosphere**
  [1.2] **Radio waves deflected / reflected**; wireless communication; telecommunication | **Ionosphere**
  [1.3] **Aurora Borealis** (north) / Australis (south) | **Ionosphere** (the ionised part of the thermosphere)
  [1.4] **Communication satellites** (geostationary ~36,000 km) | **Exosphere**
[2] table: Fact | Key detail
  [2.0] Ionosphere | **80–400 km** (80–640 km in older accounts); **electrically charged ions** reflect radio waves back to Earth; expands by day, contracts at night
  [2.1] Aurora | Solar charged particles excite upper-air atoms near the magnetic poles
  [2.2] **MST (Mesosphere–Stratosphere–Troposphere) radar** | **National Atmospheric Research Laboratory (NARL), Gadanki, near Tirupati**
### part 6: Carbon Dioxide, Water Vapour, Dust and the Greenhouse Property
[0] h: CO₂ as a greenhouse gas
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] CO₂ | **Transparent to incoming solar (short-wave) radiation**, **opaque to outgoing terrestrial (long-wave, infrared) radiation** – it **absorbs infrared** and re-radiates heat
  [1.1] **Greenhouse effect** | Heating of the atmosphere by absorption of infrared by **CO₂** and other gases; CO₂ contributes most and its rise enhances the effect
  [1.2] Other GHGs | **Methane, nitrous oxide, water vapour (most abundant GHG), CFCs**; not hydrogen, krypton, neon, helium or argon
[2] h: Water vapour and dust
[3] table: Fact | Key detail
  [3.0] **Water vapour** | Up to **4%** in warm wet tropics, **under 1%** in deserts and polar air; decreases with height and from equator to poles
  [3.1] Role of water vapour | Absorbs insolation and Earth's heat (**blanket**); decides air stability
  [3.2] **Dust** | Sea salt, fine soil, smoke-soot, ash, pollen, meteor dust; mostly in lower layers
  [3.3] Where most dust | **Subtropical and temperate regions – because of dry winds** – more than equatorial and polar regions
  [3.4] Dust and salt | **Hygroscopic nuclei** for condensation → clouds
### part 7: Homosphere, Heterosphere and Ionospheric Layers
[0] h: Homosphere and heterosphere
[1] table: Region | Height | Layers | Composition
  [1.0] **Homosphere** | 0 to ~90 km | **Three: troposphere, stratosphere, mesosphere** | Gases evenly mixed
  [1.1] Heterosphere | Above ~90 km | Thermosphere (ionosphere), exosphere | Layered by molecular weight: N₂ → atomic O → He → H
[2] b: The **ionosphere** lies in the heterosphere, outside the homosphere.
[3] h: Ionospheric layers
[4] table: Ionospheric layer | Height | Role
  [4.0] D | 60–90 km | Absorbs medium/long waves; fades at night
  [4.1] E (Kennelly-Heaviside) | 90–150 km | Reflects medium-frequency waves
  [4.2] F (Appleton) | 150–400 km | Main reflector of short-wave radio
[5] b: Solar flares and geomagnetic storms disturb the ionosphere and disrupt **GNSS** signals.

## topic group2 4-26 | Ocean Movements – Waves, Tides and Ocean Currents of the World
### part 0: Ocean Currents of the World – Warm and Cold
[0] h: Warm and cold currents
[1] b: **Warm** currents flow from the equator towards the poles, usually on the **east coasts** of continents in low and middle latitudes; **cold** currents flow towards the equator, usually on the **west coasts**.
[2] table: Current | Type | Ocean / region
  [2.0] **Gulf Stream** | **Warm** | **North Atlantic**; starts in the Gulf of Mexico as the **Florida Current**; poleward, warms the US east coast and NW Europe (does not reach West Pacific coral reefs)
  [2.1] North Atlantic Drift, Norwegian, Florida | Warm | North Atlantic
  [2.2] **Antilles Current** | **Warm** | East of the West Indies
  [2.3] North Equatorial, North Pacific Drift | Warm | Tropics / North Pacific
  [2.4] **Kuroshio** | **Warm** | **North Pacific** (Japan) – the Pacific twin of the Gulf Stream
  [2.5] **Brazil** (**Brazilian Current**) | **Warm** | South Atlantic
  [2.6] **Agulhas, Mozambique** | **Warm** | Indian Ocean (SE Africa)
  [2.7] **East Australian** | **Warm** | South Pacific
  [2.8] Caribbean | Warm | Caribbean Sea
  [2.9] **Labrador** | **Cold** | North Atlantic (NE Canada)
  [2.10] **Canary** | **Cold** | **North Atlantic** (NW Africa) – not in the Pacific
  [2.11] **Benguela** | **Cold** | **South Atlantic** (Namibia)
  [2.12] **Falkland** | **Cold** | South Atlantic (the **Falkland Islands** are in the **South Atlantic**)
  [2.13] **Humboldt (Peru)** / **Peruvian** | **Cold** | **SE Pacific** (Peru–Chile); the only Pacific current among Benguela, Brazil, South Equatorial and Peruvian
  [2.14] **California** | **Cold** | NE Pacific
  [2.15] **Oyashio (Kurile), Kamchatka** | **Cold** | **North Pacific**
  [2.16] **West Australian** | **Cold** | Indian Ocean
  [2.17] West Wind Drift / **South Atlantic Drift** | Cold | Southern Ocean / South Atlantic
[3] b: Pairs: warm – **North Atlantic Drift and Brazil**; cold – Humboldt and Benguela, Canaries and Benguela.
[4] h: Cape de Sao Roque (Brazil)
[5] b: **Cape de Sao Roque:** the 'shoulder' of **Brazil** where the **South Equatorial Current bifurcates** – one part (the **Cayenne Current**, north-west) enters the Caribbean and Gulf of Mexico with the North Equatorial Current; the rest flows east of the West Indies as the Antilles Current; the other branch flows south-west as the Brazilian Current.
### part 1: Waves and Tsunamis
[0] h: Waves
[1] table: Term | Meaning
  [1.0] **Wavelength** | Distance between two successive crests – two consecutive points of the **same phase**
  [1.1] Wave height / amplitude | Trough to crest / half the height
  [1.2] Period / frequency | Time between crests / waves per second
  [1.3] Swells | Slow, steady, long-period waves from distant storms
  [1.4] Breakers | Waves break when depth < **half the wavelength**
  [1.5] Rip current | Narrow, fast channel flowing away from the shore
  [1.6] Kallakkadal | Kerala swell surge from far southern storms
[2] b: Waves carry **energy, not water**; wind gives them energy.
[3] h: Tsunami
[4] table: Fact | Key detail
  [4.0] Name | Japanese "**harbour wave**"
  [4.1] Cause | Sudden **displacement of the sea floor**: **tectonic activity**, chiefly **fault movement in undersea earthquakes** (most important), also submarine landslides and volcanoes – not wind, the Moon or sunspots
  [4.2] Deep water | Speed about √(g × depth): **faster in deep water** (over 700 km/h); a **gentle rise and fall** – a **ship in the deep sea is not affected**
  [4.3] Near the shore | **Slows down** and grows to **enormous height** – greatest impact at the coast
  [4.4] **Warning sign** | The sea **recedes far, exposing the sea floor**
  [4.5] Mitigation | Build houses on **elevated sites**
  [4.6] **26 Dec 2004** (Sumatra quake, M 9.1) | In India the **Coromandel coast** (Tamil Nadu) was hit hardest; Indira Point was submerged
  [4.7] **ITEWS** (INCOIS, **Hyderabad**, since 2007) | Confirms a tsunami with **bottom pressure recorders** in the deep ocean (seismic stations detect only the quake; tide gauges are coastal)
  [4.8] Working tools | Tide gauges, BPRs, seismic networks; satellite observation is not an effective real-time tsunami warning system
[5] table: Institution | Role
  [5.0] **INCOIS, Hyderabad** | Runs ITEWS; **PFZ** advisories; **SAMUDRA** app
  [5.1] IMD | Cyclone and multi-hazard warnings
  [5.2] **NIOT, Chennai** | **OMNI buoys** (temperature, salinity, currents); station at **Nellore** (AP)
  [5.3] RTGS Vijayawada (AP) | Cyclone, high-wave and fish-shoal alerts to fishermen
### part 2: Tides
[0] h: Causes of tides
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] **Tides** | Periodic rise and fall of sea level, once or twice a day, caused by the gravitational pull of the **Sun and Moon on the Earth**
  [1.1] **Moon's pull is greater** | The Moon is nearer
  [1.2] **Centrifugal force** | Balances gravity
  [1.3] **Direct tide** | Bulge facing the Moon (gravity)
  [1.4] **Indirect tide** | Opposite side (centrifugal force)
  [1.5] Earth's rotation (west to east) | Real, but it does not explain tides
  [1.6] **Surges** | Irregular, weather-driven (winds, pressure) – not tides
  [1.7] Coastal **inundation** | Sea water spreading over nearby low land ("oceanic logging")
  [1.8] **Tidal currents** | Tidal water channelled between islands or into bays and estuaries
[2] h: Spring and neap tides
[3] table: Basis | Spring tide | Neap tide
  [3.0] Sun–Earth–Moon | In a straight line | At right angles
  [3.1] Moon phase | **New and full moon** | **First and third quarter**
  [3.2] Height | About **20% higher** than normal | About 20% lower
  [3.3] Tidal range | Maximum | **Minimum: high tide lower, low tide higher**
[4] table: Fact | Key detail
  [4.0] **Tidal range** | Difference between high and low tide
  [4.1] **Perigean** tides | Unusually high when the Moon is closest
  [4.2] Timing | High tides recur about every **12 h 25 min** – about **50 minutes** later each day
[5] table: Type | Feature
  [5.0] Semi-diurnal | Two highs + two lows a day (most common)
  [5.1] Diurnal | One high + one low
  [5.2] Mixed | Unequal heights
[6] h: Tidal places and energy
[7] table: Place | Feature
  [7.0] Bay of Fundy (Canada) | World's greatest tidal range
  [7.1] **Gulf of Khambhat (Bhavnagar)**, Gujarat | 5–10 m; about 7,000 MW; India's **most potential tidal-energy area**
  [7.2] Gulf of Kutch | 5–8 m
  [7.3] Sundarbans | About 5 m
  [7.4] **Chandipur (Odisha)** | Sea **recedes a few km at ebb tide** twice a day; you can walk on the sea floor
[8] table: Fact | Key detail
  [8.0] Highest tides | Gulfs with **wide fronts and narrow rears**
  [8.1] Tidal **bore** | Forms in narrow, shallow estuaries
  [8.2] Hooghly | Its tides matter to **Kolkata port**
  [8.3] Tidal energy | **Barrage** (tidal range) or **underwater turbines** (tidal stream)

### part 4: Causes of Ocean Currents
[0] h: Causes of ocean currents
[1] table: Factor | Effect
  [1.0] **Planetary winds** | **Main driver**; trades drive equatorial currents west, westerlies drive drifts east
  [1.1] **Coriolis force** | Deflects **right in the NH, left in the SH** → gyres
  [1.2] Solar heating | Water expands near the equator → slight slope
  [1.3] **Temperature, salinity (density)** | **Saltier and colder water is denser**; cold polar water sinks and creeps slowly to the equator (**thermohaline**)
  [1.4] Coastline shape, floor relief | Deflect / steer currents (a landmass does not always obstruct them)
[2] b: The **revolution of the Earth** does not cause ocean currents.
[3] table: Fact | Key detail
  [3.0] Ocean currents | Large masses of surface water circulating in regular patterns – a slow **surface movement**, set in motion **mainly by prevailing winds**; affected by the **configuration of the ocean**; keep the **heat balance**
  [3.1] **Equatorial Counter Current** | Flows **west to east** between the North and South Equatorial Currents (through the doldrums) – their **convergence piles up water in the west**
  [3.2] Surface vs deep currents | Surface = upper 400 m, about 10% of ocean water; deep = 90%
[4] table: Movement | Nature
  [4.0] Waves | Oscillations moving energy
  [4.1] Tides | Periodic vertical rise and fall
  [4.2] **Currents** | **Continuous horizontal flow**; major effects on **climate, fisheries and navigation**; **redistribute heat**

### part 7: Waves – Formation, Types and Wave Energy
[0] h: Formation and motion
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] **How waves form** | **Wind** blowing over the sea passes its **energy** to the water; waves are the **horizontal movement of energy**, not of water
  [1.1] **Motion of water** | Particles move in **circular (orbital) paths** and come back almost to the same place; motion fades with depth, negligible below about **half the wavelength**
  [1.2] **Sea** | Steep, irregular waves still under the generating wind
  [1.3] **Swell** | Smooth, regular, long-period waves that have left the storm; travel thousands of km
[2] h: At the shore
[3] table: Fact | Key detail
  [3.0] **Breaker** | As water shallows the wave **slows, shortens and steepens**; the crest topples
  [3.1] **Swash** | Foamy water rushing up the beach
  [3.2] **Backwash** | Water running back
  [3.3] **Wave refraction** | Waves bend round headlands – energy concentrates on **headlands (erosion)**, spreads in **bays (deposition)**

[5] table: Fact | Key detail
  [5.0] India's first wave-energy plant | **150 kW oscillating water column (OWC)** pilot at **Vizhinjam (Thiruvananthapuram, Kerala), 1991**

## topic group2 4-33 | Indian Climate – Monsoon Mechanism, Theories and Seasons
### part 0: Winter Season, Retreating and North-East Monsoon, Western Disturbances
[0] h: Retreating monsoon (October–November/December)
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] Cause | The **southward movement of the Sun's vertical rays**; the NW low disappears, the **subtropical westerly jet returns south of the Himalayas**, the TEJ disappears
  [1.1] Weather | **Fine weather and clear skies**; land temperature rises again → hot, humid "**October heat**"
  [1.2] Signs | Clear sky, pressure change over the Bay of Bengal, rising temperature on land
  [1.3] **Tropical cyclones** | The hallmark: they form mostly in the **Bay of Bengal** (rarely the Arabian Sea); frequency peaks in **October–November**; heavy rain on the east coast → **bimodal** rainfall in the south
  [1.4] Winds | Blow from land **towards the Bay of Bengal**, pick up moisture there and rain on the Coromandel coast
  [1.5] Share | About **13%** of annual rainfall
[2] h: North-east monsoon and Tamil Nadu
[3] b: The winter high over NW India sends winds NW → SE down the plains; over the Bay of Bengal they become the **north-east monsoon** and pick up moisture.
[4] b: The **Tamil Nadu / Coromandel coast** gets its **main rain in October–December (Nov–Dec)** from the NE monsoon; South Arcot and Ramanathapuram get **over 50%** of their rain then. It also reaches **Rayalaseema, Nellore, south coastal AP, SE Karnataka, SE Kerala** – the Gujarat coast gets none.
[5] b: **Why Tamil Nadu is dry in the SW monsoon:** it lies in the **rain shadow** of the Western Ghats for the Arabian Sea branch, and the Bay branch blows **parallel to the coast** – so **Chennai** gets the least SW-monsoon rain among Kolkata, Mangalore, Chennai and Delhi.
[6] b: States fed mainly by other rain than the June–September monsoon: **Tamil Nadu** (NE monsoon) and **Jammu & Kashmir** (western disturbances).
[7] h: Western disturbances
[8] table: Fact | Key detail
  [8.0] Nature | **Temperate (extra-tropical) cyclones from the Mediterranean**, carried east by the westerlies through Iran–Afghanistan–Pakistan into **north-west India**; not part of the monsoon system
  [8.1] Rain | **10–15 cm** of winter rain to the **north-western plains** – **beneficial for rabi crops (wheat)**; **snowfall** in J&K, Himachal Pradesh, Uttarakhand
  [8.2] Gradient | Winter rain in the NW plains **decreases from west to east** (and from north to south)
  [8.3] **Northern plains' winter rain** | Caused by **western disturbances** – the NE monsoon is active in winter but is not the cause, nor is the retreating monsoon
[9] h: Winter season (December/January–February)
[10] b: **High pressure over NW India** (the **1019 mb** isobar passes through north-west India, **1013 mb** in the far south) → **anticyclonic**, dry, outblowing winds; cold northern plains, cold waves, fog; **no loo or thunderstorms**.
[11] b: **Anticyclonic conditions** form because the land is **cold and the air dense**.
[12] b: Winter is the **driest season (~2%)**: most of India is dry under the **north-east trade winds**, which blow from land to sea. **Dry winters** (Koeppen Cwg, "monsoon with dry winter"): **Bengal / Ganga plain**; J&K and Punjab–Haryana get western-disturbance rain, Tamil Nadu NE-monsoon rain.
[13] b: **South India:** maritime – **low diurnal and annual range**, high temperature all year, **no extremes**; the 20 °C January isotherm runs from the Tapi mouth to the Mahanadi delta.
### part 1: Monsoon – Meaning, Characteristics and Controls of Indian Climate
[0] h: Meaning and characteristics
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] Climate | **Tropical monsoon**
  [1.1] Word | From Arabic **Mausim** = season; **Al Masudi** (Arab geographer) described the seasonal rhythm of winds
  [1.2] Nature | **Seasonal (secondary/periodic) winds** that **reverse direction** with the season – not primary (planetary) winds
  [1.3] Belt | Confined to the tropics, roughly **20°N–20°S**
  [1.4] Main cause | The **temperature (pressure) contrast between the land and the Indian Ocean**; driven by the size of the Asian landmass; upper-air circulation is associated with it
  [1.5] SW-monsoon distribution | Governed mainly by **relief**
  [1.6] Rain | **Not steady**, **unevenly distributed**, has **breaks**, **uncertain/variable**
  [1.7] History | **Early maritime links** of India with South-East Asia across the Bay of Bengal were helped by the **monsoon winds**
[2] b: India is a "**thirsty land**" despite abundant rain: the rain is **concentrated in a few months**, **runs off rapidly** and **evaporates quickly**.
[3] h: Controls (factors) of Indian climate
[4] table: Factor | Key detail
  [4.0] **Latitude** | The **Tropic of Cancer** crosses the middle of India; the south is tropical, the north sub-tropical; India lies about **8°4'–37°6'N**, so it is not wholly between the tropics
  [4.1] **Himalayas** | Block cold Central Asian winds, trap the monsoon and give India its **climatic distinctiveness**
  [4.2] **Altitude** | **Amritsar and Shimla** lie on nearly the same latitude; Shimla (~2,200 m vs ~230 m) is cooler; likewise **Ooty vs Coimbatore**, **Agra (16 °C) vs Darjiling (4 °C)** in January
  [4.3] **Distance from the sea (continentality)** | **Mumbai** is moderate while **Nagpur** is extreme at a similar latitude; in **Kerala and the Andaman Islands** the day–night range is only about **7–8 °C**
  [4.4] Others | **Relief**, **pressure and winds**, **upper-air circulation (jet streams)**, thermal contrast, **ocean currents**, **cyclones/western disturbances**
### part 2: Onset, Advance, Branches and Breaks of the South-West Monsoon
[0] h: Onset and advance
[1] b: Season: **June–September** (IMD), also stated as **mid-June to mid-September**; gives **~75%** of annual rain. The sudden onset = "**burst**" in the first week of June; rain in Kerala is called **Varsha**.
[2] table: Stage | Normal date
  [2.0] Andaman & Nicobar Islands (first Indian territory) | ~25 May
  [2.1] **Kerala** (first state, Malabar coast) | **1 June** (2026: 4 June)
  [2.2] Bhopal / Delhi | ~15 June / ~25 June
  [2.3] Whole country | **8 July** (IMD revised normal)
[3] table: Fact | Key detail
  [3.0] Kerala onset rule | IMD declares it after **10 May** when **60% of 14 stations** get **2.5 mm** on two consecutive days (5-day **pentads** used)
  [3.1] Order of onset | **Kerala → West Bengal → Uttar Pradesh → Rajasthan**
  [3.2] Duration | **> 120 days** in the SW peninsula, **< 75 days** in west Rajasthan; the monsoon reaches **west Rajasthan last**
[4] h: Arabian Sea branch
[5] b: Strikes the **Western Ghats** and Malabar first; heaviest rain at **Agumbe, Hulikal** on the crest; the **windward west coast** gets very heavy **orographic** rain because the **Western Ghats** lie across the winds.
[6] b: **Mumbai** (windward) gets far more than **Pune** (leeward); the eastern side of the Ghats is a **rain-shadow** area.
[7] b: Enters central India via the **Narmada–Tapi** valleys; one arm runs **parallel to the Aravallis** → scanty rain in Rajasthan, which still gets a little SW-monsoon rain.
[8] b: The **Chhattisgarh Basin** is **little affected** by the Arabian Sea branch (it gets Bay branch rain). The **Palghat Gap** lets only part of the branch through to Tamil Nadu – most monsoon winds do not enter through it.
[9] h: Bay of Bengal branch
[10] b: Strikes Myanmar and the **funnel-shaped Garo–Khasi–Jaintia hills** → heavy orographic rain in **Meghalaya** (Mawsynram, Cherrapunji).
[11] b: Deflected **westward by the Himalayas** along the Ganga plain → rain **decreases east to west**; **Jharkhand** and the east get their rain from the **SW monsoon**.
[12] h: Breaks
[13] b: A **break** occurs when the **monsoon trough (ITCZ) shifts north to the Himalayan foothills**: the plains stay dry, the hills get heavy rain and temperature rises; one or two breaks a season, most often in the **second week of August**.
[14] b: The **west coast** gets more rain (narrow plain, high Ghats), but the **east coast** produces more **rice** (wide deltas, alluvial soil).
### part 3: Hot Weather Season, Local Storms and the Six Traditional Seasons
[0] h: Hot weather (pre-monsoon) season – March to May
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] Heat | Northward march of the Sun; over **42.5 °C** in the north and NW; **Ganganagar** recorded about **50 °C** (1935)

### part 4: Theories of the Monsoon Mechanism, ENSO and Monsoon Research
[0] table: Theory / driver | Core idea
  [0.0] **Thermal** (differential heating) – **Edmund Halley, 1686** | Land heats faster than sea → the summer low over land draws moist sea winds; reversed in winter
  [0.1] **Dynamic / ITCZ shift** (1950s) | The ITCZ moves north over the Ganga plain as the **monsoon trough**; SE trades cross the equator and turn south-westerly
  [0.2] **Jet streams** | The **STJ** shifts **north of the Himalayas** → onset; returns **south** → withdrawal. The **TEJ** (South China Sea → **East Africa**) coincides with the SW monsoon; the **Somali jet** + **Somalia Current** strengthen it
  [0.3] **Tibetan Plateau** (~5,000 m) | Summer **heat island** → upper-air **anticyclone**, stronger TEJ; heavy snow cover weakens it
  [0.4] **ENSO / IOD / MJO** | **El Nino weakens** the monsoon (delayed, weak monsoon, drought); La Nina strengthens it; a **positive IOD** can offset El Nino; the MJO repeats every 30–60 days
[1] table: Fact | Key detail
  [1.0] **Mascarene High** | Near **Madagascar**; drives the cross-equatorial flow
  [1.1] **Dust aerosols** | Over the Indo-Gangetic Plain, help pull the monsoon inland
  [1.2] **Gilbert Walker** (1920s) | Described the **Southern Oscillation**: low pressure over the Indian Ocean (Darwin) + high over the Pacific (Tahiti) = good monsoon
  [1.3] **MONEX** (Monsoon Experiment) | **1979** under the **WMO**; confirmed the role of the shifting ITCZ (an earlier Indo-Soviet experiment in 1973)
  [1.4] **El Nino** | A warm current off **Peru** every **3–7 years**, replacing the cold Peruvian current; it **causes drought-like conditions in parts of India**
[2] h: Research and forecasting
[3] b: **IMD** works under the **Ministry of Earth Sciences (MoES)**.
[4] table: Mission | Key detail
  [4.0] **National Monsoon Mission** | **2012, MoES**, through **IITM Pune + IMD**; dynamic coupled model (MMCFS)
  [4.1] **Mission Mausam** | **14 Jan 2025** with IMD Vision 2047; **Rs 2,000 crore**; **Bharat Forecast System** (6 km, 10-day rain)
[5] b: **LPA** of SW-monsoon rain (1971–2020) = **868.6 mm**. **Ocean Mean Temperature (OMT)** – the upper ocean down to the **26 °C isotherm** – predicts better than **SST**.
[6] b: 2026: April forecast 92% of LPA, revised to 90%; June deficit 43% → contingency plans; El Nino building. **Final (30 Sep 2026): the season ended about 13% below LPA (~87% of LPA) – the lowest since 2015**; East & Northeast driest since 1901.
