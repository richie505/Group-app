### part 1: Receipts, Expenditure and Deficits – Classification
[0] h: Receipts
[1] table: Basis | Revenue receipts | Capital receipts
  [1.0] Liability | **Create no liability** | **Create a liability**
  [1.1] Assets | Do not reduce assets | **Or reduce assets**
  [1.2] Examples | Tax: income, **corporation tax**, GST, customs, **excise**; non-tax: **interest receipts, dividends and profits**, fees, fines, grants | **Borrowings** (market loans, T-Bills, **World Bank loans**), **small savings/NSC**, recovery of loans, **disinvestment**
[2] table: Fact | Key detail
  [2.0] **Order of classification** | **Revenue receipts → Tax revenue → Non-tax revenue → Capital receipts**
  [2.1] **Interest received** | **Non-tax revenue** – creates no liability
  [2.2] **Disinvestment** | Capital receipt (reduces assets)
  [2.3] **Small savings** | Outside the revenue account; debt receipts in the capital account
  [2.4] **Treasury Bills** | Short-term (91, 182, 364-day) borrowing of the Centre – a debt capital receipt
  [2.5] **Current (revenue) account income** | **Corporation tax, profits of public enterprises, excise duties**; NSC sales and World Bank loans are capital
  [2.6] **Non-tax revenue of the Centre** | Mainly interest, dividends and profits, fees
  [2.7] Biggest head among mines, forests, opium and roads | **Roads and Bridges** (national-highway fees: ₹3,681 crore in 2012-13 BE; forestry ₹23 crore, non-ferrous mining ₹27.5 crore); mines and forests yield mainly to the **States**
  [2.8] **Paper taxes** | Wealth tax, gift tax, estate duty – never large, now abolished
[3] h: Expenditure
[4] table: Pair | Distinction
  [4.0] Revenue vs capital | Revenue creates no asset (salaries, pensions, **interest**, subsidies, grants); capital creates assets or cuts liabilities (capital works, loans given, debt repayment)
  [4.1] Plan vs non-plan (to 2016-17) | Non-plan = **interest**, salaries, pensions, **defence**, subsidies, **public administration**
  [4.2] Developmental vs non-developmental | Development = **economic and social services** (railways, power, S&T, education, health, grants to States); **defence** = non-development
[5] table: Fact | Key detail
  [5.0] **Interest payments** | **Largest non-plan item (2015-16)** and the **most important revenue-account expenditure**; still the largest single item of spending
  [5.1] **Plan/non-plan ended** | **2017-18** (revenue/capital only)
  [5.2] **Capital receipts < revenue receipts** | In 2010-11 and now (2026-27 BE: revenue ₹35.33 lakh crore, capital ₹18.14 lakh crore)
[6] h: Deficits
[7] table: Deficit | Formula
  [7.0] **Budget (budgetary) deficit** | **Total expenditure − total receipts** (revenue + capital); discontinued from **1996-97**
  [7.1] **Fiscal deficit** | Total expenditure − receipts excluding borrowings
  [7.2] **Primary deficit** | FD − interest (e.g. FD ₹80,000 crore − interest ₹25,000 crore = **PD ₹55,000 crore**)
  [7.3] **Revenue deficit** | Revenue expenditure − revenue receipts
[8] b: **Golden rule:** borrow only to invest, not to finance revenue spending.
[9] x: The budget deficit nets **all** receipts – it is total expenditure minus revenue **and** capital receipts.

### part 3: Trade Terms and Trade Theory
[0] table: Term | Meaning
  [0.0] **Trade** | Exchange between buyers and sellers – represents **both demand and supply**
  [0.1] **Balance of trade** | Exports minus imports of **goods** only (visible trade)
  [0.2] **Intra-industry trade** | A country **imports and exports the same product** (e.g. cars)
  [0.3] Inter-industry trade | Exchange of different products
  [0.4] **Entrepot trade** | **Imported goods re-exported**, with or without processing or packaging ("goods imported for export")
  [0.5] **Free trade** | **Absence of tariffs** and quantitative restrictions
  [0.6] **Dumping** | Exporting at a price below the home-market price or cost
  [0.7] **Anti-dumping duty** | Protects against goods exported below normal value
  [0.8] **Countervailing duty** | Levied on **imported** products to offset foreign subsidies; raises their domestic price
  [0.9] Tariff | Tax on imports
  [0.10] Import quota | Quantitative restriction on imports
  [0.11] Import licensing | Administrative restriction on imports
  [0.12] Rules of origin | Decide a product's national source and which goods get FTA concessions
  [0.13] SPS measures | Sanitary and phytosanitary rules (human, animal, plant health) – a **non-tariff barrier**

## topic group2 3-83 | Exchange Rate Systems and Rupee Movement; Currency Internationalisation
### part 0: Effects of Devaluation
[0] table: Fact | Key detail
  [0.0] Effect on prices | Devaluation makes **exports cheaper abroad** (their foreign-currency price falls) and **imports dearer at home**
  [0.1] Result | It **promotes exports** – because the price of the country's products in the international market falls
  [0.2] Policy type | An **expenditure-switching** policy (spending switches to home-produced goods)
  [0.3] Purpose | To **correct the balance of trade / BoP**; it **raises the cost of imports** and does **not curb inflation**
  [0.4] What it does **necessarily** | Only improves export competitiveness – it does **not necessarily** improve the trade balance or raise the currency's foreign value
  [0.5] Limits | **Imported inflation** (it **raises domestic prices**), inelastic oil imports, costlier ECB repayment
  [0.6] **When does it work?** | When exports actually **become cheaper to importers** abroad – export prices fall in proportion to the devaluation and domestic prices do not rise to cancel it
  [0.7] **1991** | The rupee was devalued to **make exports cheaper**; export growth rose after 1991 and the devaluation is credited for it
[1] h: Correcting a trade imbalance
[2] b: **Import duties, exchange controls and devaluation** are used to **wipe out** the trade imbalance and keep the rate stable.
[3] b: BoP correction methods: monetary policy, **exchange depreciation, devaluation, exchange control**, import duties and quotas, export promotion, foreign loans and investment.

### part 5: Exchange Rate Regimes and Determinants
[0] table: Regime | Meaning
  [0.0] **Fixed / pegged** | Government fixes the rate (to gold, a currency or a basket); the central bank buys / sells forex to hold it, so private demand and supply **need not come into balance**
  [0.1] **Exchange control** | Rate fixed and held by **rationing out the available foreign exchange**
  [0.2] **Flexible / floating** | Set by **demand and supply** of foreign currency in the market, without the central bank fixing it
  [0.3] **Managed float (dirty float)** | Market-set, with central-bank intervention to curb volatility – **India's market-determined system**
[1] b: **Post-Bretton Woods classes:** **fixed** – currency unions, dollarised regimes, **currency boards (Argentina 1991 – Jan 2002)**, pegs; **intermediate** – bands, crawling pegs; **flexible** – managed and independent floats.
[2] b: **Floating rates:** traders face the risk of an **exchange-rate loss**; price differences between markets give scope for **arbitrage**.
[3] h: Determinants
[4] table: Factor | Key detail
  [4.0] **Determinants** | Demand for the country's **goods and services**, **stability of its government**, economic potential (the **World Bank** does not set a currency's price)
  [4.1] **Raghuram Rajan** | **High inflation, high fiscal deficit and high crude oil prices** all weaken the rupee's exchange value
  [4.2] **PPP theory** | Equality of purchasing power (the **inflation differential**) decides the long-run **rate of exchange**
  [4.3] Long-run anchors (ES 2025-26) | PPP, savings-investment balance, current account
  [4.4] **Capital flows** | Large inflows → **appreciation** (Dutch disease); higher imports can coexist with a stable rupee if backed by **net investment inflows**
  [4.5] Repo hike | Attracts **carry-trade** capital
  [4.6] Deliberate undervaluation (alleged of China) | **Currency manipulation**
[5] x: **Impossible trinity (Mundell-Fleming trilemma):** no country can have a **fixed exchange rate, free capital flows and an independent monetary policy** at once.

[0] h: Definition
[1] b: **World Food Summit 1996 / FAO:** food security exists when **all people, at all times**, have **physical and economic access** to **sufficient, safe and nutritious food** for an active and healthy life – access to "**the food of their choice**" is no part of it.

  [3.2] Limited self-sufficiency | Green Revolution **restricted to small pockets**; **food costly relative to the earnings of the poor**
  [3.3] **Ecological cost** | Soil fertility loss, falling water table, salinity, toxic residues – a high social and environmental cost, though it modernised agriculture
  [3.4] Groundwater | Punjab and Haryana extract **35-57% more than recharge**
  [3.5] Nutrient use | Skewed at **N:P:K 10.9:4.1:1** (not balanced); rice–wheat monoculture
  [3.6] **Johl Committee** | Shift **20% of paddy area** (diversification)
