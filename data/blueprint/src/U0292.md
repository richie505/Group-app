# NOTES OF APP TOPIC 2:18 - Amendment of the Constitution - Article 368, types of majorities, procedure, limitations
### Article 368 basics
- Neither rigid nor flexible but a synthesis of both
- Part XX, sole Article 368
- Amend "by way of addition, variation or repeal"
- Procedure borrowed from South Africa (not USA)
- Granville Austin: "appears complicated" but "is merely diverse"
- Latest enacted = 106th (2023)
### Procedure
- 1. Bill in either House (not State Legislature); minister or private member; no prior Presidential sanction ↓
- 2. Each House separately: special majority; no joint sitting ↓
- 3. Federal provisions: ratified by half the States, simple majority, no time limit ↓
- 4. President must assent - no veto, no return (24th Amendment, 1971)
### Three routes of amendment
| Route | Requirement | Examples |
| Simple majority (outside 368) | Present & voting; not deemed amendment | Arts 2-4 new States; 169 Councils; 11 citizenship; official language; Fifth & Sixth Schedules; quorum |
| Special majority | Majority of total membership + two-thirds present & voting | FRs incl. Art. 32, DPSP, rest |
| Special + State ratification | + one-half of States | President's election, Seventh Schedule, SC/HCs, Art. 368 itself, GST Council (279A) |
### Bill types compared
| Basis | Ordinary | Money | Amendment |
| Origin | Either House | Lok Sabha only | Either House |
| Prior recommendation | Not required | Required | Not required |
| Joint sitting | Yes | No | No |

### Article 368 — location, source, nature and persons
- Article 368 (Part XX): The amendment power and procedure — the Constitution balances flexibility and rigidity
- Location: Part XX, Article 368 (sole Article of Part XX) (PYQs)
- Wording: Parliament "may, in exercise of its constituent power, amend by way of addition, variation or repeal any provision" (UPSC)
- Amending power: Parliament alone - including power to amend Fundamental Rights, by special majority
- Source: borrowed from South Africa (amendment procedure + election of Rajya Sabha members)
- Nature: neither rigid nor flexible but a synthesis of both (neither purely rigid nor purely flexible)
- Partly rigid, partly flexible - unlike the very rigid US Constitution and the flexible British one
| Person | Statement |
| Granville Austin | Process "one of the most ably conceived aspects"; "appears complicated" but "is merely diverse" (UPSC) |
- ⚠ Source country of the procedure = South Africa (not the US, which is "very rigid")
### Amendment procedure → step-by-step rules
## 3. Procedure
- Introduction: In either the Lok Sabha or the Rajya Sabha — not in a State Legislature; no prior recommendation of the President
- Only by a Bill in Either House of Parliament only - never a State Legislature (PYQ)
- Mover: Minister or private member
- Prior Presidential sanction Not required - No prior permission of the President (PYQ 2024 trap)
- Passage: Each House independently by special majority — no joint sitting; the Bill fails completely if one House rejects it
- Special majority in each House separately = majority of total membership + two-thirds present and voting
- No joint sitting (Art. 108 does not apply) - failure in either House makes the bill lapse / Bill falls
- Federal provisions: Also ratified by legislatures of half the States by simple majority
- Assent: The President must give assent — no veto, no power to return — made mandatory by the 24th Amendment (1971)
- Obligatory - President must give assent; can neither withhold nor return the Bill; 24th Amendment, 1971 [GK]
- Exam angle: "prior permission", "joint sitting" and "President may return" are all false for amendment bills.
- Exam angle: statement sets on "private member needs prior permission" (false) and "joint sitting possible" (false).
### Amendment Bill vs Ordinary and Money Bills
## 4. Comparison — Ordinary, Money and Constitution Amendment Bills
| Basis | Ordinary Bill | Money Bill | Constitution Amendment Bill |
| Articles | 107 and 108 | 109 and 110 | 368 |
| House of origin | Either House | Lok Sabha only | Either House — not a State Legislature |
| President's prior recommendation | Not required (except Arts. 3, 117 types) | Required | Not required (APP) |
| Rajya Sabha's power | Equal — may amend or reject; no fixed time limit | Only recommendations within 14 days | Equal — passed separately by each House |
| Majority | Simple | Simple | Special — a majority of the total membership + two-thirds of members present and voting, in each House separately (UPSC) |
| Joint Sitting (Article 108) | Yes — in a deadlock | No | No |
| President's options | Assent, withhold, or return once | Assent or withhold — cannot return | Must give assent — no veto |
- Federal provisions in an Amendment Bill also need ratification by at least one-half of the State Legislatures - half the states, no time limit (APP); an ordinary bill gives States no role
### Types of majority → requirement → used for
## 1. Three kinds of amendment
- Simple majority — outside Article 368:
- Creation or alteration of States — Article 3
- Creation or abolition of State Legislative Councils — Article 169
- Special majority: Passed by each House separately — a majority of the total membership + two-thirds of members present and voting
- Special majority + State ratification: Federal provisions — ratified by at least half of the State Legislatures, by simple majority (not all States)
| Route | Requirement | Examples |
| Simple majority (outside Art. 368) | Majority of members present and voting; like ordinary law; not deemed an amendment under Art. 368 (not counted as amendments) | New States, alteration of areas (Arts. 2, 3); creating new states (Art. 4); Legislative Councils (Art. 169); citizenship; official language; quorum |
| Special majority (Art. 368) | Majority of total membership of each House + two-thirds of members present and voting | Fundamental Rights (incl. Art. 32), DPSP, everything / all provisions not in the other two routes |
| Special majority + State ratification | Above + ratification by not less than one-half of the States | Election of President, executive power of Union/States, Supreme Court/High Courts, distribution of legislative powers (Seventh Schedule), Art. 368 itself |
- Article 32: needs special (two-thirds) majority - no State ratification (APPSC-G2 2017 key; PYQ G2 2017: "two-thirds majority")
- But SC powers under Art. 32 are part of the basic structure → cannot be destroyed
### Special majority — different meanings across Articles
## Special majority — different meanings across Articles
| Type | Requirement | Used for |
| Art. 368 type | Majority of total membership + 2/3 present and voting, each House | Amendment (368); removal of SC/HC judges (124, 217), CAG (148), CEC (324), SEC (243K); approval of National Emergency (352) |
| Art. 61 type | Two-thirds of total membership of the House | Impeachment of the President - strongest majority |
| Art. 249/312 type | Two-thirds of members present and voting in Rajya Sabha only | Parliament legislating on a State List subject (249); new All-India Services (312) |
- ⚠ 61 = two-thirds of total membership (President); 249/312 = two-thirds present and voting, Rajya Sabha only; 368 = both Houses, total + two-thirds
### Simple-majority provisions → Articles (not amendments)
## 1. Union, territory and citizenship
- Article 1: India is a Union of States
- Article 2: Admission or establishment of new States
- Article 3: Formation of new States and alteration of State boundaries
- ⚠ Laws under Articles 2 and 3 are not constitutional amendments under Article 368
- Articles 5 to 11: Citizenship — Article 11: Parliament's power to regulate citizenship
- Nature: changed like ordinary law; not counted as Art. 368 amendments
- Simple-majority changes (Arts. 2, 3, 169 etc.) are "not deemed amendments"
- Articles 2, 3, 4: new States, boundaries, names - simple majority; Art. 4(2): such laws not amendments under Art. 368

### Can Parliament amend Fundamental Rights? → cases
## 1. Can Parliament amend Fundamental Rights?
| Case | Year | Held |
| Shankari Prasad v. Union of India | 1951 | Yes — upheld Parliament's power under Article 368 and the First Amendment; "law" in Article 13(2) does not include constitutional amendments (Article 13 vs Article 368); Fundamental Rights: amendable by Parliament - upheld |
| Sajjan Singh v. State of Rajasthan | 1965 | Yes — reaffirmed the power to amend any part of the Constitution; upheld the First Amendment (Article 368) |
| Golaknath v. State of Punjab | 1967 | No — reversed (overruled) the earlier cases; a constitutional amendment is law under Article 13(2); Parliament cannot abridge or take away any Fundamental Right — the first case to limit the amending power (denied the power to amend FRs) |
| 24th Amendment | 1971 | 24th (1971) answered Golaknath (1967) - restored the power; amended Arts. 13 and 368 → Parliament can abridge/take away any FR; inserted Art. 13(4): an Art. 368 amendment is not "law" under Art. 13 (2026) |
| Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala | 1973 | Yes, but — Parliament can amend any part, including Fundamental Rights, but cannot alter, damage or destroy the Basic Structure; upheld the 24th; FRs and the Preamble amendable - Limit: Parliament cannot alter the basic structure |
| Indira Gandhi | 1975 | First applied the doctrine |
| 42nd Amendment | 1976 | 42nd (1976) declared amending power unlimited + amendments non-justiciable / barred review (clauses 368(4) and (5)) |
| Minerva Mills v. Union of India | 1980 | Reaffirmed the Basic Structure — a limited amending power is itself a basic feature; struck down the 42nd's clauses |
| Rajendra N. Shah | 2021 | Part IXB (97th) struck down for State co-operatives for want of ratification |
- Chain: Shankari Prasad 1951 = yes; Golaknath 1967 = no; restored by the 24th; Kesavananda 1973 = yes, but not the basic structure
### Kesavananda Bharati and the FIRSTs of the limit
## 2. Kesavananda Bharati (24 April 1973)
- Largest bench: 13 judges; decided by a 7:6 majority
- Propounded the Basic Structure Doctrine — linked to Article 368 (Part XX) and the 24th Amendment
- Recognised the Preamble as part of the Constitution
- Parliament's constituent power is plenary but limited — it cannot abrogate the essential foundation or basic features
## Case that FIRST propounded the doctrine vs case that FIRST limited the amending power
- Golaknath (1967) = the first case to limit the amending power; Kesavananda (1973) = Basic Structure propounded
- ⚠ Kesavananda (1973) propounded the limit; Indira Gandhi (1975) = doctrine first applied
- ⚠ Sources differ: other notes say Kesavananda (1973) (not Golaknath) is the case that first propounded the limit on the amending power

### Criticism of the amendment procedure
- Criticisms:
- No special body (constitutional convention/assembly) or referendum - Parliament alone amends most provisions; no outside agency
- No joint sitting - no mechanism to resolve deadlock between Houses on an amendment Bill
- No time limit for State ratification
- States cannot initiate; ratification needed only for a few provisions

# MCQ NOTES OF APP TOPIC 2:18
### Article 368 basics
- Article 368, in Part XX — Parliament's power to amend the Constitution and the procedure
- Wording: Parliament may, in exercise of its constituent power, amend any provision by way of addition, variation or repeal (within the basic-structure limit)
- Amendment procedure taken from South Africa (as was the way of electing Rajya Sabha members)
- Granville Austin — process looks "complicated" but is only "diverse"; one of the best-planned parts of the Constitution
- Citizenship is changed by simple majority under Article 11, not under Article 368
- Latest enacted amendment (as of 2026): 106th Amendment (2023); the 131st was only a Bill that failed in the Lok Sabha (April 2026)
### Procedure
## Nature
- A synthesis of rigidity and flexibility — neither fully rigid nor fully flexible
## Initiation and passage
- A minister or a private member may move the Bill, with no prior permission of the President
- Can start in either House; needs a special majority in each House separately
- No joint sitting (Art. 108 does not apply) for an amendment Bill — a known criticism of the procedure
- Ordinary Bill: simple majority; deadlock between Houses goes to a joint sitting
- 24th Amendment Act, 1971 — made the President's assent obligatory for an amendment Bill
## Role of States
- A State Legislature cannot initiate an amendment; it can only ratify certain Bills passed by Parliament
- Changes to listed federal provisions need approval by at least one-half of the State Legislatures
- No time limit is set for State ratification
- President's qualifications and tenure need no ratification
- Creation/abolition of a Legislative Council follows Article 169, outside Article 368
### Three routes of amendment
## Special majority
- Special majority = majority of the total membership of each House + two-thirds of members present and voting
- Fundamental Rights and DPSPs need only the special majority procedure
## Special majority + State ratification
- Needs ratification: Seventh Schedule / distribution of legislative powers, election of the President, representation of States
- 101st Amendment Act, 2016 added the GST Council (Article 279A) to the provisions needing State ratification
- Ratification only for a limited group of federal provisions, not every amendment
- Needs only half the States, not every State; by simple majority of members present and voting in each State Legislature; no time limit
- First amendment needing State ratification: 3rd Amendment (1954), on Entry 33 of the Concurrent List
## Simple majority, outside Article 368
- Quorum in Parliament, MPs' salaries, parliamentary privileges
- Legislative Councils (Article 169)
- Citizenship, Fifth and Sixth Schedules, number of puisne judges of the Supreme Court
- The basic structure cannot be amended even by the special procedure
## Related Articles
| Subject | Provision |
| Election of the President | Articles 54 and 55 |
| GST Council | Article 279A |
| Representation of States | Fourth Schedule |
| Citizenship | Article 11 |
- Impeachment of the President (Article 61 type) — two-thirds of the total membership of the House concerned
### Bill types compared
- Constitutional amendment Bill: President must give assent; can neither withhold nor return it (made compulsory by the 24th Amendment, 1971)
- Money Bill — President may assent or withhold, but never return it
- Ordinary Bill — can be returned once for reconsideration
- Financial Bill (I) — starts only in the Lok Sabha, but the Rajya Sabha has full power to reject or amend it
### Can Parliament amend FRs?
## Sequence

- Rajendra N. Shah (2021) — Part IXB provisions on State co-operative societies struck down for lack of State ratification
- Procedure is a combination of rigidity and flexibility; the basic-structure limit allows judicial review of amendments
