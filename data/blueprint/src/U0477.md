### Types of unemployment → cause, nature, example
| Type | Cause | Nature | Example | Where predominant |
| Frictional | Transitions between jobs | Voluntary, short-term — a normal part of labour churn | A recent graduate searching for a first job | - |
| Structural | A mismatch of skills or geography | Long-term — driven by technology or industry shifts | Factory workers displaced by industrial automation | Whole economy; long-run (structural change) |
| Cyclical | A deficiency in aggregate demand | Directly tied to business cycles — recessions and downturns | Layoffs across sectors during an economic recession | Industry and services in recession |
| Seasonal | Predictable demand shifts by season | Annual, periodic and anticipated fluctuations | Agricultural labourers or resort staff unemployed during the off-season | Agriculture, tourism, agro-industries - no work in certain months (agricultural off-season) |
| Disguised | Surplus labour with zero marginal productivity — more workers than required | Employed on paper — output remains unchanged if they are removed | Five family members working on a farm plot that needs only two workers | Agriculture (family farms), rural |
| Underemployment | Underutilization of worker capacity | Working below one's skill level or involuntary part-time work | An engineer working part-time as a cashier | - |
| Institutional | Regulatory and policy barriers | Arises from labour laws, high minimum wages or union rules | Employers halting hiring because of rigid layoff regulations | - |
| Vulnerable | A lack of formal contracts and social security — daily-wage work | High job insecurity — common in informal sectors | Construction labour or unorganized street vending | - |
| Educated | Educated persons unable to find jobs matching qualification | - | - | Urban areas |
| Technological | Jobs lost due to change in technology | - | - | Manufacturing, routine services |

### Disguised, frictional, structural and cyclical → features
## 1. Disguised unemployment
- Definition: More workers engaged than actually required; marginal productivity of labour is zero or negative
- Nature: Appear employed (e.g. all members on a family plot) but add no productive value; common among family members in agriculture
- Identification: Withdrawing some workers leaves total output unchanged (MPL = 0)
- Type: Hidden (not open) unemployment; PLFS headline rates do not capture it
## 2. Frictional vs structural
| Feature | Frictional (search) | Structural |
| Cause | Time lag while changing/searching jobs | Mismatch of skills and jobs; change in technology or demand pattern |
| Nature | Normal, exists even at full employment | Deep-rooted; needs reskilling/structural reform |
| Example | Worker quits to look for a better job | Labour-intensive economy turning capital-intensive |
- Technological unemployment: Job loss due to changes in technology; sub-type of structural unemployment
- Drivers: Automation in manufacturing and AI in services may reduce demand for low-skilled, routine jobs
## 3. Cyclical unemployment
- Cause: Fall in aggregate demand for goods and services during recession/depression → firms lay off workers to cut costs
- Exam angle: "unemployment caused by depression in an economy" = Cyclical (APPSC 2019).
### Other classifications → meaning
## 1. Paired classifications
| Term | Meaning |
| Open | Able and willing to work but no job at all |
| Hidden/disguised | Appears employed, contributes nothing |
| Voluntary | Unwilling at prevailing wage (high benefits, high marginal tax, waiting for better job) |
| Involuntary | Willing at going wage but no job; from market structure/aggregate demand |
| Visible underemployment | Working fewer than normal hours |
| Invisible underemployment | Full-time work but very low income or work below one's ability (e.g. MBA working as a driver) |
- Labour force counts only involuntary unemployment
## 2. Other types
| Term | Meaning |
| Long-term | Unemployed > 12 months |
| Unemployment trap | Benefits discourage work |
| Full employment | Everyone willing at prevailing wage finds work; frictional + structural still exist |
| Natural rate of unemployment | = frictional + structural at full employment |
### Economists → laws and statements on unemployment
| Economist | Statement / contribution |
| Keynes | Unemployment in developed economies = deficiency in effective demand (cyclical/demand-deficient) |
| Milton Friedman | Associated with the natural rate of unemployment |
| A.W. Phillips (1958) | Phillips curve: inverse relationship between unemployment and inflation - lower unemployment ↔ higher inflation |
| Friedman-Phelps | Long-run Phillips curve vertical at the natural rate |
| Arthur Okun | Okun's law: 1 percentage point rise in unemployment above natural rate → roughly 2% fall in output below potential GDP |
- Phillips curve breakdown: In stagflation (high inflation + high unemployment)
- Okun's implication: Inverse link between growth and unemployment
- ⚠ Phillips = unemployment vs inflation; Okun = unemployment vs output

### Nature of India's unemployment — predominant type and causes
## 1. Predominant type
- Core nature: Indian unemployment is basically structural - labour demand < supply; long-run
- Due to rapid population growth + labour immobility + shift to capital-intensive production
- Other features: High seasonal + disguised unemployment (large agricultural workforce) + underemployment
- Differs from demand-deficient (Keynesian) unemployment of developed countries
## 2. Causes
- India-specific structural causes:
- Neglected structural transformation of secondary sector
- Jobless growth - growth driven by capital-intensive sectors (IT, finance), not labour-intensive manufacturing
- Less focus on MSMEs
- Stringent labour laws; delays in paperwork; slow infrastructure
- Import-oriented rather than export-led economy
- Exam angle: odd-one-out questions list causes (population growth, slow industrialisation, faulty education) and slip in a non-cause such as "high growth of labour-intensive manufacturing".
### Universal angles
- Current relevance: Monthly PLFS (CWS) UR rose to 5.5% in May 2026 from 5.2% in April (16 Jun 2026)
- Monthly data mostly reflect seasonal movement; 30-35 million seasonal labourers migrate annually (7 Sep 2026)
- AP angle (PLFS 2023-24, 2024-25):
- Youth (15-29) 17.5%; urban female youth 29.1%
- Criticism & debates: Falling UR may reflect distress entry into low-productivity work, not real job creation
- Comparative note: India = structural + disguised; developed countries = mainly cyclical (demand-deficient) (Keynes)
