### Local time — rules and worked examples
## 1. Longitude and time
| Longitude difference | Time |
| 1° | 4 minutes (Earth turns 360° in 24 h; 1° = 4 minutes) |
| 15° | 1 hour (15° = 1 hour[GK]) |
| 180° | 12 hours |
| 1 minute of arc (1') | 4 seconds of time [GK] |
| Place to the east | Time is ahead - add [GK] |
| Place to the west | Time is behind - subtract [GK] |
- Longitude difference produces local-time difference
- One degree of longitude = 4 minutes of time difference; fifteen degrees = 1 hour
- Eastward movement: Local time advances; westward movement: local time retards (decreases)
- Time increases toward the east; decreases toward the west — East is ahead (add time), West is behind (subtract)
## 2. Working it out
- Longitude difference:
- Opposite sides (one E, one W) → add the degrees
- Steps: Longitude difference x 4 min = time difference → add for east, subtract for west
- Message/flight problems: Local arrival time = departure local time + journey time +/- longitude time difference (add when flying east)
- Worked PYQ (APPSC-G1 2019): A = 60°W at 10 a.m.; B = 90°E
- Difference 150° x 4 = 600 min = 10 h; B is east → 8 p.m.
- Finding longitude: Local time 2 h behind Greenwich → 30°W

### International Date Line — course, places and crossing rule
## 1. Course
- Approximately 180° longitude — the nearest strait: the Bering Strait
- 180° meridian: Associated with the International Date Line — the date changes across it
- Crossing eastward: Subtract 1 day; crossing westward: add 1 day
- IDL: Imaginary line from North Pole to South Pole roughly along the 180° meridian
- Through the middle of the Pacific Ocean (APPSC 2021) and the Bering Strait
- Zigzags: zigzags to avoid land / keep island groups on one date
- Around the Bering Strait (Russia-Alaska), Siberia (Chukotka), Aleutians, Kiribati, Fiji, Tonga, Samoa (the Fiji-Tonga-Kiribati islands)
- Samoa moved to the Asian side of the line in 2011

## 2. Solstices

## 3. Equinoxes
- Equinox: Approximately equal day and night — occurs twice a year (two equinoxes per year) — a transition point between seasons
- Dates: March equinox approximately 20 or 21 March; September equinox approximately 22 or 23 September — the equinoxes occur in March and September
- The direct rays of the Sun fall on the Equator; both hemispheres have approximately equal day and night
- March equinox: Spring begins in the Northern Hemisphere; autumn begins in the Southern Hemisphere
- September equinox: Autumn begins in the Northern Hemisphere; spring begins in the Southern Hemisphere
- APPSC-G1 2019 match: Winter solstice - 22 Dec; summer solstice - 21 June; vernal equinox - 21 March; autumnal equinox - 23 Sept

## 2. The poles
- North Pole: Six months of continuous daylight (around June) and six months of darkness (around December)
- North Pole is continuously lit from ~21 March to ~23 September; South Pole in the other half-year
- South Pole: The opposite daylight and darkness pattern — continuous darkness around June, continuous daylight around December
- Polar day and polar night each last approximately six months
- Main reason: Axial inclination combined with revolution — the 23½° axial tilt and revolution tilt a pole toward the Sun; during polar day the Sun remains above the horizon
- Continuous daylight: Beyond the Arctic Circle during Northern Hemisphere summer; beyond the Antarctic Circle during Southern Hemisphere summer
### Axial tilt and inclination — terms, consequences and changes
## 1. Axial tilt and parallelism
- Axial inclination: 23.5° (23½°) from the perpendicular to the orbital plane — the axis makes an angle of 66.5° (66½°) with — is inclined to — the orbital plane
- ⚠ 23½° from the perpendicular = 66½° to the orbital plane (same tilt, two references)
- A major factor in seasons — changes the angle and duration of solar illumination
- Parallelism: Earth's axis maintains approximately the same orientation — remains approximately parallel — as Earth revolves
- Produces opposite seasons — seasonal reversal — between the two hemispheres
- Consequences: Seasons; unequal day length; positions of tropics (23½°) and polar circles (66½°)
## 2. Changes
| Term | Meaning |
| Precession | Axis wobbles (precession) → pole star changes; celestial pole pointed to Vega ~14,000 years ago; will again in ~12,000 years |
| Milankovitch cycles | Orbital and axial cycles drive long-period climate change |
- Observed change: Melting polar and glacier ice shifts mass away from the axis → slows rotation
### Debates — the two-time-zone question
- Problem: North-east sees sunrise far earlier than the west; 30 deg of longitude span → about 2 hours local-time difference between Arunachal and Gujarat
- Proposals:
- Advance IST by 30 minutes (30 min)
- Separate zone ~1 h ahead for the North-East, keeping 82.5° E (Kakinada) as the reference
- Assam tea gardens: Informal "Bagan time"
### Universal angles
- Current relevance:
- Legal Metrology (IST) Rules, 2026 notified 27 Aug 2026 → IST the single legal time reference
- White Rabbit IST network
- Significance: Synchronised IST time-stamps for banking/digital payments, railways, airports, power grids, telecom
- Protects against GPS spoofing

- Criticism & debates: One time zone for a ~2-hour-wide country costs the north-east daylight hours; separate-zone proposals undecided

## Tropic of Cancer
- ★ 23°30′ N divides India into almost two equal halves; does not divide the international boundary into halves
- ★ Crosses: Gujarat, Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand (not Uttar Pradesh); Bihar (Jharkhand) in older keys
- ★ Manipur lies north of it
- ★ Nearest cities by key: Jabalpur (23°11′N; Ujjain 23°9′, Ahmedabad 23°2′, Varanasi 25°18′), Kolkata (22°30′–22°37′N), Gandhinagar
- ★ Mahi: starts in MP, enters Rajasthan at Banswara, crosses the Tropic twice, into the Gulf of Khambhat
## IST and India
- ★ City closest to IST meridian: Rewa; meridian passes through Mirzapur district, UP
- ★ India's latitudinal and longitudinal extent each about 30° (8°4′–37°6′N; 68°7′–97°25′E)
- ★ Gujarat to Arunachal time gap: two hours (not two and a half); 82°30′ E is the Standard Meridian
- ★ Midnight at IST, place at 6 A.M.: 172°30′ E (90° east)
## Time zones
- ★ More than four time zones: UK (9), Denmark (5; 6 with Danish Realm), New Zealand (5), Australia (9): only four; Brazil exactly 4
- ★ Same time as GMT: Accra, Dublin, Lisbon (not Madrid)
### Solstices and equinoxes — the year at a glance
- ★ Uttarayana: Sun's apparent northward movement, 23 December to 21 June
- ★ Equinox ("equal day and night"): 21 March and 23 September; Sun overhead at the Equator
- ★ Summer solstice: 21 June
- ★ Longest day in the Southern Hemisphere: 22 December
- ★ Longest day in June (of the options): Delhi (NH days grow longer after Sun moves north)
### Day length through the year and the poles
- ★ On 21 June, the Sun does not set at the Arctic Circle; rays direct on the Tropic of Cancer
- ★ NH longest day: second half of June (20 or 21 June); SH: second fortnight of December
- ★ At NH summer solstice, 12-hour day at the Equator
- ★ India extends 8°4′N–37°6′N and 68°7′E–97°25′E (options keyed "None of the above")
- ★ Stars rise perpendicular to the horizon at the Equator
### Axial tilt and inclination — terms, consequences and changes
- ★ To walk east using the pole star: keep the pole star to the left (it is in the north)
- ★ Date Line at 180° meridian, so 12 hours (180 × 4 = 720 min) from Greenwich
### Debates — the two-time-zone question
- Pole star (Polaris) shows north; to walk east, keep the pole star on your left
