### Supreme Court — establishment, constitutional basis, seat and FIRSTs
## 1. Establishment
- Part V, Chapter IV: Articles 124 to 147 (Arts 124-147)
- Inaugurated: 28 January 1950 — succeeded the Federal Court (set up under the GoI Act, 1935) and the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council
- Inaugurated in part of the old Parliament House; Jan 28, 1950 = first sitting of the SC (FIRST/LAST)
- Federal Court functioned from 1937; replaced by the SC on 26 Jan 1950
- Moved to its Tilak Marg building in 1958
- Privy Council appeals abolished in 1949
- ⚠ Trap: 1937 Federal Court; 1949 Privy Council appeals end; 26 Jan 1950 SC replaces it; 28 Jan 1950 first sitting; 1958 Tilak Marg
## 2. Constitutional basis and role
- Article 124: Establishment and constitution of the Supreme Court; appointment of judges
- 124(1): Parliament alone can increase the number of judges by law
- 124(2): Judges retire at 65 years
- Article 130 — seat: Delhi — the Chief Justice may decide other places for sittings with the President's approval
- No regional benches yet; private member's Bill for regional benches introduced in 2026
- Role: Custodian of the Constitution → final interpreter and guardian of Fundamental Rights
- Bench size: Usually 2 or 3 judges; 5+ for constitutional matters

### Appointment, qualifications and tenure — Articles 124 to 128 — Appointment (Art. 124(2))
## 1. Chief Justice of India
- Heads the Union Judiciary
- Appointed by the President — Article 124(2); by convention, the senior-most SC judge
- Term: No fixed term in years — holds office until 65 years of age
- Oath: By the President (or a person he appoints) — form in the Third Schedule
- Resignation: In writing to the President
## 2. Appointment (Art. 124(2))
- Appointing authority: The President appoints judges after consulting the CJI; for other judges consulting the CJI is compulsory
- CJI: Appointed by the President (CJI is appointed by the President, APPSC-GS 2025 key)
- Convention upheld by the Court: senior-most judge becomes CJI
- Senior-most as CJI: Convention broken in 1973 (A.N. Ray) and 1977 (M.H. Beg)
- Second Judges Case said only the senior-most should be CJI
## 3. Qualifications (Art. 124(3))
- Eligibility: A citizen of India, and either:
- 5 years as an HC judge
- 10 years as an HC advocate
- A distinguished jurist in the President's opinion → never used in 76 years (Justice Bhuyan, 31 Aug 2026)
### Appointment, qualifications and tenure — Articles 124 to 128 — Acting, ad hoc and retired judges
## 1. Acting, ad hoc and retired judges
- Acting Chief Justice — Article 126: Appointed by the President when the CJI's office is vacant, the CJI is absent or unable to perform duties (APPCA)
- Ad hoc judges — Article 127: Appointed by the Chief Justice, with the previous consent of the President and after consulting the concerned High Court Chief Justice — to meet a lack of quorum (APP, APPCA)
- Appointee: a qualified High Court judge (an HC judge qualified for the SC)
- Retired judges — Article 128: The Chief Justice may request them to attend sittings, with the President's prior consent (President's previous consent)
- Also needs the judge's consent (APP; APPSC-G2 2025 key "Both i and ii")
- Who: a retired SC judge (or a qualified retired HC judge; a retired SC, Federal Court or HC judge)
- Art. 128 status: Full powers of an SC judge but not deemed a judge of the court (not deemed regular judges)
- Additional judges: No additional judges in the SC; additional and acting judges only in High Courts (Art. 224)
- ⚠ Trap: 126 = acting CJI (President); 127 = ad hoc (CJI, HC judge); 128 = retired judges (both consents)
## 2. Retirement and resignation
- Retirement: At 65 (65 years)
- Age determined by the authority and manner Parliament provides by law (Art. 124(2A), 15th Amendment 1963)
- Resignation: Addressed to the President

### Judges cases → holding, and the collegium — Collegium
## 1. Collegium
- Governs: Appointment and transfer of Supreme Court and High Court judges
- Not a constitutional body — not expressly in the Constitution, not created by an Act of Parliament — judicially evolved through the Judges Cases (Supreme Court rulings)
- No mention in the Constitution (No place in the Constitution or any amendment) → judicial creation of the Three Judges Cases
- Formal appointment: By the President — SC judges under Article 124(2), HC judges under Article 217(1) — on the Collegium's binding recommendations ("consultation" with the prescribed collegium = concurrence)
- Executive representation: Zero
- Government's role: IB background checks; can return a name once → if the Collegium reiterates, it is bound to appoint
- Timeline: Created by Second Judges case, 1993; in present form since 1998
## 2. Collegium compositions
- SC appointments and elevations: CJI + four senior-most SC judges — five members
- High Court appointments: Initiated by the HC Chief Justice + two senior-most HC judges → forwarded to the SC Collegium of the CJI + two senior-most SC judges
- High Court transfers: CJI + four senior-most SC judges, consulting the Chief Justices of the transferring and receiving High Courts
| Collegium | Members | Handles |
| Supreme Court | CJI + 4 senior-most SC judges (5 members) | SC appointments, HC transfers |
| For HC appointments (SC side) | CJI + 2 senior-most SC judges | HC judges and HC Chief Justices |
| High Court | HC Chief Justice + 2 senior-most HC judges | Initiates HC appointments |
- SC collegium (June 2026): CJI Surya Kant (CJI Surya Kant), Vikram Nath, B.V. Nagarathna, M.M. Sundresh and P.S. Narasimha
- Narasimha joined after Justice J.K. Maheshwari retired (29 Jun 2026)

### NJAC and the 99th Amendment, 2014 — struck down in 2015

### Removal of judges — Art. 124(4), Judges (Inquiry) Act 1968, attempts
## 1. Removal — Article 124(4)
- By order of the President after an Address by Parliament
- Grounds: Proved misbehaviour or incapacity (only proved misbehaviour or incapacity)

### Jurisdictions → Articles — original, writ, appellate, advisory — Jurisdiction map
## 1. Original jurisdiction — Article 131 (exclusive)
- Federal disputes involving a legal right:
- One State vs another State (State vs state; Between two or more states)
- Excludes:
- Political questions that involve no legal right
- Treaties and agreements made before the Constitution (pre-Constitution treaties)
- Inter-State water disputes — Article 262 (Inter-state river waters (Art. 262))
- Finance Commission matters — Article 280
- Recovery of damages by a private party against the Union
- Adjustment of expenses between Centre and states (Art. 290)
- Election disputes of Parliament and state legislatures → go to High Courts (APPSC-G1 2022 key: "II and III" excluded)
- Art. 32 writ jurisdiction: Also original but not exclusive (HCs have Art. 226)
- President and Vice-President elections: Disputes = original, exclusive and final

### Jurisdictions → Articles — original, writ, appellate, advisory — Appellate jurisdiction and Constitution Bench
## 1. Appellate jurisdiction
- Article 132 — constitutional: High Court judgments involving a substantial question of law on interpreting the Constitution — needs a High Court certificate (Article 134A)
- Article 133 — civil: Civil proceedings with a substantial question of law of general importance — the High Court certifies under Article 134A that the SC must decide it
- Article 134 — criminal: Where a High Court reversed an acquittal into a death sentence, or withdrew a trial to itself — capital-punishment reversals appealable as of right, without a certificate
- Article 136 — Special Leave Petition: Discretionary, plenary and residual power — special leave to appeal from any judgment or order of any court or tribunal
- Article 136(2): Not against courts-martial / Military Tribunals / Armed Forces Courts
## 2. Constitution Bench — Article 145(3)
- At least five judges — for cases with a substantial question of law on interpreting the Constitution, and for Article 143 references
## 3. Other powers (Arts 129 to 145)
| Article | Jurisdiction / power |
| 132 / 133 / 134 | Appeals: constitutional / civil / criminal cases from HCs |
| 136 | Special Leave Petition: discretionary appeal from any court or tribunal except military ones |
| 129 | Court of record, with power to punish contempt of itself |
| 145 | Rules; at least 5 judges for constitutional questions |
### Jurisdictions → Articles — original, writ, appellate, advisory — Review, advice and complete justice
## 1. Review, advice and complete justice
- Article 137 — review: The SC reviews its own judgments, orders and pronouncements — distinct from judicial review of statutes; subject to Parliamentary law and court rules
- Article 143 — advisory: The President refers questions of law or fact of public importance
- 143(1): SC may tender (or refuse) its opinion on matters of public importance
- 143(2): SC must (is bound to) tender its opinion on pre-constitutional disputes
- Opinions are strictly advisory — not binding on the President
- The 16th Presidential Reference (Nov 2025) answered questions on bill timelines (see B-37)
- Article 141: Law declared by the SC is binding on all courts in India
- Article 142: Pass any decree or order for complete justice in any cause pending before it — unhindered by ordinary statutory limitations
- Declared a Right to Safe Travel under Art. 21 + nationwide safety directives (27 May 2026)
- HCs have no equivalent power (Anil Kumar Jain v. Maya Jain)
- ⚠ Trap: 131 = original (exclusive); 32 = writ (not exclusive, HCs Art. 226); 136 = SLP; 143 = advisory (not binding)

### Judges cases
## The Judges Cases
| Case | Year | Holding |
| First (S.P. Gupta) | 1981 | Consultation does not mean concurrence; executive primacy |
| Second (SCAORA v. Union of India) | 1993 | Reversed 1981; consultation means concurrence; created the (two-judge) collegium; judges' primacy |
| Third (Presidential reference) | 1998 | Collegium enlarged to CJI + four senior-most judges; plural consultation; settled working of collegium |
| Fourth | 2015 | Struck down the NJAC |
- Collegium — made by judges in the Three Judges Cases; not in Constitution
- Assertion-Reason: Second Judges Case created the collegium because it held consultation means concurrence (R explains A)
## NJAC and the 99th Amendment
- 99th Constitutional Amendment, 2014 — added Article 124A (NJAC); tried to replace the collegium
- Ratified by 16 States; assent 31 December 2014; in force 13 April 2015
- Struck down in 2015 — executive role hurt judicial independence, part of the Basic Structure (R explains A)
- Fourth Judges Case 4:1; lone dissent J. Chelameswar (upheld NJAC); Bench led by J.S. Khehar
## Appointment, transfer, misconduct
- Formal appointment — Article 124(2) (SC judges) and Article 217(1) (HC judges), by the President
- Transfer of an HC Chief Justice — Article 222
- In-House Procedure (1999) — followed C. Ravichandran Iyer (1995); for misconduct short of removal
- Retired HC judge may plead only in the Supreme Court and other High Courts (Article 220); Article 124(7) fully bars a retired SC judge
- Reform ideas: judicial standards and accountability law, permanent disciplinary authority, asset disclosure, RTI (administrative side), transparent appointments — not abolition of the collegium by executive notification
## Jurisdiction and comparisons
- Article 131 — federal disputes only; a dispute between private companies is outside it
- Election disputes concerning Parliament and State Legislatures go to the High Courts
- 'Procedure established by law' (India) — narrower review than US 'due process of law' (substantive + procedural); US Court called a 'third chamber'
- Supreme Court replaced the Federal Court (1935 Act) and the Privy Council's Judicial Committee; Council of States is the Rajya Sabha, not a court
### Collegium vs NJAC
- Government may return a name once; bound to appoint if the collegium reiterates
- HC appointments started by the HC Chief Justice + two senior-most HC judges, then to CJI + two senior SC judges
- SC collegium — five members: CJI + four senior-most judges; no executive member

## Appeals
- Article 134 — HC reverses acquittal and sentences to death: appeal lies as of right, no HC certificate
- Article 136 — special leave discretionary, against any judgment of any court or tribunal, except military tribunals (136(2)); needs no HC certificate
- Article 137 review = the Court's own judgments, different from judicial review of laws
- Curative petition — after a review under Article 137 is dismissed; based on inherent powers under Article 142
## Advisory (Article 143)
- 143(1) — President may refer questions of law or fact of public importance; Court may give its opinion
- 143(2) — pre-Constitution treaty/agreement matters: Court must give its opinion
- Advice does not bind the President
## Appointment and transfer
- CJI appointed by the President (Article 124(2)); by convention the senior-most judge; collegium only recommends
- Article 222 — HC judge transferred by the President after consulting the CJI

### High Court vs Supreme Court
## Composition and appointment
- Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Act, 1956 — under Article 124(1); Parliament raises number by law, simple majority
- HC Chief Justice appointment begins with CJI + two senior-most SC judges, then Law Minister, State, appointment by President
- High Court judges appointed by the President (not the Governor), by warrant under hand and seal; both SC and HC judges resign to the President
- Additional judges — only in High Courts (Article 224, with acting judges); none in the Supreme Court
- Ad hoc judge of SC — appointed by the CJI with the President's consent (not President on collegium advice)
- HC transfers — CJI + four senior-most SC judges + Chief Justices of both High Courts
- HC judge retires at 62; SC judge at 65
## Removal
- SC judge — Article 124(4); HC judge — Article 218
- CAG (Article 148) and CEC (Article 324(5)) removed like an SC judge
- No SC judge removed so far; motion against V. Ramaswami failed; Soumitra Sen resigned after Rajya Sabha passed the motion

### Removal & independence
- Removal motion — signed by 100 Lok Sabha or 50 Rajya Sabha members
- Constitution uses 'removal', not 'impeachment', for judges
- Article 121 — judge's conduct discussed in Parliament only on a removal motion
- Removal address needs special majority in each House (majority of total membership + two-thirds present and voting), not simple majority
- Grounds — proved misbehaviour or incapacity
- Judges' salaries fixed by Parliament by law; cannot be cut after appointment except in a Financial Emergency (Article 360)

### NJAC and the 99th Amendment, 2014 — struck down in 2015
- ★ 99th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2014 (NJAC) — declared unconstitutional on 15 October 2015 by a five-judge Constitution Bench
- ★ Ground — broke the basic structure by weakening judicial independence; collegium reaffirmed
- ★ NJAC composition — CJI as ex-officio Chairperson, two senior-most SC judges next to the CJI, and the other listed members (all statements correct); India has no NJAC now
- ★ Of CJI, SC/HC judges, Attorney General and Governors, only the CJI keyed as appointed on the PM's recommendation (simplified framing); SC/HC judges go through the collegium
