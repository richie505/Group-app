# NOTES OF APP TOPIC 2:14 - Right to Constitutional Remedies - Article 32; the five writs and their scope
### Article 32: clauses
- Art. 32 = "the very soul of the Constitution and the very heart of it" - Dr. B.R. Ambedkar
- 32(1): move Supreme Court directly - itself a Fundamental Right (APPSC GS 2025)
- 32(2): directions, orders, writs (five named)
- 32(3): Parliament may empower other courts - no such law
- 32(4): not suspended except as Constitution provides (Art. 359)
- Only FRs; original but not exclusive; part of basic structure
- Writs from English common law (prerogative writs)
### The five writs
| Writ | Meaning | Against | Purpose |
| Habeas Corpus | "To have the body" | Public authorities and private individuals | Release from unlawful detention |
| Mandamus | "We command" | Public officials, inferior courts, tribunals | Perform public legal duty |
| Prohibition | "To forbid" | Judicial/quasi-judicial only | Stop excess of jurisdiction (pending) |
| Certiorari | "To be certified" | Judicial, quasi-judicial, administrative (since 1991) | Quash order already passed |
| Quo-Warranto | "By what authority?" | Holder of public office | Stop illegal usurpation |
### Writ limits
- Mandamus not against: private bodies, President, Governors (Art. 361), discretionary duty
- Mandamus can lie against govt company performing public function
- No mandamus to make a law / in absence of policy (A-R G2 2025)
- Quo-warranto: any public-minded citizen can file; not for ministerial/private office
- Habeas corpus: only writ directly against private individuals
### Prohibition vs Certiorari
| Point | Prohibition | Certiorari |
| Stage | Proceedings pending | After order passed |
| Nature | Preventive only | Preventive and curative |
| Effect | Stops lower body | Quashes order |
### Art. 32 vs Art. 226
| Point | Art. 32 (SC) | Art. 226 (HC) |
| Nature | Fundamental Right | Discretionary remedy |
| Scope | Only FRs (narrower) | FRs + "any other purpose" (wider) |
| Territory | Whole of India | Own territory (15th Amendment, 1963) |
### Cases & amendments
- 1976 — 42nd: Art. 32A inserted, Art. 226 curbed
- 1977 — 43rd: omitted 32A, 131A, 144A, 226A, 228A
- 1981 — S.P. Gupta: PIL (Bhagwati, Krishna Iyer)
- 1997 — L. Chandra Kumar: Art. 226 = basic structure
- 2026 — SC to examine whether ED can invoke writ jurisdiction
### PIL & res judicata
- PIL relaxes locus standi; not itself an FR
- Epistolary jurisdiction: letters as petitions (Sunil Batra)
- HC dismisses on merits → no fresh Art. 32 on same facts; only appeal
- Before 1950 only Calcutta, Bombay, Madras HCs issued writs

### Article 32 — clauses, scope, status and FIRSTs
## 1. Right to Constitutional Remedies — Article 32 (one Article)
- Right to move the Supreme Court for enforcement of Fundamental Rights — itself a Fundamental Right
- Dr. B.R. Ambedkar: "Heart and Soul" of the Constitution
## 2. Clauses of Article 32
- 32(1): right to move the Supreme Court (Supreme Court directly) by appropriate proceedings to enforce Part III rights - itself a Fundamental Right (APPSC GS 2025)
- 32(2): SC may issue directions, orders or writs incl. the five named
- Article 32(3): Parliament may by law empower any other court to exercise these powers within its local jurisdiction - no such law made so far → only SC and HCs issue writs
- 32(4): right not suspended except as the Constitution provides
- Scope: only Fundamental Rights (not statutory or other constitutional rights); jurisdiction original but not exclusive
- SC writs: only for FRs, not statutory rights (UPSC)
## 3. Status and significance
- Basic Structure: part of the basic structure - cannot be abridged even by amendment (UPSC)
- Rationale: a right without a remedy is worthless
## 4. FIRSTs and numbers
| FIRST / NUMBER | FACT |
| Number of writs | Five writs - Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Certiorari, Quo-Warranto |
| Origin | Prerogative remedies borrowed from English common law - "prerogative writs" of the King, the "fountain of justice" (UPSC) |
| Before 1950 | only the Calcutta, Bombay and Madras HCs could issue writs; Art. 226 now empowers all HCs |
### Writs → literal meaning, purpose and scope
| Writ | Literal meaning | Issued against | Purpose | Does not lie against |
| Habeas Corpus | "To have the body" / "Let us have the body" ("to have the body of") | Public authorities and private individuals | Release from unlawful or illegal detention — safeguards individual liberty (bodily freedom); produce a detained person before court | Lawful detention, contempt of court/legislature, detention outside jurisdiction [GK] |
| Mandamus | "We command" | Public officials, statutory bodies, inferior courts, tribunals; public corporations, govt | Compels performance of a mandatory public or legal duty refused or neglected (public legal duty) | Private individuals/bodies; President, Governors[GK]; discretionary duty or legislative functions, contractual obligation, non-statutory instructions [GK]; Chief Justice of a HC acting judicially [GK] |
| Prohibition | "To forbid" / "To restrain" | Judicial and quasi-judicial bodies only | A higher court stops a lower court or tribunal from exceeding or usurping jurisdiction — while the proceeding is pending; Preventive | Administrative authorities, legislative bodies, private persons [GK] |
| Certiorari | "To be certified" / "To be informed" | Judicial, quasi-judicial and administrative bodies (administrative: certain bodies affecting rights, since 1991) | Quashes an illegal order already passed — lack or excess of jurisdiction, or error of law on the face of the record (postmortem review); also transfers a case from a lower court; curative and preventive | Legislative bodies, private persons [GK] |
| Quo-Warranto | "By what authority?" / "By what warrant?" | A person holding or claiming a substantive public office | Prevents illegal usurpation of a permanent public office created by statute or the Constitution; inquires into the legality of a claim to a public office | Ministerial or private office [GK] |
- Recap wording: Mandamus directs a public official, inferior court or statutory authority; Certiorari quashes for lack of jurisdiction or an error of law
### Writs — PYQ match, Habeas Corpus and Quo Warranto
- Match (PYQ G2 2025):
- Quo warranto = by what authority
- Mandamus = we command
- Habeas corpus = to have the body
- Certiorari = to be certified
- Habeas Corpus: Applies whenever detention (custody) is illegal — the only writ directly against private individuals in cases of illegal confinement
- Producing a person before court: habeas corpus (APPSC 2021)
- Writ safeguarding personal liberty - the "bulwark of individual liberty against arbitrary detention"; the greatest safeguard of liberty (UPSC)
- Quo-Warranto: Challenges the legal qualification or title of a person holding public office
- Any public-minded citizen (any member of the public) can move a petition — no aggrieved-party status needed (the petitioner need not be personally aggrieved); the traditional locus standi requirement is relaxed
- Prep wording: may be sought by any interested person, not only the aggrieved
- Art. 361: President and Governors not answerable to any court for their official acts
### Writ limits — Mandamus; Prohibition vs Certiorari
## 1. Mandamus limits
- Mandamus:
- Does not lie against purely private bodies unless they discharge a public duty
- Does not lie against the President or Governors
- Can lie against a government company or statutory corporation performing a public function
- ⚠ Sources differ: other notes say mandamus does not lie against a private individual/body at all (no public-duty exception stated)
- Mandamus and law/policy:
- Cannot lie in the absence of a policy; once a policy is laid down its terms can be enforced
- PYQ A-R G2 2025: both true, R explains A
- Mandamus not against: a non-statutory departmental instruction (see the writs table)
## 2. Prohibition vs Certiorari
- Prohibition: Before the order — halts a pending proceeding to prevent excess of jurisdiction
- Certiorari: After the order — corrects or quashes decisions with jurisdictional error
| Point | Prohibition | Certiorari |
| Stage | While proceedings are pending | After an order is passed (also to transfer a pending case) |
| Nature | Preventive only; directs inactivity | Preventive and curative; Curative when issued after the order/decision |
| Effect | Stops the lower body from continuing | Quashes the order already passed |
- Common features: both only against judicial/quasi-judicial bodies (traditionally); both check excess of jurisdiction
- ⚠ Prohibition = pending, preventive only; Certiorari = after order, preventive and curative
### Article 32 vs Article 226 — SC and HC writ jurisdiction
- Supreme Court — Article 32: Narrower — only for Fundamental Rights (Part III); a direct and effective Fundamental Right to constitutional remedies
- A Fundamental Right to approach the SC directly for enforcement of Part III rights — the SC cannot refuse an Article 32 petition
- High Courts — Article 226: Wider — can be approached for Fundamental Rights and any other purpose, including ordinary statutory or legal rights (legal rights)
| Point | Art. 32 (SC) | Art. 226 (HC) |
| Nature | Itself a Fundamental Right | Constitutional (discretionary) remedy |
| Rights enforced / scope | Only FRs (Fundamental Rights) | FRs and "any other purpose" (legal rights) - wider |
| Territory | Whole of India | Own territory; beyond it if cause of action arises within (15th Amendment, 1963) |
| Writs | Five | Five |
- Jurisdiction: original but not exclusive - concurrent with HCs for FRs; aggrieved person may choose either
- Guardian of Fundamental Rights = the Judiciary (Supreme Court as "protector and guarantor") (APPSC 2021)
- Shared role: HCs share the role under Art. 226
- Res judicata: HC dismisses a writ on merits → same plea cannot go to the SC under Art. 32 (fresh Art. 32 petition on same facts barred), only in appeal; dismissed not on merits → SC can be moved (UPSC)

# MCQ NOTES OF APP TOPIC 2:14
### Article 32: clauses
## Status and significance
- ★ Article 32 — called the "heart and soul" of the Constitution by Dr. B.R. Ambedkar
- Makes the right to a remedy itself a Fundamental Right; direct access to the Supreme Court
- 'A right without a remedy is a worthless declaration' — shows why Article 32 matters; makes Part III effective, not only declaratory
- Article 32 is part of the basic structure — cannot be reduced even by constitutional amendment
## Clauses
- Article 32(2) — five writs: Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Certiorari, Quo Warranto
- Article 32(3) — Parliament may let any other court use SC's Article 32 powers; no such law made yet
- Article 32(4) — right cannot be suspended except as the Constitution provides (not by an ordinary law)
- SC jurisdiction under Article 32 — original but not exclusive (High Courts also issue writs under Article 226)
## Article 32 vs Article 226
- Both SC and High Courts can issue all five writs
- Article 226 — writs for Fundamental Rights and 'any other purpose' → HC wider in subject matter; SC wider territorially
- HC can issue writs beyond its territory where the cause of action, wholly or partly, arises inside it — after the Fifteenth Amendment (1963)
- Before 1950, only the Calcutta, Bombay and Madras High Courts had writ jurisdiction
- Writs began as prerogative remedies of English common law
## Writ points
- Habeas Corpus — lies against public authorities and private individuals; no release where detention is lawful
- Quo Warranto — checks a claim to a substantive public office made by statute or the Constitution
- Any member of the public (any interested person) can seek it; literally 'by what authority or warrant'
- Mandamus — not usually against private bodies, nor for discretionary or legislative functions or non-statutory instructions
- Certiorari — usually post-decision, but may also be used to transfer a pending case
## PIL and access
- PIL loosens the rule of locus standi (only the directly aggrieved person may approach the court)
- S.P. Gupta (1981) — key PIL case; Justices P.N. Bhagwati and V.R. Krishna Iyer linked with PIL
- Epistolary jurisdiction — letters/postcards treated as writ petitions; Sunil Batra (prisoner's letter)
- SC studied an SOP for 24x7 urgent hearings under Articles 32 and 226 in matters of threats to life and liberty
### The five writs
## Meanings
| Writ | Literal meaning |
| Habeas Corpus | 'to have the body' |
| Mandamus | ★ 'we command' |
| Prohibition | 'to forbid' |
| Certiorari | 'to be certified or informed' |
## Mandamus
- ★ Mandamus — orders a public authority/institution to perform a duty it has refused or neglected
- ★ Judiciary orders the Executive to perform its duty
- Lies against public officials, statutory bodies, inferior courts and tribunals; not usually against private individuals
## Certiorari
- ★ Certiorari — issued by a higher court to check the working of a subordinate court; case record sent to the higher court for review
- Cancels an order already passed by a judicial, quasi-judicial or administrative body
- Grounds: lack or excess of jurisdiction, or error of law clear on the face of the record
- Since 1991 — may lie against certain administrative bodies whose actions affect rights; not against private persons or legislative bodies
## Prohibition and Habeas Corpus
- Prohibition — tells a lower judicial body to stop continuing proceedings in excess of jurisdiction (acts while pending)
- Habeas Corpus — the bulwark of individual liberty; quick remedy against unlawful detention
- Lies against both public authorities and private individuals; the only writ available against private persons
- Habeas Corpus is among the five writs under Article 32; review, advisory opinions and special leave are other kinds of jurisdiction
### Writ limits
- Mandamus cannot force the Government to make a law or policy, but can enforce the terms of a policy once laid down
- Mandamus does not lie against the President or Governors, or private bodies without public duties
- Mandamus lies against a government company or statutory corporation doing a public function
### Prohibition vs Certiorari
- Prohibition — preventive writ; acts while the case is pending
- Certiorari — curative writ; acts after an order is made
### Cases & amendments
- Article 32A (barred challenges to State laws under Article 32) — inserted by the 42nd Amendment (1976)
- 43rd Amendment omitted Articles 32A, 131A, 144A, 226A and 228A
- L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India (1997) — judicial review under Articles 226 and 227 is part of the basic structure
- 20 January 2026 — SC (Justices Dipankar Datta and S.C. Sharma) agreed to check whether the Enforcement Directorate can invoke writ jurisdiction
- Appeals by Kerala and Tamil Nadu against a Kerala High Court ruling
### PIL & res judicata
- HC dismisses a writ petition on merits → same plea under Article 32 barred by res judicata; remedy lies in appeal
- If dismissal was not on merits, Article 32 may be used
### Exam corner
- The Judiciary — guardian of Fundamental Rights; Supreme Court is their protector and guarantor
