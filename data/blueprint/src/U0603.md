### Vertical distribution, variable constituents and evolution of the atmosphere
## 1. Vertical distribution
- Carbon dioxide: Concentrated relatively low — up to approximately 20 km
- ⚠ Sources differ: other notes say CO2 and water vapour reach only up to about 90 km
- Oxygen and nitrogen extend much higher than the lower atmosphere; hydrogen extends higher than nitrogen and oxygen; oxygen negligible by 120 km
- Water vapour: Decreases with altitude — strongly concentrated near the surface or in the troposphere; more abundant in the tropics than at the poles
- Ozone: Concentrated mainly in the stratosphere
## 2. Variable constituents
- Water vapour:
- Up to 4% in warm, wet tropics; under 1% in deserts and polar air
- Absorbs insolation + keeps in earth's heat (blanket effect)
- Most abundant greenhouse gas; decides air stability
- Dust:
- Sea salt, soil, smoke, soot, ash, pollen, meteor dust
- Concentrated in lower layers; more in subtropical and temperate belts (dry winds) than equatorial and polar
- Act as hygroscopic nuclei for clouds / condensation

### Mesosphere, exosphere; temperature trend
## 1. Mesosphere
- Lies above the stratosphere — approximately 50 km to 80 km
- Temperature decreases with altitude — very cold
- Meteors burn or ablate here; upper boundary: the mesopause
- Exam angle: APPSC-G2 2025 - meteorites burn up in the mesosphere (not thermosphere/ionosphere).
## 2. Exosphere
- The outermost atmospheric region — very high and rarefied conditions; density extremely low
- Contains predominantly very light gases; gradually merges with — transitions into — outer space
- Communication satellites operate in its outer region; satellites are associated with the outer or exospheric region
## 3. Temperature trend
- Temperature trend:
- Falls in the troposphere
- → Rises in the stratosphere (ozone absorbs UV: an inversion)
- → Falls in the mesosphere
- → Rises in the thermosphere (absorption of high-energy radiation)
- Reversal (inversion) layers: Stratosphere and thermosphere
### Stratosphere, aurora; phenomena → layer
## 1. Stratosphere and the ozone layer
- Lies above the troposphere; extends up to 50 km
- Temperature increases with altitude — ⚠ upward in the stratosphere, whereas it decreases upward in the troposphere
- Ozone absorbs ultraviolet radiation; this absorption converts solar energy into heat, which causes temperature to increase upward
- Very little water vapour; clouds and weather very limited; relatively stable — suitable for jet aircraft
- Ozone layer: Ozone gas (O₃) — an ozone molecule contains three oxygen atoms
- Located mainly in the stratosphere — present mainly in the lower stratosphere; maximum concentration roughly between 15 km and 35 km
- Absorbs harmful ultraviolet radiation — protects life from excessive ultraviolet radiation
## 2. Aurora
- Caused by solar charged particles that interact with Earth's upper atmosphere or magnetic environment
- The interaction excites atmospheric particles; excited atmospheric particles produce a light display
- Aurora Borealis: Northern Hemisphere; Aurora Australis: Southern Hemisphere
## 3. Phenomena → layer (quick revision)
| Phenomenon | Layer |
| Weather, clouds, rain | Troposphere |
| Ozone layer | Stratosphere (APPSC 2025) |
| Meteors burn up | Mesosphere (APPSC-G2 2025) |
| Aurora, airglow | Thermosphere (ionosphere) |
| Radio-wave reflection | Ionosphere |
| Jet aircraft | Lower stratosphere, free of clouds and turbulence: the standard exam answer [GK] (some notes put commercial aircraft in the troposphere) |
### Troposphere — thickness, lapse rate, weather
## 1. Troposphere
- The lowest atmospheric layer — average thickness 13 km; 8 km thick at the poles, 18 km at the equator
- Normal lapse rate: 6.5°C per kilometre — approximately 1°C per 165 metres (falls 1 °C per 165 m = 6.5 °C per km); temperature is higher near the surface than at higher altitudes and very low near the tropopause
- Most atmospheric mass is concentrated here; water vapour and dust (particles) are concentrated here
- Strong vertical convection and condensation occur here — almost all weather phenomena occur in the troposphere
- Clouds, rainfall, storms and weather result from tropospheric water vapour, dust, convection and condensation
- The most important layer for biological activity
## 2. Thickness
- Thicker at the equator than at the poles (thickest at the equator, thinnest at the poles) — the chain:
- Strong solar heating causes strong convection, which transports heat upward at the equator (convection currents lift heat higher)
- Strong convection causes air to rise higher; rising air causes the tropopause to rise
- A higher tropopause produces a thicker troposphere
### Tropopause and other boundaries → height
## 1. Tropopause
- The upper boundary of the troposphere — the boundary between the troposphere and the stratosphere
- Higher at the equator (stronger convection there) and lower at the poles: ~8 km at the poles, ~18 km at the equator; higher in summer than in winter
- Separates the weather-dominated troposphere from the relatively stable stratosphere; caps the weather and vertical mixing of the troposphere
- Temperature: About -80 °C over the equator vs -45 °C over the poles
- ⚠ Tropopause higher and colder over the equator (-80 °C) than over the poles (-45 °C)
## 2. Other boundaries (upper limits)
- Upper limits: Tropopause, stratopause (~50 km), mesopause (~80 km)
### Thermosphere and ionosphere — layer → height → scientist name; radio communication
## 1. Thermosphere and ionosphere
- Thermosphere: Lies above the mesosphere — temperature rises sharply with altitude; air density extremely low; ionisation significant
- Ionosphere: Electrically charged or ionised particles — traditional approximate region 80–640 km
- ⚠ Sources differ: other notes say about 60 km to over 1,000 km; within the thermosphere, about 80-400 km
- Reflects radio waves — supports long-distance radio communication; radio communication and telecommunications are closely associated with it
- Associated with auroral phenomena — aurora occurs in the upper atmosphere or ionospheric region; also airglow
- Formation: Solar UV and X-rays strip electrons from atoms
- Daily cycle: Expands by day, shrinks at night
- Disruption: Solar flares and geomagnetic storms disrupt GNSS signals (9 Jul 2026)
## 2. Ionosphere layers
| Layer | Height | Role / note |
| D | ~60-90 km | Absorbs medium/long (low-frequency) waves; fades at night |
| E (Kennelly-Heaviside) | ~90-150 km | Reflects medium-frequency radio waves |
| F (Appleton; F1, F2 by day) | ~150 km upward (~150-400 km) | Main reflector of short-wave radio; key for long-distance radio |
- ⚠ E = Kennelly-Heaviside; F = Appleton
### Ozone layer — location, UV bands, formation cycle, role, unit
## 1. Location
- The ozone layer is mainly in the stratosphere — which extends from about 10 km to 50 km — the main ozone layer about 15 km to 30 km
- ⚠ Sources differ: other notes say densest around 15-35 km (some notes: ~20-30 km); normal peak 25-30 km
- About 90% of ozone in the stratosphere and 10% in the troposphere (per the source)
| Basis | Stratospheric ozone | Tropospheric ozone |
| Nature | Protective — absorbs harmful ultraviolet radiation; protects life on Earth | A pollutant — harmful to living organisms |
- Main function: Absorbing harmful ultraviolet radiation — the layer protects from UV radiation; Earth's shield - absorbs harmful UV-B and UV-C (APPSC 2025 / APPSC-GS 2025)
- UV-B causes skin cancer, cataract, immune suppression, lower crop yield
## 2. Ultraviolet bands
| Band | Wavelength |
| UV-A | 315–400 nm |
| UV-B | 280–315 nm |
| UV-C | 100–280 nm |
## 3. Formation, unit, ground-level ozone
- Formation - ozone-oxygen (Chapman) cycle:
- O2 + UV → O + O; O + O2 → O3
- O3 absorbs UV and splits back - a natural balance
- Ground-level (tropospheric) ozone: "Bad" secondary pollutant; part of photochemical smog; formed from NOx + VOCs in sunlight
- ⚠ Stratospheric ozone = shield; ground-level ozone = pollutant
### Ozone depletion — ODS → use; halocarbons and the ozone hole
## 1. Ozone-depleting substances
- Chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) — used as refrigerants; halons; carbon tetrachloride; hydrochlorofluorocarbons (HCFCs); methyl chloroform; methyl bromide
- Ozone depletion is associated with CFCs and other ODS
- Primary cause: Halocarbons (APPSC 2023 / APPSC-GS 2023) such as CFCs, HCFCs → release chlorine and bromine atoms that destroy ozone faster than it forms
| ODS | Use |
| CFCs | Refrigeration, ACs, aerosols, foam blowing |
| HCFCs | Transitional CFC substitute |
| Methyl bromide | Agricultural fumigant |
| Carbon tetrachloride, methyl chloroform | Solvents |
## 2. Ozone holes
- Antarctic ozone hole: Discovered by a British team
- Seasonal thinning over Antarctica, Aug-Nov (southern spring); helped by polar stratospheric clouds
- Came into focus / reported in 1985 (older notes give 1983)
- G.W. Kent Moore: Associated with the Tibetan ozone hole or halo
## 3. Halocarbons - ozone depletion and warming
- CFCs: Destroy stratospheric ozone + potent GHGs; GWP 1,000-23,000; about 1,000 times more heat-absorbent than CO2 (older notes); make up 15% of the enhanced effect
- HFCs: Replaced CFCs; do not harm ozone, but high GWP

- ★ CO2 and other greenhouse gases — good absorbers of longwave radiation
- ★ Stratospheric ozone stops ultraviolet effects on the ground
