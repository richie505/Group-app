### Biodiversity — definition, levels and FIRSTs
## 1. Levels of biodiversity
- Genetic diversity: Variation within species; species diversity: the variety of species; ecosystem diversity: the variety of ecosystems
- Walter G. Rosen: Associated with the term biodiversity
| Level | Meaning / example |
| Genetic | Variation within a species = basic source of biodiversity; all rice varieties are Oryza sativa but differ in size, aroma, nutrients |
| Species | Variety of species in an area (richness + abundance) |
| Ecosystem | Variety of ecosystems (rainforest, deciduous, boreal); one ecosystem cannot replace another (boreal conifers ≠ tropical deciduous trees) |
- Genetic example: Wild rice Oryza rufipogon (progenitor of cultivated rice) - see Agro-biodiversity
## 2. Definition
- CBD (1992) definition: Variability among living organisms from all sources (terrestrial, marine, other aquatic) + ecological complexes they belong to
- Covers diversity within species (genetic), between species and of ecosystems (= within species, between species and of ecosystems)
- CBD's three goals: Conservation + sustainable use + fair and equitable sharing of benefits from genetic resources
## 3. FIRSTs / richest
| FIRST / richest | FACT |
| Term "biodiversity" first coined | coined by W.G. Rosen (Walter G. Rosen, 1985) (UKPSC 2025) |
| Richest ecosystem on land (PYQs 2017 and 2023) | tropical rainforests (tropical moist forests) = tropical rain / moist forests |
| Richest ecosystem in the sea | coral reefs |
- ⚠ Trap: Taiga, deserts and temperate forests are the usual wrong options

### Terms → meaning: measuring biodiversity
| Measure | Scale |
| Alpha (α) diversity | Within a particular habitat — local scale; diversity within one area, community or ecosystem (species richness there) |
| Beta (β) diversity | The change or comparison of diversity between ecosystems; change (turnover) in species between ecosystems/habitats, along a habitat gradient |
| Gamma (γ) diversity | Overall diversity at a regional or geographical scale; total (overall) diversity across the ecosystems of a whole region or landscape |
| Species richness | Number of species |
| Species evenness | Relative abundance (how equally individuals are spread); low evenness = a few species dominate |
- Point richness: Species count at a single point
- Richness vs evenness example: Forest A (2 tigers, 6 deer, 7 rabbits) more even than B (1, 6, 8); both 3 species + 15 individuals
- Indices: Shannon-Wiener index + Simpson index (species diversity)
- Functional diversity: Range of ecological roles
- Functional richness: niches occupied
- Evenness: abundance spread across niches
- Divergence: dominance of extreme traits
## Species-area relationship
- Rule: Species richness rises with area, but only up to a limit
- ⚠ Alpha = within; Beta = between (turnover); Gamma = whole region
### Species richness patterns — features and causes
## Gradients
- Latitudinal gradient: Biodiversity highest in the tropics → falls from the tropics toward the poles
- Ant example: About 10 species locally near the North Pole vs up to 2,000 at the equator
## Why the tropics are richest
| Factor | Effect |
| Evolutionary time | Long, undisturbed evolutionary time (no glaciation) |
| Climate | Less seasonal, more stable/predictable → niche specialisation |
| Energy | More solar energy → higher productivity |
| Other factors | Moderate disturbance + habitat heterogeneity also raise diversity |
- Himalayas are rich: Meeting point of different biogeographical zones
- Exam angle: A/R that butterflies peak in the tropics because they cannot tolerate low temperature: both true, and R explains A (UPPSC 2019).
### Values of biodiversity — types and hypothesis
| Value | Meaning / example |
| Consumptive use [GK] | used directly by local people, not sold: food, fuelwood, fodder, wild food, medicine |
| Productive use [GK] | sold commercially: timber, fibre, medicines, drugs, gums, genetic material for crops |
| Ecosystem services | clean air and water, crop pollination, climate regulation, resilience |
| Social / cultural | sacred species and groves, indigenous knowledge, religious significance, livelihoods |
| Aesthetic | natural beauty, recreation, ecotourism |
| Option [GK] | unknown possible future uses, such as undiscovered medicines (new drugs) and crop genes |
- ⚠ Consumptive = used, not sold; Productive = sold commercially
- Role in sustainable development (APPSC 2025): Supports ecosystem services such as food, water and air quality
- Most significant aspect (UPPSC 2015): Maintenance of the ecosystem
## Rivet popper hypothesis (Paul Ehrlich)
- Analogy: Ecosystem = aeroplane; species = rivets
- Losing a few rivets seems harmless but each loss weakens it
- Losing a key rivet (keystone species) can bring it down
