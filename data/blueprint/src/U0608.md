### Tropical cyclones — meaning, formation conditions, limits of occurrence
## 1. What a cyclone is
- A cyclone is a low-pressure area — a large-scale rotating low-pressure system over warm tropical oceans; scientific name tropical cyclone; a rapid-onset disaster
- Produces strong winds, heavy rainfall and storm surge
- Origin: Only over warm oceans (not rivers or lakes - APPSC-GS 2025; PYQ 2025); draw energy from them
- Lose energy on landfall → cut off from moisture (p.20)
## 2. Conditions for formation
| Condition | Role |
| Warm sea-surface temperature — a warm tropical ocean | Supplies heat and moisture — the energy source |
| Warm, moist air | Provides latent-heat energy; moisture fuels convection |
| Coriolis force | Provides and maintains rotational circulation |
| An unstable troposphere — instability | Supports vigorous vertical convection |
| Low vertical wind shear | Lets the vertical structure remain organised — maintains vertical organisation |
| A pre-existing disturbance — an initial disturbance | The initial low-pressure or convergence centre — the seed |
| Sufficient distance from the Equator | Lets the Coriolis force become effective |
- ⚠ The Coriolis force is too weak near the Equator for tropical cyclones to form exactly there
- Favourable conditions (2026) - extra detail:
- SST above 26.5 °C (some notes: above 27 degC) over a large sea surface
- Low vertical wind shear (small variation in vertical wind speed); enough Coriolis force; pre-existing weak low / low-level cyclonic circulation
## 3. Limits of occurrence
- Belt: Between 30 deg N and 30 deg S (APPSC 2022 key); roughly 5-30° N and S, western parts of tropical oceans
- Do not form:
- Within about 5 deg of the equator (Coriolis negligible)
- South Atlantic and south-east Pacific (cold waters, strong shear)
### Cyclone and anticyclone structure — parts → feature; rotation and steering
## 1. Structure
- Centre: The region of lowest pressure
- Eye: The central region of comparatively light winds and fair weather; higher temperature
- Eyewall: The most intense zone — strong winds, heavy rainfall and thunderstorms
- Spiral rainbands: Curved cloud bands that produce rain and wind
- Rotation: Anticlockwise in the Northern Hemisphere; clockwise in the Southern Hemisphere
| Part | Feature |
| Eye | Calm, clear, sinking air; lowest pressure (20-50 km across) [GK] |
| Eyewall | Ring of tallest, towering cumulonimbus clouds around the eye - strongest winds and heaviest rain[GK] |
| Rain bands | Spiral bands of cloud and rain outside the eyewall [GK] |
- ⚠ Eye = calm, lowest pressure; eyewall = strongest winds
## 2. Hurricane
- A regional name for a tropical cyclone — North Atlantic cyclones are called hurricanes
- A large, rotating tropical storm system forming over warm ocean waters — sustained winds of at least 119 km/h (74 mph), the minimum for a hurricane, typhoon or cyclone
- A low-pressure centre — the eye; spiral rain bands; produces strong winds, heavy rainfall, storm surge
- Rotation: Counter-clockwise in the Northern Hemisphere; clockwise in the Southern Hemisphere
- Measured by the Saffir–Simpson Wind Scale
### Cyclones vs anticyclones — rotation, steering, weather
## 1. Comparison
| Basis | Cyclone | Anticyclone |
| Centre | Low-pressure (low at centre; Coriolis deflection - Nov 2025) | High-pressure |
| Air movement | Inward; air rises | Outward; air descends (outward, sinking (diverging) winds) |
| Weather | Cloudier and stormier | Generally clearer |
| Northern Hemisphere rotation | Anticlockwise (anticlockwise in the northern hemisphere; anticlockwise in the NH) | Clockwise (clockwise in the NH) |
| Southern Hemisphere rotation | Clockwise | Anticlockwise |
- Steering: Mid/upper-troposphere winds
- Trade winds push them west onto eastern coasts
- Westerlies recurve mature storms north-east
## 2. Anticyclone weather
- Weather: Clear, dry, calm (settled) weather
- Heat dome: Stalled warm high traps hot air (27 May 2026)

### Tornadoes and waterspouts
## 1. Nature
- A violently rotating column of air extending from a thunderstorm to the ground — very strong rotating winds; a localised, short-duration atmospheric hazard
- Wind speeds: 105 to 322 km/h — can exceed 300 km/h in severe cases
- A funnel-shaped cloud — narrow or wide
- Lasts a few minutes — rarely more than 15–20 minutes; can remain stationary or travel up to about 90–100 km/h
- Measured on the Fujita Scale / Enhanced Fujita Scale — 6 categories
## 2. Tornadoes in the tornado-prone USA
- The Gulf of Mexico supplies warm, moisture-rich air
- Cold, drier continental air and atmospheric instability contribute to tornado formation
- Strong wind shear produces severe thunderstorms or supercells, which create tornado potential
## 3. Land vs ocean
- Tornadoes = land-based; hurricanes, typhoons, cyclones = ocean-born
| Feature | Tornado | Hurricane |
| Size and life | Small, minutes-long funnel from a thunderstorm | Hundreds of km wide, lasts days |
| Where | Mostly over land (USA) | Forms over warm ocean |
- Waterspout: Tornado-like funnel over water (the tornado's counterpart over water) → seen over the Brahmaputra in Barpeta, Assam (26 Aug 2026)
### Temperate (extra-tropical) cyclones and Western Disturbances
## 1. Temperate or extratropical cyclones
- Occur mainly in the mid-latitudes — approximately 35°–65° in both hemispheres
- Main wind influence: Westerlies — generally move from west to east
- Key mechanism: Interaction of contrasting air masses; associated with fronts
- Indian example: The Western Disturbance
- Origin: Mid and high latitudes along the polar front
- Fronts: Have a clear frontal system (warm and cold fronts)
- ⚠ Trap: "They do not have a clear frontal system" = the incorrect statement (APPSC 2024; APPSC-G1 2024)
- Area: Cover a larger area than tropical cyclones
- Origin surface: Can originate over land and sea (PYQ 2024); tropical ones only over the sea
- Weather: Winds weaker, but rain spread over a wide area and longer period
## 2. Western Disturbances
- An extratropical or cyclonic disturbance — originates in the Mediterranean region
- Occur mainly in winter; main region affected: northwestern India
- Bring winter rainfall and snowfall to mountains — agriculturally important for Rabi crops
- Rainfall decreases from west to east across the northwestern plain
- Reach NW India in winter at the occluded stage (UPSC Geo p.43; 28 Aug 2026)
### Temperate vs tropical cyclones — comparison
| Feature | Tropical | Temperate |
| Latitude | 5-30° | 35-65° (mid and high latitudes) [GK] |
| Origin | Only over warm seas | Land and sea |
| Fronts | None | Clear frontal system (warm, cold, occluded) |
| Size | Smaller, very intense | Larger area, gentler winds |
| Movement | East to west (trade winds) | West to east (westerlies) |
### Hailstorm factors; thunderstorms and local storms → season and region; lightning alerts
## 1. Hailstorms — factors
- Atmospheric: Strong convective instability; abundant moisture; strong updrafts
- Synoptic: Western Disturbances; wind discontinuity lines
- Terrain: Plateaus and hilly regions enhance convection
- Climate change: Increasing frequency and intensity of extreme convective events
## 2. Thunderstorms and local storms

### North Indian Ocean cyclones — India's exposure, Bay of Bengal vs Arabian Sea, season

# MCQ NOTES OF APP TOPIC 4:19
### Tropical cyclone formation
## Conditions
- Sea-surface temperature above about 26.5–27°C over a large area — most essential (APPSC-GS 2025: form over warm oceans)
- Need low vertical wind shear; moisture favourable
- Belt of origin: about 5°–30° N and S, especially western parts of tropical oceans
- Not at the Equator — Coriolis force too weak within about 5°
- Absent in South Atlantic and south-east Pacific — cool waters, strong wind shear
- Not over rivers or lakes; cold currents suppress them
## Structure and behaviour
- Rotation: anticlockwise (Northern Hemisphere), clockwise (Southern) — Coriolis
- Lowest pressure — centre (eye); strongest winds — eyewall; spiral rainbands bring rain and wind
- In a cyclone, air moves inward and rises
- Weakens after landfall — cut off from warm, moist oceanic energy
- Cloudburst — about 100 mm per hour over 20–30 sq km; Himalaya during June–August
### Tropical vs temperate
- Tropical cyclones pushed westward by trade winds; mature ones may recurve north-east under westerlies
- Tropical cyclones — smaller and more intense; temperate — larger, gentler winds
- Temperate cyclones — clear frontal system (warm and cold fronts; occluded front may develop)
- Temperate cyclones move west to east with the westerlies
- East coast of India (Odisha, AP, West Bengal, Tamil Nadu) — more cyclone-prone

### Anticyclones, tornadoes, Western Disturbances
## Cyclone structure
- Eyewall — ring of towering cumulonimbus around calm eye; strongest winds, heaviest rain
## Tornadoes and thunderstorms
- Tornado — small, short-lived funnel tied to a thunderstorm; winds 105–322 km/h, moves up to about 90–100 km/h
- Tornadoes spin from severe rotating convection — supercells
- Ordinary thunderstorm — short-lived single-cell cumulonimbus; rotating supercells give tornadoes
- Waterspout — tornado-like funnel over water; seen over Brahmaputra, Barpeta, Assam (26 August 2026)
- Thunder — air bursting outward after lightning heats it
- Damini App — lightning alerts in 15 languages; 104 detection locations
- Kaal Baisakhi / Nor'westers — Bihar, Odisha, West Bengal, Assam; approach from north-west
- Cloudburst — very local storm (like thunderstorms, tornadoes, squalls); cyclones and anticyclones large-scale
## Anticyclones
- High-pressure centre, sinking air flowing outward; clear, dry, calm, settled weather
- Northern Hemisphere anticyclone rotates clockwise
- Fronts belong to temperate cyclones only
## Western Disturbances

- Western Disturbances and wind discontinuity lines — synoptic causes of hailstorms; plateaus and hills boost convection
