### Floods — nature, extent and types
## 1. Nature
- A flood is the overflow of water onto normally dry land — water in rivers, streams and other water bodies that cannot be contained
- Causes: Excess rainfall; river overflow; other causes
- A rapid-onset disaster — the most frequent natural disaster in India
- Aggravated by poor drainage, urbanization and poor planning (urban flooding); prolonged rainfall can cause heavy landslides, which can block rivers and cause downstream havoc
- Flood (Prep): Water exceeds channel capacity and spills onto normally dry land; slow-onset, region- and season-predictable
- ⚠ Sources differ: other notes say floods are slow-onset (ROCKET: rapid-onset)
## 2. Extent in India
- Flood-affected areas: About 10% of India
- Flood-prone area: About 40 million hectares (Rashtriya Barh Ayog estimate); recent estimates up to about 49 million hectares
- Worst-affected states: Assam and Bihar; next: Uttar Pradesh and West Bengal
- Annual losses: Damage worth thousands of crores; significant loss of life and of crops
## 3. Types of flood
| Type | Cause | Commonly affected |
| Riverine / fluvial | Overflow of rivers due to heavy monsoon rainfall | The Ganga, Brahmaputra, Kosi and Godavari basins |
| Flash floods | Sudden intense rainfall or cloudbursts | Himalayan regions; urban areas |
| Urban floods | Poor drainage, heavy rain, encroachment on wetlands | Mumbai, Chennai, Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Delhi |
| Coastal floods | Storm surges, cyclones, high tides | The east and west coasts |
| Glacial Lake Outburst Floods (GLOFs) | Bursting of glacial lakes | Himalayan states — Uttarakhand, Sikkim |
- Types (Prep): Riverine, flash, coastal, urban (UPSC)
### Floods — causes, dam failure, management and committees
## 1. Causes of flooding
| Cause | Effect |
| Heavy rainfall | Discharge rises rapidly |
| Cloudburst | Very intense rainfall over a small area |
| Snowmelt | A sudden increase in river flow |
| Siltation | Reduces channel depth or capacity |
| Embankment failure | Water escapes the channel |
| Deforestation | Increases runoff |
| Floodplain encroachment | Increases human exposure |
| Poor drainage | Water accumulates |
| Dam failure | Sudden downstream discharge |
- Causes (APPSC 2025 key): heavy/prolonged rainfall, poor drainage, deforestation, dam failure
- Others: storm surges/cyclonic surge, low infiltration, siltation from soil erosion (UPSC)
## 2. Causes of dam failure
- Structural: A weak foundation; foundation instability; material failure; design failure
- Natural: An extreme flood exceeding design capacity; landslides; earthquakes; extreme rainfall
- Overtopping: Water exceeds the dam crest
- Sedimentation: Reduces dam storage capacity

### Flash floods and cloudbursts — nature and definition
## 1. Flash floods
- Very rapid onset; very short warning time
- Triggers: Intense rainfall or a cloudburst; sudden water release; steep terrain
- Vulnerable: Mountains; steep catchments; urban areas — highly vulnerable
- Associated with landslides, erosion and infrastructure damage
## 2. Cloudburst — definition
- IMD: Extremely intense rainfall over about 20–30 square kilometres — at least 100 mm (10 cm) in one hour
- A highly localised, short-duration extreme weather event — often with thunderstorms; conceptually a rapid-onset disaster
- Triggers flash floods and landslides — the major secondary hazards
- Mostly at altitudes of 1,000 to 2,500 metres in mountainous regions
- HKH "monsoon paradox": Seasonal rain declining/erratic while short, intense cloudbursts rise
## 3. Cloudburst vs ordinary heavy rainfall
| Basis | Cloudburst | Ordinary heavy rainfall |
| Intensity | Extremely intense | Can have varying intensity |
| Timing | Sudden | Can be prolonged |
| Flood potential | Strong flash-flood potential | Depends on basin conditions |

### Droughts — nature, types and triggers
## 1. Nature
- Drought: A serious shortfall in the availability of water — a prolonged deficiency of rainfall or moisture leading to water scarcity
- A slow-onset natural disaster — also called a creeping emergency or creeping hazard
- Mainly affects: Agriculture, drinking-water supply and industry
## 2. Types of drought
| Type | Indicator | Time lag |
| Meteorological | Rainfall deficiency of at least 25% from the long-period average; Prep: rainfall well below normal over a period | Immediate |
| Agricultural | Soil moisture insufficient for crop growth; Prep: soil-moisture deficit causing crop failure | Intermediate |
| Hydrological | Depletion of reservoirs, rivers, aquifers and other surface-water and groundwater sources; Prep: after prolonged deficiency (streams too) | Delayed — weeks to months |
| Socio-economic | Demand for water exceeds supply — causes social and economic stress; Prep: water/food shortage hitting livelihoods and markets | Long-term |
| Ecological | Ecosystem productivity collapses; environmental distress | - |
## 3. Causes and triggers
- Climatic triggers: irregular or delayed monsoon/erratic rainfall, monsoon failure, monsoon breaks, high temperature
- El Nino: 6 of 10 El Nino years depressed Indian rain
- Heat: High heat + evaporation speed up soil-moisture loss
- Normal SW-monsoon rainfall: 868.6 mm (1971-2020 base)
### Droughts — consequences and drought-prone regions
## 1. Long-term consequences
- Effects: groundwater depletion/falling water tables, land degradation and desertification, rural (distress) migration, rural indebtedness, lower farm output, ecosystem damage
## 2. Drought-prone regions
- Drought-prone regions: Western Rajasthan, Kutch-Saurashtra, Marathwada-Vidarbha, interior Karnataka, Rayalaseema (AP)
- Rayalaseema (AP): Highly drought-prone
- Monsoon failure (some notes stress the North-East monsoon), crop loss, distress migration
- Deep borewells → severe groundwater depletion
### Tsunamis — nature, causes and wave behaviour
## 1. Nature and causes
- Large waves generated by sudden movements of the ocean floor — displacing a large volume of water; a natural disaster; the term is Japanese in origin
- Meaning: Japanese "tsu" = harbour + "nami" = wave ("harbour wave")
- Most commonly associated with earthquakes — an earthquake beneath the sea
- Mainly undersea earthquakes (tectonic, chiefly at subduction zones) (UPSC)
- Other triggers: Submarine and terrestrial impacts, landslides, volcanic eruptions, explosions, bolide impacts
- Prep adds: submarine landslides, asteroid/meteorite impacts
- Can occur at any time of day or night; slows down as it approaches the shore
- Sequence: An undersea disturbance → sudden displacement of the ocean floor → displaces a large volume of water → generates tsunami waves → waves reach coastal areas → inundation and destruction of life and property
- Definition (Prep): Series of waves from sudden vertical displacement of the ocean floor
- ⚠ Trap: Not a "tidal wave" (tides come from gravitational pull)
## 2. Wave behaviour
| Deep ocean | Near the coast |
| Very long wavelength; height under 1 m; speed ~700-800 km/h (up to 800 km/h); low height, barely noticed [GK] | Wave slows in shallow water, height rises sharply (shoaling); massive inundation |
- Warning signs: Sudden receding of sea water, rapid sea-level rise, earthquake shaking
### Tsunamis — risk zones and the 2004 event
## 1. 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami
- December 2004 — severe damage in Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu, Pondicherry, Kerala and the Andaman and Nicobar Islands
- Date and trigger (Prep): 26 Dec 2004; Sumatra-Andaman earthquake, Mw ~9.1
- Worst-hit state: Tamil Nadu (Nagapattinam worst district/coast) (APPSC 2025 key)
## 2. Source ocean and risk zones

### Storm surges, coastal flooding, cyclone-prone coasts and coastline data
## 1. Coastal flooding
- A cyclone produces a storm surge; a high tide produces a higher baseline water level
- Heavy coastal rainfall causes drainage congestion
- Sea-level rise increases coastal exposure; a low-lying delta experiences greater inundation
- Salinity intrusion: Agricultural and water impacts
- Storm surge: Abnormal sea-level rise pushed by cyclone winds + low pressure; with sea-level rise → coastal flooding, permanent inundation of low-lying land
## 2. Cyclone-prone areas
- Coverage: Cyclones affect 13 coastal states/UTs and 84 coastal districts
- East vs west: East coast gets far more cyclones than the west
- Mangroves: With coastal shelter belts, anchor sediment + buffer storm surges; with dune stabilisation and seawalls → coastal defence

### Landslides — nature, extent and prone regions
## 1. Nature
- Landslide: The mass movement of rocks, debris or earth down mountain slopes or riverbanks — a rapid downward movement of rock, soil, debris or vegetation, influenced by gravity
- May occur gradually or suddenly without warning; common terrain: hilly regions (Prep: mostly in hilly terrain, APPSC 2025 key)
- Both a geological hazard and a hydro-meteorological hazard
- Associated with earthquakes, floods and volcanic eruptions
- Prolonged rainfall: A major rainfall trigger; heavy rainfall is the main trigger for landslides in India
## 2. Extent and institutions
- Landslide-prone: About 12.6% of India's land area — about 0.42 million square kilometres
- Most vulnerable regions: The Himalayas, the Northeast and the Western Ghats
- Geological Survey of India (GSI): Associated with landslide forecasting; conducts the National Landslide Susceptibility Mapping programme; Prep: GSI = nodal agency for landslide studies
- National Landslide Forecasting Centre: A key landslide initiative
- NDMA — Guidelines on Management of Landslides and Snow Avalanches: Issued 2009
- National Landslide Risk Management Strategy: Issued 2019
- Recent major event: Wayanad, Kerala — 2024
## 3. Landslide-prone regions
| Region | Areas | Triggers |
| Northwest Himalayas | Jammu and Kashmir, Himachal Pradesh, Uttarakhand | Earthquakes, heavy rainfall, road cutting |
| Northeast Himalayas | Sikkim, Arunachal Pradesh, Manipur, Mizoram, Nagaland, Meghalaya | Heavy rainfall, Jhum cultivation |
| Western Ghats and Konkan region | Kerala, Karnataka, Maharashtra, Goa, Tamil Nadu's Nilgiris | Intense monsoon rainfall, deforestation |
| Eastern Ghats | The Araku region | Localised heavy rainfall |
- Why more frequent (Prep):
- Unregulated construction on unstable slopes (HP flash floods 2023)
- Deforestation + road widening (Char Dham: 43 ha diverted, ~7,000 deodars approved for felling, 23 Jan 2026)
- Cloudbursts and deglaciation
### Avalanches — nature, institutions and types
## 1. Nature
- Avalanche: A rapid flow of snow, ice and debris down a mountain slope — a geological and snow-related natural hazard
- Occur in India mainly in the Himalayan region — in high-altitude areas
## 2. Institutions
- Nodal ministry: Ministry of Defence
- Key technical agency: Defence Geoinformatics Research Establishment (DGRE) — earlier known as the Snow and Avalanche Study Establishment (SASE); functions under the Defence Research and Development Organisation (DRDO)
- Conducts avalanche forecasting and research; undertakes hazard mapping; develops mitigation technologies
- Issues daily avalanche warning bulletins during the winter season
- Border Roads Organisation (BRO): Constructs and maintains roads and installs protection structures in avalanche-prone areas
- NDMA guidelines: Risk zonation, early warning, structural and non-structural mitigation measures
## 3. Types
| Type | Description |
| Slab avalanche | A large cohesive slab of snow breaks off and slides — ⚠ the most dangerous type |
| Loose snow avalanche — sluff | Small slides of dry, powdery snow |
| Ice avalanche | Ice masses from glaciers |
| Rock / debris avalanche | A mixture of rock, soil and snow |
### Avalanches — causes, prone areas and measures
## 1. Causes
- Natural: Heavy snowfall; weak snow layers caused by a temperature gradient; steep slopes of 30°–45°; earthquakes and seismic activity; wind loading; a rapid temperature rise; rain falling on snow
- Human / other: Skiing; mountaineering; road construction; artificial triggering using explosives; deforestation; climate change effects
## 2. Avalanche-prone areas of India
| State / UT | Areas |
| Jammu and Kashmir | The most affected region |
| Ladakh | High-altitude zones |
| Himachal Pradesh | Lahaul-Spiti, Kinnaur, Rohtang Pass, Chamba |
| Uttarakhand | Higher reaches of the Garhwal and Kumaon Himalaya |
| Sikkim, Arunachal Pradesh | High-altitude Himalayan stretches |
- Prep: Avalanches in snow-bound Himalaya (J&K, Ladakh, HP, Uttarakhand); landslides in Himalaya (J&K, HP, Uttarakhand, NE) + Western Ghats (Kerala, Konkan, Nilgiris)
## 3. Management measures
- Structural: Snow bridges; snow nets; retaining walls; galleries
- Non-structural: Hazard zonation mapping; restricted movement; training; awareness
