### Monetary policy — authority, objectives and framework phases
## 1. Monetary policy basics
- Authority: Formulated and implemented by the RBI under the RBI Act, 1934 (APPSC-GS 2023)
- Objectives: Price stability (paramount), growth, financial stability, external balance, development goals
## 2. Phases and key changes
| Phase | Framework | Landmarks |
| 1985-1998 | Monetary targeting (M3) with feedback | Chakravarty Committee 1985; Vaghul Committee 1987 (money market) |
| 1998-2016 | Multiple indicator approach (growth, inflation, exchange rate, credit) | LAF 2000; MSS 2004; Base Rate 2010; MSF 2011; MCLR 2016 |
| 2016 onwards | Flexible inflation targeting | Urjit Patel Committee 2014; MPFA 2015; RBI Act amended 2016; SDF 2022 |
- Key post-1991 change: Deregulation of interest rates (APPSC-GS 2023)
- Multiple indicator approach dropped: Opaque → markets could not predict RBI's moves when indicators conflicted
## 3. Persons
- Seven Ages: Governor Shaktikanta Das traced the phases in "Seven Ages of India's Monetary Policy", 24 Jan 2020, St. Stephen's College (APPSC-GS 2024 statement)
### Instruments — quantitative vs qualitative, main instrument
| Basis | Quantitative (general) | Qualitative (selective) |
| Aim | Volume and cost of total credit | Direction of credit to sectors or borrowers |
| Other name | Traditional methods of control | Selective credit control |
| Tools | Bank rate, repo, reverse repo, OMO, CRR, SLR (variable reserve ratio) | Margin requirements, moral suasion, credit rationing, direct action, selective credit control, consumer credit regulation |
- Main instrument: The policy repo rate
- Single operating policy rate under FIT + most-watched signal
- MPC sets it to hit the inflation target; liquidity kept so money-market rates stay near it
- OMOs: Main tool for durable liquidity (e.g. ₹1 lakh crore of purchases in Dec 2025)
- Margin requirement: Gap between the value of the security and the loan
- Higher margin = smaller loan against the same security → cuts credit against it
- ⚠ Moral suasion, margin requirements, direct action = qualitative; CRR, SLR, OMO = quantitative
### Terms → meaning: policy rates, LAF corridor, stance — Policy rates
## 1. Instruments at a glance
| Instrument | Meaning | Liquidity effect when raised | Rate now |
| Bank Rate | The RBI's lending or discount rate — a traditional rate associated with RBI lending or rediscounting; involves bills of exchange or commercial paper; rediscount rate / longer-term lending, rediscounting of bills with no collateral; aligned with MSF; penal rate for CRR/SLR shortfalls (penal rates linked to it) | Tightens | 5.50% |
| Repo Rate (policy repo rate) | Lending by the RBI to banks against securities — the rate at which the RBI lends to banks; overnight, against approved collateral (G-secs); the policy rate | Tightens | 5.25% (MPC, 5 Aug 2026) |
| Reverse Repo (fixed-rate reverse repo) | Enables the RBI to absorb liquidity from banks — the rate at which the RBI borrows from banks; against G-secs; used at RBI's discretion since SDF | Absorbs more | 3.35% (May 2026) |
| CRR — Cash Reserve Ratio | A cash reserve maintained with the RBI (share of NDTL kept as cash with RBI; earns no interest) | Reduces lendable funds | 3% of NDTL |
| SLR — Statutory Liquidity Ratio | Liquid assets maintained by banks themselves (share of NDTL in cash, gold, approved G-secs) | Reduces lendable funds | 18% |
| OMO | The RBI buying or selling government securities (outright, done through banks and not directly with the public) | Sale absorbs; purchase injects | - |
| MSF (rate) | An emergency overnight borrowing facility — penal overnight borrowing by dipping into SLR portfolio up to 2% of NDTL; ceiling of the corridor, repo + 25 bps | Tightens | 5.50% |
| SDF (rate) | Banks park surplus / overnight deposits with RBI without collateral; floor of the LAF corridor | Absorbs more | 5.00% |
- Monetary policy instruments: Repo Rate, Reverse Repo Rate, Bank Rate, Cash Reserve Ratio, Statutory Liquidity Ratio — ⚠ fiscal deficit is associated with fiscal policy
- Rate sources: Rates as of 6 Aug 2026 (MPC unanimous; neutral stance); CRR/SLR/reverse repo as of May 2026
- Repo cuts: 125 bps since Feb 2025 (6.50% → 5.25%)
### Repo vs reverse repo, bank rate and MSF
## 1. Repo vs reverse repo
| Basis | Repo Rate | Reverse Repo Rate |
| Use | Liquidity injection; regulate money supply | Liquidity absorption — absorb surplus liquidity |
| Mechanism | A bank borrows from the RBI | A bank parks money with the RBI |
## 2. Repo vs Bank Rate
| Basis | Repo Rate | Bank Rate |
| Nature | Short-term lending by the RBI | A traditional rate |
| Against | Eligible securities | Bills of exchange or commercial paper |
| Use | Liquidity management | Monetary policy |
| Associated with | Repo transactions | Bank lending or rediscounting |
## 3. MSF vs repo
| Basis | Marginal Standing Facility (MSF) | Repo |
| Nature | An overnight borrowing facility | A short-term borrowing mechanism |
| Borrower | Banks borrow from the RBI | Banks borrow from the RBI |
| Use | Marginal or short-term liquidity needs | Regular liquidity management |
| Collateral | Can involve dipping into the Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR) portfolio | Against eligible securities |
| Cost | ⚠ Traditionally a higher cost than the repo rate | Principal policy lending rate in the source framework |
### Terms → meaning: policy rates, LAF corridor, stance — Bank rate, MSF corridor and stance
## 1. Bank rate and stance
- Lower bank rate: Borrowing cheaper; expands liquidity — an expansionary stance
- Higher bank rate: Borrowing costlier; contracts liquidity — a tight stance
- Central bank policy rates directly regulate the capacity for credit creation
## 2. MSF and the corridor
- MSF design:
- Began May 2011 at repo + 100 bps, borrowing up to 1% of NDTL (older notes)
- Now 25 bps above repo (5.25% vs 5.50%)
- Exam angle: order of the corridor - SDF (floor) < repo < MSF = bank rate (ceiling).
- ⚠ MSF borrowing limit: 1% of NDTL (2011, older notes) vs 2% now; spread 100 bps then vs 25 bps now
## 3. Policy stance terms
| Stance | Meaning |
| Accommodative | Supports growth, ample liquidity, rate cuts likely |
| Neutral | Rates can move either way, data-dependent |
| Hawkish | Worried about inflation; tightens (raises rates, drains liquidity) |
| Dovish | Worried about growth; bias towards easing [GK] |
- Stance path: Neutral → accommodative (Apr 2025) → neutral (June 2025) → neutral retained through Aug 2026
### CRR vs SLR — features and legal basis
## 1. SLR in detail
- The minimum percentage of Net Demand and Time Liabilities (NDTL) that commercial banks must maintain in liquid assets before lending
- Eligible assets: Liquid cash, gold and other unencumbered approved securities
- Government securities are commonly used — most commonly held in Government Securities (G-Secs), which earn interest income while meeting SLR requirements
## 2. CRR vs SLR
| Basis | CRR | SLR |
| Kept | Deposited with the RBI — an RBI-held reserve | Maintained by the bank itself — not deposited with the RBI; the bank's own holdings |
| Form | Only cash (Cash balance with RBI) | Cash, gold and approved securities or other liquid assets (unencumbered approved securities held by the bank itself) |
| Returns | Do not earn interest | Can earn returns (earns interest on G-secs) |
| Role | Controls liquidity and credit — regulates credit and liquidity | Controls liquidity; a liquidity buffer — regulates credit and liquidity |
| Law | Sec 42, RBI Act[GK] | Sec 24, Banking Regulation Act, 1949[GK] |
| Limits | No floor or ceiling since 2006 [GK] | Ceiling 40%; RBI range 0-40% |
| Current level | 3% of NDTL (100 bps cut released about ₹2.5 lakh crore by Dec 2025, ES 2025-26) | 18% (UPSC notes; your current lane has no newer value) |
- Trap: "Required reserve ratio" = CRR (APPSC 2023)
- ⚠ Sec 42 RBI Act = CRR; Sec 24 BR Act = SLR
### SLR — holding, statutory range, trend and penalty
- SLR form of holding: Held by banks themselves (not deposited with RBI) as cash, gold, unencumbered G-secs/T-bills/SDLs and approved securities (UPSC)
- MSS securities count towards SLR
- SLR statutory range: 0% to 40% of NDTL
- Earlier 25% floor removed by Banking Regulation (Amendment) Act, 2007
- SLR trend: 38.5% of NDTL in 1991-92 → cut gradually on the Narasimham Committee's advice → 18% now
- SLR shortfall penalty: Bank rate + 3% a year → bank rate + 5% if shortfall continues
### Tool → effect: money supply, demand, transmission channels, bond yields — Dear money vs cheap money
## 1. Dear money vs cheap money
| Tool | To control inflation (dear money, contractionary) | To boost growth (cheap money, expansionary) |
| CRR | Raise | Cut |
| SLR | Raise | Cut |
| Repo, reverse repo, bank rate | Raise | Cut |
- Repo transmission chain: Repo up → money-market rates up → costlier credit → lower consumption and investment → lower aggregate demand → inflation cools
- CRR/SLR cut: Raises lendable funds and the money multiplier
- M3/M0 = 6.21 on 31 Dec 2025; 6.0 adjusted for SDF
- Raising CRR: Less money in circulation + lower demand (APPSC-G1 2022: statements I and II)
- ⚠ Trap: Does NOT raise purchasing power or banks' lending capacity
### Tool → effect: money supply, demand, transmission channels, bond yields — Transmission channels
## 1. Transmission channels
| Channel | How a rate cut works |
| Interest rate | Cheaper loans raise consumption and investment |
| Credit | Banks have more funds and lend more |
| Exchange rate | Lower rates can weaken the rupee, helping exports [GK] |
| Asset price | Bond and equity prices rise, creating a wealth effect [GK] |
| Expectations | A credible target anchors inflation expectations |
- Effect of rate cut: Increases investment expenditure (UPSC 2014)
- ⚠ Trap: Does not raise savings or tax collection
- Mechanism: Repo change → money-market rates → aggregate demand → inflation
## 2. What moves G-sec yields
- Drivers of G-sec yields: US Federal Reserve's actions + RBI's actions + inflation and short-term rates → all three (UPSC 2021)
- OMO purchases: ₹1 lakh crore (Dec 2025) meant to stabilise bond yields
- Sovereign spread: Spread of Indian yields over US bonds narrowed → lower borrowing costs
### Liquidity management and sterilisation — LAF, SDF, VRR/VRRR, I-CRR, MSS — Short-term windows
## 1. Short-term windows
| Tool | Meaning / feature |
| LAF | Repo + reverse repo/SDF windows → main short-term liquidity tool; primary window for injecting and absorbing day-to-day liquidity (UPSC) |
| SDF | Proposed by RBI in 2015; enabled by Budget 2018-19 (RBI Act amendment); began April 2022; absorbs surplus liquidity without collateral → RBI need not hold G-secs to drain funds (APPSC-G2 2025) |
| VRR / VRRR | Auction-based (competitive) LAF tools; last accepted rate = cut-off; VRR injects liquidity; VRRR absorbs it for longer maturities than the overnight SDF → keeps short rates near repo |
| Fine-tuning operations | Short VRR/VRRR auctions to keep liquidity in line with the monetary policy stance; 49 in FY2024 (FY24) (APPSC-G2 2025) |
### Liquidity management and sterilisation — LAF, SDF, VRR/VRRR, I-CRR, MSS — Durable liquidity and reserve tools
## 1. Durable liquidity and reserve tools
- I-CRR: Temporary 10% incremental CRR announced 10 Aug 2023 → impounded about ₹1.1 lakh crore (APPSC-G2 2025: both statements correct)
- OMO: Outright purchase/sale of G-secs for durable liquidity
- FY26 injections: ₹2.39 lakh crore OMO purchases (Apr-May 2025)
- LTRO (2020): 1-3 year funds at the repo rate
- USD/INR buy-sell swap: e.g. $5 bn, 3-year, Dec 2025 → injects rupee liquidity without changing the size of RBI's balance sheet
## 2. Sterilisation
- Sterilisation: Forex inflows swell rupee liquidity → RBI sells G-secs (OMO/MSS) or runs VRRRs to offset
- MSS (April 2004): For sterilisation; sells securities to mop up surplus liquidity
- RBI issues T-bills/securities (MSBs) to absorb rupees created by forex purchases
- Proceeds sit in a separate MSS account the Government cannot spend
### Liquidity management and sterilisation — LAF, SDF, VRR/VRRR, I-CRR, MSS — Liquidity conditions - surplus vs deficit
## 1. Liquidity conditions - surplus vs deficit
- Surplus vs deficit: Surplus liquidity speeds transmission + pulls market rates below repo vs deficit liquidity weakens it
- Dec 2025 tightness: Currency leakage, tax outflows, reserve needs → RBI injected ₹1 lakh crore via OMO + $5 bn swap
- 2026 stance: RBI to keep "sufficient liquidity" for transmission (Governor, Feb 2026)
- By 3 Sep 2026: surplus about ₹10.32 lakh crore → had pushed the call rate below repo; RBI draining via VRRR
- 4 Sep 2026: RBI absorbed about ₹6.02 lakh crore through two 3-day VRRRs

## 1. Who sets which rate
| Fixed by RBI/MPC | Set by each bank | Market-determined |

### Lending rate benchmarks — who sets which rate, committees and directions — Benchmark evolution
## 1. Benchmark evolution
| Benchmark | From | Key point |
| PLR | 1994 [GK] | Rate for the best borrowers, set by each bank |
| Base rate | 1 July 2010 | Minimum lending rate, based on the average cost of funds → slow transmission |
| MCLR | 1 April 2016 | Based on the marginal cost of funds (marginal cost of deposits) + cost of CRR/SLR carry + operating cost + tenor premium; an internal benchmark |
| EBLR | 1 October 2019 | All new floating-rate personal/retail and MSME (MSE) loans are linked to an external benchmark: the repo rate, the 3-month or 6-month T-bill yield, or an FBIL-published rate; loans linked directly to repo → fastest route |
- Purpose of MCLR (UPSC 2016: both statements correct):
- Faster transmission of policy rates to borrowers
- MCLR in practice: HDFC Bank raised MCLR by up to 10 bps on 8 Jun 2026 → 1-year MCLR 8.40%

### Issues — challenges after 1991, monetary vs fiscal policy, obstacles to transmission — Monetary vs fiscal policy - complementary roles against inflation
## 1. Policy links
- A decrease in the Bank Rate increases liquidity; an increase is contractionary
- OMO: A monetary policy instrument; ⚠ public revenue is associated with fiscal policy
## 2. Monetary vs fiscal policy - complementary roles against inflation
| Basis | Fiscal policy | Monetary policy |
| Authority | Government (Finance Ministry, Parliament) | RBI / MPC |
| Tools | Spending, taxes, subsidies, borrowing | Repo, OMO, CRR/SLR, LAF |
| Effect on demand | Direct | Indirect, through interest rates |
| Flexibility | Rigid, slow; legislative lags (needs legislative approval) | Quick, fast (bi-monthly meetings) |
- Limit: Monetary tools are demand-side tools only
- Food inflation: Mostly supply-driven → rate hikes cannot add onions or tomatoes to the market
- Supply-driven food/fuel inflation needs fiscal steps too: buffer-stock releases, duty cuts and farm reform
### Issues — challenges after 1991, monetary vs fiscal policy, obstacles to transmission — Obstacles to transmission
## 1. Obstacles to transmission
| Obstacle | Effect |
| Administered small-savings rates | Hold deposit rates up → banks cannot cut lending rates |
| NPAs | Weak balance sheets (though GNPA now at a multi-decadal low of 2.2%, Sep 2025) |
| Dependence on deposits | Banks reprice only new deposits |
| Heavy Government borrowing | Keeps yields high (crowding out) |
| Informal credit | Large unorganised money market outside RBI's reach; people prefer cash |
| Structural rigidities | In banking (2026); liquidity mismatches |

### Policy rates (Aug 2026)
## Objective
- Price stability — main objective (RBI Act, 1934); growth, financial stability, external balance also pursued

## LAF corridor
- Corridor, floor to ceiling — SDF < Repo < MSF = Bank rate

## CRR and SLR
- CRR — cash balance with the RBI, earns no interest
- SLR — held by banks themselves in cash, gold, unencumbered G-secs and T-Bills (incl. SDLs) and approved securities

- Contractionary policy — raise CRR, SLR, repo; sell G-secs
- CRR/SLR cut → more lendable funds, higher money multiplier
## Transmission and markets
- MCLR — aimed at faster transmission; an internal benchmark computed by each bank
- Call money rate — market-determined; RBI fixes CRR and SLR (true, but not the explanation)
- Repo cut works via five channels — interest-rate, credit, exchange-rate, asset-price, expectations
- Informal credit markets — outside the full reach of RBI policy (a block to transmission)
- G-sec yields — moved by US Fed actions, RBI actions, domestic inflation and short-term rates
- Policy rates directly regulate capacity for credit creation
### CRR vs SLR
- ★ Repurchase option (Repo) — used by RBI to regulate money supply and inflation
- SLR cut from its 1991–92 level on advice of the Narasimham Committee
- CRR — Section 42, RBI Act, 1934; SLR — Section 24, Banking Regulation Act, 1949
- SLR — 38.5% (1991–92) → 18% (September 2023)
### Quantitative vs qualitative
- Quantitative tools — repo, reverse repo, OMO, CRR, SLR
- Qualitative / selective tools — margin requirements, moral suasion
- Margin = value of security − loan; raising the margin reduces the loan available against the same security

## Lending-rate benchmarks
- Base Rate — 1 July 2010; based on average cost of funds (slow transmission)
- MCLR — 1 April 2016; marginal cost of funds (marginal deposit cost, CRR/SLR carry, operating cost, tenor premium)
- RBI Internal Study Group on MCLR (2017) — chaired by Janak Raj; found weak transmission, recommended an external benchmark
- EBLR mandatory from 1 October 2019 — new floating-rate personal, retail and MSME loans linked to an external benchmark (repo, 3- or 6-month T-bill yield, or FBIL-published rate)
- EBLR-linked loans — fastest transmission; MCLR loans reset only periodically
- Who sets the rate — MSF rate by RBI/MPC; MCLR by each bank; EBLR by external benchmark; call rate by the market
- MSF rate is higher than repo (repo + 0.25%); overnight emergency window
### Repo transmission
- MPC sets repo to achieve the inflation target and guide money-market rates
- Repo hike sequence — higher money-market rates → costlier credit → lower consumption and investment → lower aggregate demand
- Surplus liquidity pulls market rates below repo, speeding transmission; deficit liquidity weakens it
- Expectations channel — a credible target anchors expectations; does not remove all uncertainty
### Liquidity tools
## Day-to-day liquidity
- LAF — RBI's primary window: injects via repo, absorbs via reverse repo and SDF
- VRRR — auction-based, absorbs liquidity for longer than overnight SDF; VRR injects
- Fine-tuning operations (short-term VRR/VRRR) — keep liquidity in line with the policy stance
- Hawkish — worried about inflation, tightens; dovish — worried about growth, easing bias
- Higher CRR → reduces money in circulation
## Durable liquidity and sterilisation
- OMO — outright purchase or sale of government securities; durable liquidity
- I-CRR — 10%, announced 10 August 2023; blocked about ₹1.1 lakh crore
- USD/INR buy-sell swap (e.g. 3-year, $5 billion, December 2025) — injects rupee liquidity without changing RBI balance-sheet size
- MSS (April 2004) — sterilises liquidity from forex purchases; T-Bills issued, proceeds in a separate account Government cannot spend
- Sterilisation — offsetting excess rupee liquidity from forex inflows; tools MSS, OMO, VRRR
### Obstacles & limits
- Supply-driven food/fuel inflation — buffer stocks, duty cuts, farm reforms (CRR, SLR, rates act on demand)
- Ad hoc Treasury Bills (automatic monetisation) ended 1997 → Ways and Means Advances
- Main post-1991 challenge — exchange-rate volatility from capital flows
- Fiscal dominance — large government borrowing pushing money supply and inflation
- Narrower spread between Indian and US bond yields → cuts India's borrowing costs

### Terms → meaning: policy rates, LAF corridor, stance — Policy rates
## Rates
- ★ Repo Rate — rate at which RBI lends short-term funds to commercial banks (against G-secs); under monetary policy
- ★ LAF — means by which banks borrow through repos (and lend via reverse repos)
- ★ Bank Rate — rate at which RBI discounts bills of exchange; RBI's long-term lending rate to banks
- ★ Bank Rate cut 7% → 6.50% in October 2001; MSF aligned to Bank Rate in February 2012
## Reserve ratios
- ★ CRR — ratio of deposits banks must keep with RBI; CRR hike → less money to lend
- ★ SLR — mechanism by which banks provide credit to the government (investing in G-secs)
- ★ SLR 19.25% (RBI review, 2019)
- ★ SLR cut of 50 bps → banks may cut lending rates
- ★ Since the reforms (Narasimham Committee) — both SLR and CRR reduced; SLR from 38.5% in 1991
## OMO and inflation control
- ★ OMO — purchase and sale of government securities by RBI (trading in securities)
- ★ Inflation control — raise Bank Rate, CRR, SLR; not purchase of government securities

### CRR vs SLR — features and legal basis

- ★ MSF rate and NDTL — terms of banking operations
- MSF — overnight emergency borrowing from RBI, dipping into SLR at a penal rate
