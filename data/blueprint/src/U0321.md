### Articles 245-255 → number game and territorial extent — Article 245 - territorial extent of laws
## 1. Article 245 - territorial extent of laws
- Parliament: Whole or any part of India; alone has extra-territorial legislation
- Art. 245(2): no Parliament law invalid for extra-territorial operation
- Not a limit: Extra-territorial operation of its laws (permitted by Art. 245(2))
- State legislature: Own state only; extra-territorial reach only with sufficient territorial nexus
- Exceptions (special areas):
- UTs A&N and Lakshadweep: President's regulations for Andaman & Nicobar and Lakshadweep have force of Parliament's Acts
- Governor can bar/modify Parliament's Acts for Scheduled Areas
- Governor of Assam for Assam tribal areas
- ⚠ Extra-territorial laws = Parliament only; State needs territorial nexus
### Seventh Schedule, Article 246 and residuary power — lists, entries, precedence
## 1. Framework - Article 246 scheme
- Governed by: Part XI (Articles 245 to 255) and the Seventh Schedule (Union List, State List, Concurrent List)
- Article 246: Operationalises the subject distribution
- 246(1): Parliament — exclusive competence over List I (Union List)
- 246(3): State Legislatures — exclusive competence over List II (State List), subject to (1) and (2)
- 246(2): Both — concurrent competence over List III (Concurrent List)
- Art. 246(4): Parliament may legislate on any matter for areas not in a state (UTs); for UTs Parliament covers all three Lists
- Article 246A: Special provision for GST — by the 101st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2016
- Parliament and State Legislatures get simultaneous power to frame GST laws — breaks the strict exclusive-list dichotomy of Article 246
- Only Parliament for inter-state supply (IGST)
- Article 254 — repugnancy: On a Concurrent subject, Central law prevails over conflicting State law
- Exception 254(2): A State law reserved for the President's assent and assented can prevail
- Source: Borrowed from Canada + a third Concurrent List (Government of India Act 1935 tradition)

## 3. Residuary legislative power - Article 248

### Parliament legislating on the State List — five special circumstances and limits — Special circumstances - overview
## 1. When Parliament can legislate on State List subjects
- Article 249 — national interest: Rajya Sabha resolution by at least two-thirds of members present and voting
- The law remains in force for one year; ceases six months after the resolution lapses
- Article 250 — National Emergency (Article 352): Law can extend to the whole or any part of India; ceases six months after the Emergency ends
- Article 252 — consent of States: Requests by at least two State Legislatures through resolutions
- Applies only to the consenting States; can be amended or repealed only by Parliament; other states may adopt it by resolution
- Article 253 — international agreements: Plenary power to implement international treaties, conventions and accords - for whole or any part of India
- Article 356 — President's Rule: Parliament exercises the legislative powers of the State Legislature (or President by delegation)
- President's Rule laws: stay in force after PR ends, until the state legislature repeals, alters or re-enacts them
## 2. Count
- Count: CDI counts these as five special circumstances
- APP's heading says "4" (lists four plus PR) but its table lists all five
- The five = Art. 249, 250, 252, 253 and 356
- Rajya Sabha resolution by 2/3 of members present and voting, valid up to 1 year, renewable; Art. 252 = request of two or more states; Art. 253 = treaties, agreements, conventions
- Exam angle: PYQ 2023 - during an Emergency, State List laws are made by Parliament; PYQ 2018 - international agreements = Art. 253.
### Parliament legislating on the State List — five special circumstances and limits — Limits on Parliament's legislative competence
## 1. Limits on Parliament's legislative competence
- State List: Cannot legislate except under Arts. 249, 250, 252, 253, 356
- Art. 301-303: Cannot prefer or discriminate between states in trade laws
- Parliament cannot discriminate between states except in scarcity
- Inter-state trade restrictions (state laws): Reasonable restrictions in public interest allowed, but bill needs President's previous sanction (Part XIII, Arts. 301-305)
### Articles 249-253 → conditions, duration and effect — Article 249 - resolution and duration
## 1. Article 249 - resolution and duration
- Majority: Supported by two-thirds of members present and voting (APPSC-GS 2025)
- Duration: In force not exceeding 1 year (up to one year)
- Renewable any number of times, 1 year at a time (APP MCQ answer: one year)
- Lapse: Laws made under it cease 6 months after the resolution lapses
## 2. Effect on state power - Articles 250 and 251
- State legislature: Power not suspended; can still legislate on the same matter
- Emergency: During a Proclamation of Emergency, Parliament (not any minister) can legislate on State List matters for all or part of India (APPSC-GS 2023)
- State's power not suspended, but Parliament's law prevails on conflict
- Art. 251: State law conflicting with a Parliamentary law made under Art. 249/250 → the Parliamentary (Union) law prevails
### Articles 249-253 → conditions, duration and effect — Article 252 - consent of states
## 1. Article 252 - consent of states
- Procedure: Legislatures of two or more states pass resolutions → Parliament legislates for those states
- Adoption: Any other state can adopt the law later by a resolution of its own
- Amendment/repeal: Only Parliament; the requesting states lose that power
## 2. Article 253 - international agreements
- Art. 253 (treaties): Parliament can make laws for the whole or any part of India to implement treaties, international agreements, conventions and decisions of international bodies, even on State List subjects (APPSC-GS 2018)
- No state consent is needed
- ⚠ 252 = needs consent of two or more states; 253 = no state consent needed
### Doctrines → meaning and cases (repugnancy, pith and substance, colourable legislation)
## 1. Constitutional doctrines
- Nature: Most doctrines are judicially evolved — not expressly defined or written in the Constitution
| Doctrine | Origin | Articles | Meaning |
| Basic Structure | Kesavananda Bharati (1973) | Article 368 (Part XX) | Parliament cannot destroy the basic features |
| Judicial Review | Marbury v. Madison (1803) — John Marshall (USA) | 13, 32, 136, 137, 226, 227 | The higher judiciary (SC and HCs) adjudicates the validity of legislative acts and executive actions |
| Severability | A.K. Gopalan (1950) and State of Bombay v. F.N. Balsara (1951) | 13(1) and 13(2) | Only the offending portion of a statute is void; the valid portion survives if it can be separated |
| Eclipse | Bhikaji Narain Dhakras v. State of M.P. (1955) | 13(1) | Pre-constitutional laws inconsistent with Fundamental Rights are not dead ab initio — they stay overshadowed (dormant) and revive if the Fundamental Right is amended |
| Pith and Substance | Prafulla Kumar v. Bank of Commerce (1947) and State of Rajasthan v. G. Chawla (1959) | Article 246 and the Seventh Schedule | Resolves overlaps between legislative fields by the law's true nature and essence; incidental encroachment on another List does not decide validity |
| Colorable Legislation | Kameshwar Singh v. State of Bihar (1952) | Article 246 | "What cannot be done directly cannot be done indirectly" — transgression of legislative competence under a permissible guise |
| Harmonious construction | - | - | Entries read together so both operate [GK] |
| Repugnancy (Art. 254) | - | - | Union law prevails in Concurrent field |
- ⚠ Sources differ: other notes say the pith-and-substance case is State of Bombay v. F.N. Balsara (1951) - Bombay Prohibition Act upheld (Pith and substance (Doctrine of Pith and Substance); APP MCQ: doctrine used for Union-State List encroachment)
- ⚠ Sources differ: other notes say the colourable-legislation case is K.C. Gajapati Narayan Deo v. State of Orissa (1953) ("Cannot do indirectly what cannot be done directly")
- ⚠ Balsara (1951) = pith and substance; Gajapati Narayan Deo (1953) = colourable legislation
## 2. Repugnancy - Article 254
- Art. 254(1): Central law vs state law on a Concurrent List subject → Central (Union) law prevails
- State law void to the extent of repugnancy ("doctrine of repugnancy")
- 254(2): State law reserved and assented by the President prevails in that state
- Governor may reserve a bill under 254(2) as an exception to aid-and-advice
- Proviso: Parliament can later override it with a fresh law (amend or repeal that state law) → states "practically powerless"
