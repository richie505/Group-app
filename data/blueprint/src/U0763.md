### Ecology — levels of organisation and the biosphere
## 1. Ecology
- Ecology: The study of relationships between organisms and the environment
- "Ecology" coined: Ernst Haeckel (1866)
## 2. Levels of organisation
- Ecological order: organism → population → community → ecosystem → biome → biosphere
- Full hierarchy: atoms → molecules → protoplasm → cells → tissues → organs → organisms → populations → communities → ecosystems → biomes → biosphere
- Community: all populations of different species in an area [GK]
- Biome: large region defined by climate and dominant vegetation - rainforest, tundra, desert (UPSC notes)
- Biosphere: All ecosystems together
- Absent at the poles, highest peaks and deepest oceans
- Energy from the sun; nutrients recycled within
### Habitat vs niche
## 1. Habitat vs niche
- Habitat: physical place where an organism lives - its "address" [GK]
- One habitat can hold many species
- All habitats are environments, not all environments are habitats
- Niche: functional role of a species in a habitat - its "profession/lifestyle"; the food, space, time, conditions it uses [GK]
- Set by habitat, food, reproduction and physical-chemical factors
- Unique to each species - no two species occupy the same niche (APPSC 2022: both statements correct)
| Fundamental niche | Realised niche |
| full range of conditions and resources a species could use free from other species (APPSC 2021) | actual range used under competition and other limits |
- Niche breadth: specialists (koala - only eucalyptus) vs generalists (most animals)
- ⚠ Habitat = address; niche = profession

### Ecosystem — concept, structure vs function, open vs closed
- Ecosystem: A functional unit of organisms and the environment — a functional unit of nature: biotic and abiotic components; an open system
- Status: basic functional unit of ecology (APPSC PYQ)
- Prep wording: a biotic community + its non-living surroundings, linked by energy flow and nutrient cycling
- Components: abiotic components, producers, consumers, decomposers
- Main processes: Energy flow and nutrient cycling; has its own productivity; no fixed universal boundary
- Term "ecosystem": Arthur Tansley (A.G. Tansley; British botanist), 1935
- "System resulting from the integration of all living and non-living factors of the environment"
| Structure | Function |
| biotic community + abiotic materials, species composition, trophic levels [GK] | energy flow (one-way, from sun), nutrient cycling (biogeochemical cycles), primary and secondary production, self-regulation (APP) |
- Flow vs cycle: nutrients cycle (biogeochemical cycles) while energy flows one way
- Open vs closed: ecosystems are open for energy and matter; the Earth is open for energy but nearly closed for matter
- Ecosystem components:
| Abiotic | Biotic |
| Energy, radiation, temperature, water, gases and wind, fire, gravity, topography, soil | Green plants, non-green plants, decomposers, parasites, symbionts, animals, man |
### Biogeochemical cycles — carbon, water, phosphorus
- Biogeochemical cycle: The movement of chemical elements — nutrient movement through biotic and abiotic components; another term is the nutritional cycle
- Also put as: the movement of nutrient elements through ecosystem components
- Biotic components: Living organisms; abiotic: the non-living environment
- Carbon cycle: CO₂ exchange through biological processes
- Takes up / removes CO₂: Photosynthesis
- Releases CO₂: Respiration, decay, fossil-fuel combustion
- Water cycle: Driven by solar energy
- Phosphorus cycle: Involves rock weathering

### Abiotic factors — types and limiting-factor laws

| Type | Factors |

### Decomposers and decomposition — steps and factors
- Main decomposers: Bacteria and fungi — they decompose organic matter; decomposer bacteria break down organic matter
- Microorganisms collectively recycle nutrients
- Who / result: dead matter broken into inorganic nutrients reused by producers; helpers = earthworms, nematodes, arthropods
- Detritivores: earthworm fragments dead matter
- Role: the link between biotic and abiotic parts
- Steps:
- fragmentation (detritivores like earthworms) → leaching (soluble nutrients into soil) → catabolism (enzymes of bacteria and fungi) → humification → mineralisation
| Factor | Effect on rate |
| Detritus rich in nitrogen and sugars + warm and moist climate | Fast |
| lignin, chitin, low temperature, anaerobic conditions | Slow |
### Food chains and trophic levels — Grazing vs detritus chains
## 1. Grazing vs detritus chains
| Chain | Starts with | Example |
| Grazing | Living green plants (autotrophs) → herbivores → carnivores | Grass → goat → human (PYQ 2025); grass → grasshopper → frog → snake → eagle (UPSC) |
| Detritus | Dead organic matter (dead leaves, carcasses, remains) → detritivores/decomposers | Leaf litter → earthworm → bird |
- PYQ 2025 (A-R) / APPSC-G2 2025:
- "Grazing chain begins with dead leaves" = false
- "Autotrophs capture energy and pass (transfer) it to herbivores" = true
- Pond: phytoplankton → zooplankton → fish; bacteria/fungi as decomposers
- Grassland: grass → grasshopper/rabbit → snake/hawk
- Aquatic sequence: phytoplankton → zooplankton → small fish → large fish
### Food chains and trophic levels — Trophic levels
## 1. Trophic levels
- Trophic level: an organism's functional position in the food chain; one species can occupy more than one level
- Energy decreases upward mainly due to heat loss
- Limit: rarely exceed 4-5 trophic levels - too little energy remains higher up
| Trophic level | Organisms | Role |
| First — I (T1) | Producers - green plants, phytoplankton, seaweeds; chemosynthetic bacteria (e.g. at hydrothermal vents) | T1 - producers (autotrophs); chemosynthetic bacteria make food from chemical energy |
| Second — II (T2) | Primary consumers — herbivores, zooplankton | T2 - primary consumers |
| Third — III (T3) | Secondary consumers — carnivores | T3 - secondary consumers |
| Fourth / highest — IV/V (T4) | Tertiary consumers — top carnivores | T4 - tertiary, quaternary consumers |
| - | Detritivores (earthworms), decomposers (bacteria, fungi) | Base of the detritus food chain; they recycle all levels, not a single slot |
- Missing-level question: "tiger, grass, snake, frog" has no herbivore (second level) (PYQ 2025)
- Exam angle: a "missing second trophic level" means no herbivore in the chain (e.g. tiger, grass, snake, frog); "correct grazing chain" means it starts with a producer and moves up in order.
### Food webs and ecosystem stability — terms
## 1. Food webs
- Food web: network of interlinked food chains
- Shows all energy paths; gives species alternative food → better survival
- Stability: more complex web → more alternative pathways → ecosystem more stable
- Stress: warming and ocean acidification disrupt food chains through coral bleaching
## 2. Stability terms
| Term | Meaning |
| Resistance | ability to withstand a disturbance without change [GK] |
| Resilience | ability to recover after a disturbance (APPSC 2024) |
| Homeostasis | self-regulation - every ecosystem resists stress up to a limit through feedback (APP) |
- Stability: rises with diversity and web complexity - diverse, complex systems (rainforest, coral reef) more stable than simple ones (tundra, desert, monoculture cropland)
- ⚠ Resistance = withstand; resilience = recover
### Energy flow — productivity and ten percent law — Nature of energy flow
## 1. Nature of energy flow
- Energy flow: Unidirectional — energy transfer upward in a food chain decreases (Lindeman's 10% law — energy transfer between trophic levels)
- sun → producers → consumers; not cycled (nutrients are)
- Primary source: Sun
- Laws of thermodynamics: energy flow obeys them
- First law: energy is transformed, not created or destroyed
- Second law: every transfer loses energy as heat
## 2. Capture and productivity
- Productivity: Primary — at the autotroph level; secondary — at the heterotroph level
- PAR: under 50% of incident sunlight is photosynthetically active radiation (PAR); plants fix only about 2-10% of PAR
- Total primary production efficiency: share of incoming solar energy is captured and used in photosynthesis (PYQ 2024; APPSC 2024)
- On average only about 1-2%
| Term | Meaning |
| GPP (gross primary productivity) | Total rate at which producers fix solar energy / make organic matter in photosynthesis [GK] |
| NPP (net primary productivity) | GPP minus respiration losses (R) of producers - energy available to consumers (herbivores) [GK] |
| Secondary productivity | Rate of new organic matter formed by consumers (APP) |
### Energy flow — productivity and ten percent law — Ten percent law
## 1. Ten percent law
- Ten percent law: only about 10% passes to the next level; about 90% lost at each step (respiration, excretion, locomotion, heat)
- Lindeman's ten per cent law - Raymond Lindeman (1942); some notes spell it "Lindemann"
- Consequence: chains limited to 4-5 trophic levels
## 2. Productivity ranking (NPP per unit area)
- Among the listed ecosystems: mangroves — the highest; oceans — the lowest
- ⚠ Sources differ: other notes say the most productive are tropical rainforests, which lead on land - standard answer among deserts, rainforests, oceans, estuaries (PYQ 2025; APPSC 2025)
- Also high: estuaries, coral reefs, swamps
- Lowest: deserts and the open ocean (deserts and open oceans); ocean's total output still large because of its area
- Exam angle: "most productive ecosystem" = tropical rainforest in APPSC keys.
### Ecological pyramids — types, inverted cases, limitations
- An ecological pyramid represents trophic-level relationships in an ecosystem
- Base vs apex: base = producers (always); apex = top consumers (top carnivore)
- Counting: count all organisms at a level; one species may occupy more than one level
- Types: Three (three types)
| Pyramid | Represents | Shape / usually upright | Inverted case |
| Numbers | The number of organisms | Upright or inverted — upright (grassland, pond) | Tree ecosystem - one tree, many insects; parasitic food chain |
| Biomass | The biomass at each trophic level | Upright or inverted — upright in a forest and grassland (most terrestrial ecosystems) | inverted in a pond and marine ecosystem - Aquatic / sea: small standing crop (biomass) of fast-turning phytoplankton supports larger zooplankton/fish |
| Energy | The energy at each trophic level | Always upright - energy lost as heat at every step | Never |
- Limitations:
- Simple chain assumed: rare in nature; food webs not shown
- Single level: a species cannot be placed at two trophic levels
- Decomposers ignored: Saprophytes / decomposers get no place, despite their vital role
- Practical issues: numbers hard to count; organism size ignored
- Exam angle: "which pyramid is inverted": pyramid of numbers in a parasitic chain; "which can never be inverted": the energy pyramid.
### Biomagnification vs bioaccumulation — terms and examples
## 1. Classes of pollutant
- Biodegradable: Sewage; non-biodegradable: DDT, plastic, asbestos
- Primary pollutants: CO, SO₂, NOx; secondary: O₃, PAN (peroxyacetyl nitrate), smog
## 2. Biomagnification
- Biomagnification: DDT and mercury — the highest concentration of DDT at the top trophic level
| Term | Meaning |
| Bioaccumulation | toxin builds up inside one organism over its life faster than it is excreted |
| Biomagnification | toxin's concentration rises at each higher trophic level, peaking in top predators |
- Condition: only persistent, fat-soluble, non-biodegradable pollutants (DDT, mercury, PCBs) biomagnify
| Pollutant | Effect |
| DDT | thinning of bird eggshells in fish-eating birds; seabird population crashes |
| Mercury | methylmercury (methyl mercury) concentrates in apex predators such as dolphins and orcas; caused Minamata disease in Japan |
| Oil spills | toxic hydrocarbons accumulate up marine food chains |
### Biotic interactions → sign → example
- Intraspecific: same species (reproduction, help, competition for food/space)
- Interspecific: different species - beneficial, harmful or neutral
- Symbiosis vs antagonism: symbiosis = one or both partners benefit; antagonism = at least one harmed
| Interaction | Sign (A/B) | Example |
| Mutualism | +/+ | Lichen (alga + fungus); fig and fig wasp (pollination for breeding site); mycorrhiza |
| Commensalism | +/0 | Orchid epiphyte on mango (epiphytes on trees); cattle egret feeding near grazing cattle; barnacles on whales |
| Parasitism | +/- | Cuscuta on host (hedge) plants; ticks and lice |
| Predation | +/- | Tiger and deer; sea star Pisaster eating mussels |
| Competition | -/- | Species using the same limited resource; Gause's competitive exclusion principle |
| Amensalism | -/0 | Allelopathic chemicals; large tree shading a small plant; Penicillium antibiosis (inhibiting bacteria) |
| Neutralism | 0/0 | - |
- Resource partitioning: lets competing species coexist - e.g. insect pests feeding on different parts of the same plant
- ⚠ Commensalism = +/0 (cattle egret); amensalism = -/0 (Penicillium)

### Wetlands — functions, Ramsar Convention, rules and day
## Ramsar and rules
- ★ Ramsar Convention — not regulatory, no punitive sanctions; not mandatory for India to protect all wetlands
- Wetlands (Conservation and Management) Rules, 2017 — India's own legal framework
- ★ World Wetlands Day — 2 February (Convention adopted 2 February 1971, Ramsar, Iran)
## Wetlands of India
- ★ Gujarat has the largest wetland area (17.6%)
- Inland about 69.23%, coastal 27.13%, small wetlands 3.64%
## Other
- ★ Photosynthesis happens day or night — marine algae use moonlight; also electric light
### Plant adaptations to habitat — halophytes, xerophytes, hydrophytes
## Desert plants
- ★ Xerophyte — adapted to dry climate (cactus, opuntia, madar, nerium)
- ★ Long roots — grow in search of water (not due to high soil temperature)
- ★ Desert plants mostly succulent (store water)
## Other types
- ★ Halophytes — salt-loving; grow on saline soils (mangrove swamps, marshes, seashores)
- ★ Hydrophyte — aquatic plant
- ★ Epiphytes — depend on other plants for mechanical support; not parasitic (orchids, mosses)
- ★ Cuscuta (Amarbel) — parasitic angiosperm; haustoria reach host xylem and phloem (not food from host leaves)
- ★ Phytoplankton abound in the limnetic zone because it is the open water zone
### Habitats and special adaptations — microbes, plants, animals
## Insectivorous plants
- ★ Pitcher plant / Nepenthes — insectivorous; pitcher is a modified leaf
- ★ Grow in nitrogen-deficient soils (marshy, acidic); trap insects for nitrogen
## Other
- ★ Desert leaf modifications: hard waxy leaves, tiny or no leaves, thorns instead of leaves
- ★ Lithophytes — grow on rocks (epipetric/epilithic)
- ★ Cuscuta (dodder) — parasite; produces haustoria
