### Sultans and the ulema → religious policy — Ulema, qazis and the Islamic character of the state
## 1. Ulema, qazis and the Islamic character of the state
- Ulema: Interpreted the sharia; wanted a say in state policy
- Relied on by some (Firoz); shut out by others (Alauddin)
- Sadr-us-Sudur: Chief Sadr; headed Diwan-i-Risalat (religious affairs, grants to mosques and madrasas) + Chief Qazi; controlled religious grants
- Qazis: Administered Sharia law in Muslims' civil cases; Qazi-ul-Quzat = chief judge
- Barani's view: Sultanate not truly Islamic (UPSC 2002 key)
- Reason: the Sultan supplemented Muslim law by framing his own regulations (zawabit)
- Ideal set out in *Fatawa-i-Jahandari*
### Sultans and the ulema → religious policy — Religious policy by Sultan
## 1. Religious policy by Sultan
| Sultan | Approach |
| Iltutmish | Tolerant; gave non-Muslims posts |
| Jalaluddin Khalji | First Sultan with a benevolent attitude towards Hindus |
| Alauddin Khalji | Kept the ulema out of state affairs; reduced ulema interference in state matters; told Qazi Mughisuddin he decreed whatever he judged good for the state; acted in the state's interest without ulema guidance → state not a true theocracy |
| Nasiruddin Khusrau Shah (1320) | Hindu gods and goddesses worshipped in the royal palace (UPPSC 2021) |
| Muhammad bin Tughlaq | Leaned on the Sufis against the ulema; visited the Ajmer shrine |
| Firoz Tughlaq | Followed the ulema's advice (ulema counsel; theocratic principles); respected the Caliph of Egypt; ordered temples destroyed, prohibited public idol worship, penalised Hindus and Shias; jizya on Hindus, extended to Brahmins [GK] |
| Sikandar Lodi | Orthodox: destroyed Mathura temples and broke the Jwalamukhi images at Nagarkot; imposed jizya |
- ⚠ Alauddin = ulema shut out; Firoz = ulema followed
### Jizya and temple destruction — features
## 1. Temple destruction
- Motives beyond fanaticism: Political + economic motives as well as religious
- Rival royal legitimacy (political)
## 2. Jizya
- Nature: Protection tax on non-Muslims in place of military service; collected unevenly / irregularly, sometimes waived
- Exempt: Women, children, the old and disabled, and at first Brahmins
- Imposed by:
- Firoz extended it to Brahmins (exempt earlier)
- Sikandar Lodi enforced it
- Golconda: Qutb Shahis never imposed jizya
### Position of women — purdah, sati, jauhar, women rulers
- Islamic law: Women's rights in property, inheritance and dower (mahr)
- Sati: Prevalent, documented cases; described by Ibn Battuta
- Jauhar: At Ranthambhor (1301) and Chittor (1303) against Alauddin
- Razia Sultana (1236-40): Only woman to rule the Delhi Sultanate; resisted due to gender norms
- Female saints: Lal Ded (Kashmir) found a voice through Bhakti and Sufism
### Slavery — sources, markets, Diwan-i-Bandagan
- Alauddin: Third regulated market = horses, slaves and cattle at fixed prices
- Firoz Tughlaq: About 1,80,000 slaves; created Diwan-i-Bandagan (also weakened the army)
- Slave dynasty: Aibak, Iltutmish, Balban were themselves slaves (bandagan) → "Slave (Mamluk) dynasty"
### Conversion — theories, causes and debates
| Theory | Status in current scholarship |
| Sword (forced mass conversion) | Rejected as a colonial trope; coercion was isolated |
| Political patronage / state employment | A contributing factor (tax relief, office) |
| Social liberation (escape from caste) | Partial: egalitarian ideals appealed, but caste-like stratification survived among converts |
| Sufi influence | Major factor, especially in Bengal and Punjab, together with agrarian expansion; Sufi saints spread Islam among lower castes + farming communities via pirs and dargahs |
### Sufis and scholars → contributions to syncretism
| Person / text | Contribution |
| Al-Biruni | Noted parallels: Sufi idea of soul vs Patanjali's Yoga Sutra; Sufi divine love vs Gita |
| Amritakund | Hatha-yoga text translated into Arabic and Persian (13th c.) |
| Nizamuddin Auliya | Struck by Nath-yogic body theory |
| Sheikh Nuruddin | Founded Kashmir's Rishi movement (echoing Lalla); vegetarianism; served all castes |
| Chishti Sufis | Muinuddin, Nizamuddin Auliya, Baba Farid |

| Pir Bhodan | Sufi musician |
| Raja Man Singh of Gwalior | *Man Kautuhal* |

- ⚠ Rishi movement = Sheikh Nuruddin (Kashmir); Alakh Niranjan = Khuda = Hamiduddin Nagori
### Bhakti-Sufi interaction — features, shared rituals, music
- Chishti Sufis:
- Spoke to people in Hindavi
- Khanqahs + dargahs drew Hindus and Muslims, alongside Bhakti and Nath traditions
- Bhakti: Indigenous roots (Alvars and Nayanars); Islam reinforced equality + rejection of ritual and shaped later Bhakti's egalitarianism (Kabir, Nanak, Ravidas)
- Sufi poetry: Human love as metaphor for divine love (Jayasi's *Padmavat*); in the Deccan written in Dakhani (early Urdu)
- Deccan: Mixed culture; Hindu Nayakas served Sultans, Sultans patronised temples
### Universal angles
- Current relevance:
- Conversion politics (6 Apr 2026) recalls Meenakshipuram (Feb 1981): 558 Dalits embraced Islam to escape caste oppression → "social liberation" argument
- AP angle:
- Islam arrived with Malik Kafur (1310) and MBT
- Gesu Daraz (Bandanawaz): moved to Gulbarga under Firoz Shah Bahmani; wrote in Persian + early Dakhni (*Mirqat-ul-Ashyaq*)
- Golconda: foreign Muslims = Afaqis; Brahmins got agraharas + Mustajir (tax-farming) rights
- Qutb Shahis = Shia rulers who patronised Hindu temples; Abul Hasan Tana Shah granted villages to Bhadrachalam temple
- Akkanna and Madanna held top posts; Telugu poets called them "Andhra Sultans"
- Criticism & debates:
- Conversion: "destruction" vs "liberation" framings; forced vs social and Sufi-driven; current scholarship stresses regional variation
- State: theocracy (Barani) vs military state adjusting to local realities
