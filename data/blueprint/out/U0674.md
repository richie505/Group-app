## U0674 | INDUSTRIAL LOCATION – FACTORS AND THEORIES (WEBER); WORLD INDUSTRIAL REGIONS
### 1. FACTORS OF INDUSTRIAL LOCATION
| Factor | Pull | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Raw material (weight-losing) | Near ore or coalfield | Iron and steel, sugar |
| Market | Near consumers or ports | Japan's market-based steel at the coast |
| Power, labour, transport, capital, policy | Hydropower, skilled labour, ports, government support | Northern Italy, Pearl River Delta |
- **Steel**: about **4 tonnes** of inputs (iron ore, coking coal, limestone, dolomite) → **1 tonne** of steel, so plants sit on coalfields or ore fields.
- **Japan**: **market-based** iron and steel; imports ore and coal in big ships, so plants are at **ports/coast** (mostly the southern coastal region; some at Kamaishi in NE Honshu and Muroran).
### 2. WEBER'S LEAST-COST THEORY (1909)
- **Alfred Weber**: firm locates where total **transport + labour + agglomeration** costs are least.
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Locational triangle** | **2 raw-material sources + 1 market**; least-transport-cost point lies inside it |
| **Material Index (MI)** | Weight of localised raw materials / weight of finished product |
| **Isodapanes** | Lines of **equal extra transport cost**; labour or agglomeration savings pull a firm off the least-transport point |
- **MI > 1** → locate at raw material; **MI < 1** → at market.
| Material type | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Ubiquities** | Available everywhere | Water, air |
| **Pure localised** | Lose no weight | Cotton |
| **Gross (weight-losing)** | Lose weight in processing | Iron ore, coal, sugarcane |
!TRAP Cotton is non-weight-losing, so textile mills are NOT tied to raw-cotton sites.
### 3. OTHER LOCATION THEORISTS
| Theorist | Idea |
|---|---|
| Alfred Weber | Least cost |
| **Edgar Hoover** | Transport-cost structure, **terminal costs**; favours ports and **break-of-bulk** points |
| **Walter Isard** | **Substitution principle** (trade-off between transport and other costs) |
| **August Losch** | **Maximum-profit** / market-area (demand) approach |
| **P. Sargant Florence** | **Location Quotient** (a region's share of an industry vs its national share) |
!PAIR Weber | least cost | 1909 || Losch | maximum profit | market area
### 4. FOOTLOOSE, SUNRISE, UMLAND AND AGGLOMERATION
- **Footloose**: not tied to raw material or a fixed place; light, high-value, low transport cost, low pollution – **diamond cutting** (APPSC 2019 answer), electronics, software, watch-making.
- **Sunrise industry**: information technology, electronics.
- **Umland**: area surrounding a town, linked by common economic and cultural activities; term first used in **1883**.
- **Agglomeration economies**: cost savings when firms cluster – shared infrastructure, specialised suppliers, skilled labour pool. They do NOT tie footloose industries to raw materials.

### 8. LEADING PRODUCERS AND THE SHIFT TO ASIA
- **Steel**: **China 1st** (over 50% of world steel); **India 2nd** – 168.4 MT crude steel; capacity about 220 MT; target **300 MT by 2030**.
- Leading cotton textile producer: **China** (2025).
- **Shipbuilding (2025 new orders)**: **China** first (about 60–66%), **South Korea** second (about 20%), **Japan** third.
- India: Rourkela = first public-sector plant (German help); **VISL Bhadravati** = oldest public-sector steel plant in the south.
- **Shift to Asia**: China and India are the top two steel producers; old Western regions (Ruhr, Lorraine, US Rust Belt) declined.
- **Asian Tigers** (newly industrialising): South Korea, Taiwan, Singapore, Hong Kong.
- Revised Index of Core Industries, base **2022-23**; iron ore added → **nine** industries (July 2026).
