## U0526 | MONETARY POLICY – OBJECTIVES, INSTRUMENTS (CRR, SLR, REPO, REVERSE REPO, MSF, OMO, LAF), TRANSMISSION
### 1. MONETARY POLICY – AUTHORITY AND OBJECTIVES
- Formulated and implemented by the **RBI** under the **RBI Act, 1934** (APPSC GS 2023).
- **Main objective: price stability**, keeping growth in mind; also financial stability, external balance, development goals.
- The **MPC** sets the repo rate to hit the inflation target; liquidity is kept so money-market rates stay near repo.
- Monetary policy instruments: **Repo, Reverse Repo, Bank Rate, CRR, SLR**. **Fiscal deficit** and **public revenue** belong to **fiscal policy**.
!KEY Repurchase option (**Repo**) is used by RBI to regulate money supply and inflation.
### 2. EVOLUTION OF THE FRAMEWORK
| Phase | Framework | Landmarks |
|---|---|---|
| **1985–1998** | **Monetary targeting** with feedback (**M3**) | **Chakravarty Committee 1985**; Vaghul Committee 1987 (money market) |
| **1998–2016** | **Multiple indicator approach** (growth, inflation, exchange rate, credit) | **LAF 2000**; **MSS 2004**; Base Rate 2010; **MSF 2011**; MCLR 2016 |
| **2016 onwards** | **Flexible inflation targeting** | **Urjit Patel Committee 2014**; **MPFA 2015**; RBI Act amended **2016**; **SDF 2022** |
- Key post-1991 change: **deregulation of interest rates** (APPSC GS 2023).
- Multiple indicator approach dropped: **opaque**; markets could not predict RBI when indicators conflicted.
- **"Seven Ages of India's Monetary Policy"**: **Shaktikanta Das**, **24 Jan 2020**, St. Stephen's College (APPSC GS 2024).
- Repo − reverse repo gap was **1.00%** (June 2010–June 2011); from 2011-12 only repo is fixed, reverse repo set automatically.
### 3. QUANTITATIVE VS QUALITATIVE INSTRUMENTS
| Basis | Quantitative (general) | Qualitative (selective) |
|---|---|---|
| Aim | **Volume and cost** of total credit | **Direction** of credit |
| Other name | Traditional methods | **Selective credit control** |
| Tools | **Bank rate, repo, reverse repo, OMO, CRR, SLR (variable reserve ratio)** | **Margin requirements, moral suasion, credit rationing, direct action**, credit ceilings, minimum interest on advances for sensitive goods, consumer credit regulation |
- **Main instrument: policy repo rate** – the single operating rate under FIT and most-watched signal.
- **OMOs**: main tool for **durable** liquidity.
- **Margin** = value of security − loan; **higher margin = smaller loan** against the same security.
!TRAP **Variable reserve ratios** and **Bank Rate policy** are **quantitative**, not selective. Moral suasion, margin requirements, direct action = qualitative.
### 4. POLICY RATES AND THE LAF CORRIDOR
#### 4.1 Instruments at a Glance
| Instrument | Meaning | Rate (Aug 2026) |
|---|---|---|
| **Repo** | RBI **lends** short-term funds to banks against G-secs; principal policy rate | **5.25%** (MPC, 5 Aug 2026, unanimous, neutral) |
| **SDF** | Banks park surplus with RBI **without collateral**; **floor** | **5.00%** |
| **MSF** | **Overnight emergency** borrowing at penal rate, dipping into **SLR up to 2%** of NDTL; **ceiling**, repo + 25 bps | **5.50%** |
| **Bank Rate** | RBI's lending/discount rate; rediscounts **bills of exchange or commercial paper**; long-term; penal rates linked to it | **5.50%** |
| **Fixed reverse repo** | RBI **borrows** from banks against G-secs; at RBI's discretion since SDF | **3.35%** (May 2026) |
| **CRR** | Share of NDTL kept as **cash with RBI**; no interest | **3%** |
| **SLR** | Share of NDTL kept by bank in cash, gold, approved G-secs | **18%** |
| **OMO** | RBI buys/sells G-secs (through banks, not directly with the public) | – |
!NUM Corridor: **SDF 5.00% < Repo 5.25% < MSF = Bank Rate 5.50%**; width **50 bps**.
- MSF began **May 2011** at repo + 100 bps, borrowing up to 1% of NDTL; now repo + 25 bps, up to 2%. MSF aligned to Bank Rate in **Feb 2012**.
- **NBFC access to LAF**: only **Primary Dealers** (classed as NBFCs).
#### 4.2 Comparisons
| Basis | Repo | Reverse repo | Bank Rate | MSF |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Who borrows | Bank from RBI | RBI from bank | Bank from RBI | Bank from RBI |
| Use | Inject liquidity | Absorb surplus | Monetary policy signal | Marginal emergency need |
| Against | Eligible securities | G-secs | Bills of exchange/CP | SLR portfolio |
| Cost | Policy rate | Lower | = MSF | Higher than repo |
- **Reverse repo** = rate at which **banks lend to RBI**. **MSF rate** and **NDTL** are terms of banking operations.
#### 4.3 Stance Terms
| Stance | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Accommodative** | Supports growth; rate cuts likely |
| **Neutral** | Either way, data-dependent |
| **Hawkish** | Worried about inflation; tightens |
| **Dovish** | Worried about growth; easing bias |
- Path: neutral → accommodative (Apr 2025) → **neutral (June 2025)**, retained through Aug 2026.
### 5. CRR VS SLR
#### 5.1 Comparison
| Basis | CRR | SLR |
|---|---|---|
| Kept | **With RBI** | **By the bank itself** |
| Form | **Cash only** | Cash, **gold**, unencumbered **G-secs, T-bills, SDLs**, approved securities |
| Returns | **No interest** | Earns interest on G-secs |
| Law | **Sec 42, RBI Act 1934** | **Sec 24, BR Act 1949** |
| Limits | No floor or ceiling (since 2006) | **0%–40%** of NDTL |
| Now | **3%** | **18%** (Sep 2023) |
!TRAP APPSC 2023: "**Required reserve ratio**" = **CRR**.
#### 5.2 SLR Details
- Minimum share of NDTL banks keep in liquid assets **before lending**; mechanism by which banks **provide credit to Government**.
- **MSS securities count** towards SLR.
- **25% floor removed** by **BR (Amendment) Act, 2007**.
- Trend: **38.5% (1991-92)** → cut on **Narasimham Committee** advice → 19.25% (2019) → **18%**. Since reforms, both SLR and CRR reduced.
- Shortfall penalty: **bank rate + 3%** → **bank rate + 5%** if it continues.
- An SLR cut of 50 bps → banks may cut lending rates. **Lower CRR** → banks have higher leverage to liquidity.
### 6. TOOL → EFFECT
#### 6.1 Dear Money vs Cheap Money
| Tool | Control inflation (dear, contractionary) | Boost growth (cheap, expansionary) |
|---|---|---|
| CRR, SLR | Raise | Cut |
| Repo, reverse repo, Bank Rate | Raise | Cut |
| OMO | **Sell** G-secs | Buy G-secs |
- **Raising CRR**: less money in circulation + lower demand for goods and services (APPSC G1 2022: statements I and II); reduces liquidity; helps control inflation.
!TRAP Raising CRR does **NOT** raise purchasing power or lending capacity. **Purchase** of G-secs does **not** control inflation. **Reducing interest rates** is expansionary, not anti-inflation.
- **Lower bank rate** → cheaper borrowing, more liquidity (expansionary); higher bank rate → tight.
- CRR/SLR cut → more lendable funds, higher **money multiplier** (M3/M0 = **6.21**, 31 Dec 2025).
- More money supply than real output growth → **inflation**.
- **Expansionary policy** cannot stop the rupee's slide (it depreciates the rupee); rupee support = curb non-essential imports, promote exports, **Masala Bonds**, ease ECB conditions.
- Policy rates directly regulate **capacity for credit creation**.
### 7. LIQUIDITY MANAGEMENT AND STERILISATION
#### 7.1 Short-term Windows
| Tool | Feature |
|---|---|
| **LAF** | Repo + reverse repo/SDF; **primary window** for day-to-day liquidity |
| **SDF** | Proposed 2015; enabled by **Budget 2018-19**; began **April 2022**; absorbs **without collateral** (APPSC G2 2025) |
| **VRR / VRRR** | Auction-based; VRR injects, VRRR absorbs for longer than overnight SDF |
| **Fine-tuning operations** | Short VRR/VRRR auctions in line with stance; **49 in FY24** (APPSC G2 2025) |
#### 7.2 Durable Liquidity and Reserve Tools
- **I-CRR**: temporary **10%**, announced **10 Aug 2023**; impounded about **₹1.1 lakh crore** (APPSC G2 2025: both statements correct).
- **OMO** purchases: **₹2.39 lakh crore** (Apr–May 2025) + **₹1 lakh crore** (Dec 2025, to stabilise bond yields); more in March 2026.
- **LTRO** (2020): 1–3 year funds at repo.
- **USD/INR buy-sell swap** ($5 bn, 3-year, Dec 2025): injects rupees without changing RBI's balance-sheet size.
#### 7.3 Sterilisation
- Forex inflows swell rupee liquidity → RBI **sells G-secs (OMO/MSS)** or runs **VRRRs** to cancel the effect on money supply.
- **MSS (April 2004)**: T-bills/MSBs issued; proceeds in a **separate account the Government cannot spend**.
#### 7.4 Surplus vs Deficit Liquidity
- **Surplus** liquidity speeds transmission and pulls rates below repo; **deficit** weakens it.
- Dec 2025 tightness (currency leakage, tax outflows) → OMO + $5 bn swap.
- By **3 Sep 2026**: surplus about **₹10.32 lakh crore** after FCNR(B) inflows; call rate below repo; on **4 Sep 2026** RBI absorbed **₹6.02 lakh crore** via two 3-day VRRRs.
### 8. LENDING-RATE BENCHMARKS
#### 8.1 Who Sets Which Rate
| Fixed by RBI/MPC | Set by each bank | Market-determined |
|---|---|---|
| Repo, SDF, MSF, Bank Rate, reverse repo, **CRR, SLR** | PLR/BPLR (old), Base Rate, **MCLR**, spread over EBLR | **Call money rate** (WACR), TREPS, T-bill and G-sec yields |
- RBI does **not** fix the **PLR**.
#### 8.2 Evolution
| Benchmark | From | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| PLR | 1994 | Rate for best borrowers, set by each bank |
| **Base Rate** | **1 July 2010** | **Average cost** of funds → slow transmission |
| **MCLR** | **1 April 2016** | **Marginal cost** of funds + CRR/SLR carry + operating cost + tenor premium; internal benchmark |
| **EBLR** | **1 Oct 2019** | New floating-rate **personal/retail and MSME** loans linked to **repo, 3/6-month T-bill yield or FBIL rate**; fastest |
- **MCLR purpose** (UPSC 2016, both correct): faster transmission. HDFC Bank 1-year MCLR 8.40% (8 Jun 2026).
- **Janak Raj Internal Study Group (2017)**: MCLR transmission weak → external benchmark → EBLR mandate.
- **Draft RBI (Interest Rates on Loans and Advances) Directions, 2026**: from **1 April 2027**; banks, RRBs, co-ops, AIFIs, NBFCs/HFCs; floating personal and MSME loans stay on external benchmark; interest on daily reducing balance.
### 9. TRANSMISSION – CHANNELS, LAGS AND YIELDS
#### 9.1 Channels
1. Repo up → 2. money-market rates up → 3. costlier credit → 4. lower consumption and investment → 5. lower aggregate demand → inflation cools.
| Channel | How a rate cut works |
|---|---|
| Interest rate | Cheaper loans raise consumption and investment |
| Credit | Banks lend more |
| Exchange rate | Weaker rupee helps exports |
| Asset price | Bond and equity prices rise (wealth effect) |
| Expectations | Credible target anchors expectations (does not remove all uncertainty) |
- Rate cut → **higher investment expenditure** (UPSC 2014), not higher savings or tax collection.
#### 9.2 Lags
- EBLR loans reprice quickly; **deposit rates and MCLR adjust slowly**. Outstanding-loan WALR **9.26%** (Sep 2025).
- Repo cut **125 bps** (Feb 2025–Feb 2026, 6.50% → 5.25%); fresh-loan WALR fell about **93 bps** (Feb 2025–Mar 2026), about 80 bps from rate changes and 13 bps from loan mix.
#### 9.3 G-sec Yields
- Driven by **US Fed actions + RBI actions + domestic inflation and short-term rates** (all three, UPSC 2021).
- Narrower spread of Indian over US yields → lower borrowing costs.
### 10. OBSTACLES AND CHALLENGES
#### 10.1 Challenges after 1991
| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Exchange-rate volatility from capital flows** | **Main challenge** (APPSC GS 2023) |
| Open capital account | Moves liquidity and rupee → sterilisation |
| External shocks | Oil, global rate cycles, West Asia conflict (2026) |
| **Fiscal dominance** | Large borrowing; **ad hoc T-bills ended 1997** → **Ways and Means Advances** |
!TRAP Forex reserves were NOT a problem; inflation was not low; revenue was not in surplus.
#### 10.2 Obstacles to Transmission
| Obstacle | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Administered small-savings rates** | Hold deposit rates up |
| NPAs | Weak balance sheets (GNPA now 2.2%, Sep 2025) |
| Deposit dependence | Banks reprice only new deposits |
| Heavy Government borrowing | High yields (crowding out) |
| **Informal credit** | Outside RBI's reach |
| Structural rigidities | Banking rigidities (2026) |
#### 10.3 Monetary vs Fiscal Policy
| Basis | Fiscal | Monetary |
|---|---|---|
| Authority | Government (Finance Ministry, Parliament) | RBI / MPC |
| Tools | Spending, taxes, subsidies, borrowing | Repo, OMO, CRR/SLR, LAF |
| Effect | Direct | Indirect |
| Speed | Slow; **legislative and implementation lags** | Quick (bi-monthly) |
- Monetary tools are **demand-side** only; **food inflation** is supply-driven → needs buffer-stock releases, duty cuts, farm reform.
