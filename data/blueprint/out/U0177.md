## U0177 | ECONOMIC IMPACT OF COLONIAL RULE – DRAIN OF WEALTH, DEINDUSTRIALISATION, COMMERCIALISATION OF AGRICULTURE
### 1. STAGES OF COLONIALISM
| Stage | Period | Feature |
|---|---|---|
| **Mercantilist** | 1757–1813 | Company trade monopoly; direct plunder; Bengal revenue buys export goods |
| **Free trade / industrial capital** | 1813–1858 | India a **market** for British goods and source of **raw materials** |
| **Finance capital** | 1858 onwards | British capital in railways, plantations, banks |
- Proposed by **R.P. Dutt** in ***India Today***, drawing on **Karl Marx**. R.P. Dutt is a later Marxist writer, not an original drain theorist.
- **1813** opened the free-trade stage; finance-capital stage began **1858**.
- India changed from a **leading exporter of manufactures** to a **supplier of raw materials**.

### 3. DRAIN OF WEALTH – MEANING AND COMPONENTS
- **Meaning:** transfer of India's wealth to England **without adequate economic or material return**.
- **Began** with the **Diwani of Bengal (1765)**.
- India generally had a **favourable balance of trade**; the drain took the form of **unrequited exports** – the export surplus funded British **office and war expenses**, with no matching gold or silver.
| Component (Home Charges and more) | What it covered |
|---|---|
| **Interest** | On the **public debt raised in Britain** |
| **Salaries and pensions** | Of British civil and military officials, remitted home |
| **Guaranteed railway returns** | **5%** assured to British investors, whether lines earned or not |
| **Military expenditure** | British Indian Army and imperial wars (Afghanistan, Burma) – the **largest single budget head** |
| **Profits** | Of British capitalists, merchants and banks |
| Unequal trade | Also a component |
- **Cause per Naoroji:** **profits taken by British capitalists from India** (APPSC 2024).
!TRAP The drain was **NOT** an excess of imports.
- **Effects:** stifled **capital formation** and industrialisation.
- **Moderates demanded:** cuts in **military spending**, lower **land revenue**, end of **salt tax**, **protective tariffs**.
### 4. FORMS OF EXPLOITATION – FREE TRADE AND TARIFFS
- **Charter Act 1813** ended the Company's monopoly → **one-way free trade**: British machine goods entered India **almost duty-free**; Indian goods faced **heavy duties in Britain**.
- **Imperial preference:** special privileges (lower duties) for British imports in India.
- **Cotton excise duty (1894–96):** on Indian mill cloth to **offset the import duty** on British goods – classic tariff-policy example.
- **Railways** carried raw materials out and British goods inland; built also for **military mobility**, yet integrated the economy.
- Suez Canal (**1869**) tightened British control of trade.
- Karl Marx: "British Economic Policy is disgusting in India."
### 5. DEINDUSTRIALISATION
#### 5.1 Chronology
- Weavers coerced by Company agents (**Bolt, 1772**).
- Lancashire cloth in Bengal **1786**; calicoes displaced by **1793**.
- 1813 one-way free trade → by **1850** India took **one-fourth** of all British cotton exports.
#### 5.2 Causes (B.D. Basu's list)
| Cause | How it hit Indian industry |
|---|---|
| Trade policy | Free trade forced on India; flood of cheap machine goods; tariffs in Britain |
| Raw materials | Export of raw cotton |
| Duties | Internal transit and customs duties |
| **British privileges** | Special privileges for British traders; Company monopolies |
| Railways | Opened markets to British goods |
| Industrial Revolution | In the first half of the 19th century Indian **handicrafts were ruined** |
#### 5.3 Results
- Town handicrafts suffered a **"sudden and complete collapse"**.
- Marx: the British **"inundated the very mother country of cotton with cottons"**.
- Artisans pushed onto land → **overcrowding of agriculture**, disguised unemployment.
- **No independent industrial development** – absence of heavy industries; India kept as raw-material supplier.
### 6. ANDHRA TEXTILES
- **Textiles = second-largest employer after agriculture** in British-period Andhra (APPSC 2025).
- **Machilipatnam:** most famous handloom and textile port of the **16th–17th centuries**; **chintz** (painted cloth), dyed fabrics, muslins; **first Dutch factory** in Andhra (APHQ 2017).
| Place | Product |
|---|---|
| **Srikakulam** | **Muslin** |
| **Machilipatnam** | **Dyed clothes** |
| **Nellore** | **Towels** |
| **Eluru** | **Carpets** |
| **Kurnool** | **Blankets** |
| Tuni | Long cloths |
| Peddapuram, Dharmavaram, Adoni, Bellary | Silk clothes |
| **Uppada** | **Jamdani silk** |
| **Ponduru** (Srikakulam district) | Fine **hand-spun khadi** |
- **Decline:** exports from Kalingapatnam, Vizag, Bhimunipatnam, Corangi, Kakinada, Narsapur, Machilipatnam to England, France, Holland shrank under high Company duties to small boats carrying salt and grain.
!TRAP Ponduru = textiles (khadi), not iron and steel. Srikakulam = muslin; Eluru = carpets.
### 7. COMMERCIALISATION OF AGRICULTURE
- Peasants grew cash crops to **pay cash revenue**, not by choice; cash crops replaced food crops → food security fell → **famines frequent**.
| Crop | Main region |
|---|---|
| **Indigo** | Bengal, Bihar – **Tinkathiya**: indigo on **3/20** of each holding; Indigo Revolt (1859–60) |
| **Cotton** | Deccan, Gujarat, Berar |
| **Jute** | Bengal |
| **Opium** | **Bengal-Bihar**, Malwa (main region under British rule: **Bihar**) |
| **Tea** | Assam |
- **Triangular trade** (based on **opium**): Indian opium → China; Chinese tea and silver → Britain; British manufactures → India.
- Deindustrialisation went with **stagnant, low-productivity agriculture**.
#### 7.1 Peasant Protection Laws – Against Whom
| Law | Protected peasants against |
|---|---|
| **Deccan Agriculturists' Relief Act, 1879** | **Moneylenders** – after the **Deccan riots of 1875** |
| **Punjab Land Alienation Act, 1900** | Land transfer from **agriculturists to non-agriculturists** (moneylenders) |

- **1854–1901:** 24 famines, about **29 million** deaths; **Bengal famine 1943:** about 3 million.
- **"Sea of Calamity"** = **Orissa Famine 1866–67**; at least **1.3 million** died.

| **Lyall** | **1897–98** – after the 1896–97 famine (Elgin II) |

#### 8.1 Famines in Andhra
| Famine | Detail |
|---|---|
| 1823 | Kadapa, Kurnool, Chittoor, Guntur, Vizag, Nellore |
| **1832–33 Guntur** | **Dokkala / Nandana Karuvu**; about **2 lakh** dead, half the population; worst in the south (APHQ 2019) |
| **Ganjam 1865–66** | Ryots shifted **paddy → cotton** during the **American Civil War**; cotton prices crashed + rice scarce; migration to South Africa, Sri Lanka, Mauritius |

- **Eleven famines** in Andhra in the late 19th century; worst **1865–66**.
- Guntur famine → **Arthur Cotton's Godavari anicut (1847–52)**.
### 9. RISE OF MODERN INDUSTRIES
| Industry | First / key fact |
|---|---|
| **Cotton mill** | First attempt **1818, Fort Gloster** (Kolkata) failed; first successful: **Bombay Spinning and Weaving Co., 1854**, **Cowasji Nanabhai Davar** (Parsi) |
| **Jute mill** | **Rishra (Bengal), 1855** |
| **Tea company** | **Assam Company, 1839** – formed in England, Rs 5 lakh; HQ **Nazira** |
| **Iron and steel** | **TISCO**, incorporated **1907**, Jamshedpur |
| **Manganese** | **Visakhapatnam district** (Vizianagaram mines, worked from 1892) – world leader **1892–1929** |
| Mica | Nellore district |
- **Cotton mills Indian-owned** (Maharashtra, Gujarat); **jute mills foreign-owned** (Bengal).
- Before 1914 about **97%** of British capital went to government projects, plantations and finance houses.
- **Andhra:** jute mills at **Chittivalasa and Nellimarla** (1920s); paper mill at **Rajahmundry**; cotton ginning at Anantapur, Guntur, East Godavari, Kurnool.
- **First railway in Andhra:** **Puttur–Renigunta, 15 Sept 1862**.
### 10. CURRENCY POLICY – HILTON YOUNG COMMISSION
- **Hilton Young Commission (1926)** = Royal Commission on Indian Currency and Finance.
- Fixed rupee at **1s 6d (18 pence)**; Indian business (**Purshottamdas Thakurdas**) wanted **1s 4d**.
- Purpose: help **remittances from India** and keep India's **creditworthiness**.
- Effect: made British imports cheaper and **Indian goods dearer abroad**.
- Recommended a central bank → **RBI (1935)**.
### 11. INDIAN ECONOMY ON THE EVE OF INDEPENDENCE
| Indicator (1947) | Value |
|---|---|
| World income share (**Angus Maddison**) | **27% (1700) → 3% (1950)** |
| Per capita income | about **Rs 265** a year |
| Life expectancy | **32 years** |
| Literacy | **under 16%** (female under 9%) |
| Workforce in agriculture | **over 75%**, giving only about **50% of GDP** |
- **No capital-goods base:** basic/capital goods only about one-fourth of industrial output.
- **Public sector** limited to **railways, power, communications, ports**.
- Exports: primary products (raw silk, cotton, wool, sugar, indigo, jute); imports: finished consumer and capital goods.
- APPSC 2023 keys: deindustrialisation by British policy; India had a **strong industrial base** before British rule.
- Challenges: foodgrain imports, inflation → **First Plan** priority to **agriculture, irrigation and power**.
- **14 May 2026:** IMF-World Bank Structural Adjustment Programmes compared to colonial one-way free trade for causing deindustrialisation in the Global South.
