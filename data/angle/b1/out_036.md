## Features of each system | from: S1, S2, S4
# 1. Permanent Settlement - features and rationale
- **Settlement:** began as a **decennial (10-year)** settlement, made **permanent in 1793**; zamindars recognised as **proprietors** - zamindari rights **alienable, rentable and heritable**
- **Share:** state **10/11** of rent collected; zamindar kept **1/11** (some notes: **9/10** to government, **1/10** to zamindar in Andhra)
- **Reasons for high demand / fixity:**
  - **Fixed, assured income** + low collection cost
  - A **loyal landlord class**
  - Hope that zamindars would improve land
  - Demand fixed at a very high level → no share of later growth to the state
# 2. Ryotwari - features (APPSC-GS 2018)
- **Ryot = cultivator:** revenue settled directly with each ryot, **ryot by ryot**
- **Rights:** **proprietary rights** - ryots could **sell, mortgage, gift**, lease land as long as the assessment was paid; not evicted while revenue paid
- **Assessment:** fixed on land, not crop; rose or fell with the actual area cultivated; **assessed periodically** (usually **20-30** years; revised about **every 30 years**)
- **Officials:** **karanams** and village officers under the **Collector**
# 3. Mahalwari - features
- **Unit of settlement:** **mahal** (village or estate)
- **Collection:** revenue fixed for the village, collected by the **headman/village community**; each cultivator liable for his share; **periodically revised**
- **Ownership:** joint village ownership - common arrangements called **"Bhai Chara"** or **"Mahali"**
- **Areas:** **NWP (Agra-Oudh region), Punjab, Central India**; about **30%** of British India
  - **Mahalwari areas:** **North-Western Provinces (NWP) and parts of Punjab** (APPSC-GS 2018; APPSC 2018)

## Effects → factor and effect | from: S1, S5, S7
# 1. Zamindari areas
- **New landed class:** many old zamindars lost estates (sold for arrears) to **urban merchants and moneylenders**
  - Old zamindars lost estates to merchants at auction
- **Litigation and coercion:** zamindars went to courts against tenants; Haftam and Panjam regulations let them seize tenants' property; endless litigation
- **Company's dilemma:** cultivation and prices rose after **1800**, but fixed demand gave the Company nothing extra
  - APPSC-GS 2022: **gains from agriculture went to the zamindars**, not the Company
  - **APPSC 2022:** under zamindari the profit from agriculture went to the **zamindars**, not cultivators
- **Agrarian effects:** absentee landlordism, **sub-infeudation**, rack-renting; peasants reduced to **tenants-at-will**
- **Absentee landlordism:** zamindars moved to cities like **Calcutta**, left collection to agents; tenants became **tenants-at-will**; rack-renting (rent-racking of tenants)
- **Andhra effects:** absentee landlordism, sub-infeudation, no investment in land, loss of estates for arrears
# 2. Ryotwari and mahalwari areas
- **Ryotwari's problems:** over-assessment, no proper land records, unclear boundaries, corrupt subordinate officials
  - Peasants → moneylenders; defaults → land auctions and alienation
- **Rigidity:**
  - No famine remission, unlike the Mughal **zabt** (**one-third** share, **taccavi** loans)
  - Mahalwari broke village solidarity through **collective liability**
# 3. Economic impact
| **Factor** | **Effect** |
|---|---|
| **Indebtedness** | Rigid cash demand even in crop failure → ryots to **moneylenders (Mahajans)**; mortgage and eviction |
| **Land** | Became a **saleable commodity**; concentration in few hands |
| **Commercialisation** | Cash revenue forced **cash crops** (indigo, cotton, opium, jute) |
| Ryotwari areas | **American Civil War** cotton slump → defaults → land to **moneylenders** |
| Agriculture | Low investment; overcrowding as ruined artisans fell back on land |

- **New classes:**
  - Zamindari areas: merchant-landlords
  - Ryotwari and mahalwari areas: moneylender-landlords
  - Rural indebtedness + land alienation

## Universal angles | from: S9 | universal
- **Significance:**
  - Land became a transferable commodity; the three systems produced a loyal landlord class + rural indebtedness → peasant revolts
  - The three settlements shaped agrarian class structure that land reforms after **1947** tried to undo
  - Rayalaseema's direct ryot-state relationship dates from Munro → Munro remembered as the **first Principal Collector of Rayalaseema** (PYQ 2025)
- **AP angle:**
  - **Munro** (Rayalaseema ryotwari) vs zamindari **Circars** = core APPSC contrast; Palegar resistance (**Uyyalawada**) followed
  - All three systems ran in Andhra: zamindari in the Circars, ryotwari in the Ceded Districts, mahalwari in **Nellore**
- **FIRST/LAST:** first ryotwari experiment - **Baramahal (1792)**; first Collector of Ceded Districts - **Munro**
- **Comparative note:** **Mahalwari** system, **Holt Mackenzie (1822)**, village community as the unit
- **Criticism & debates:**
  - All three extractive; revenue paid in **cash** regardless of harvest
  - **Alice Thorner:** ryotwari destroyed the village system and isolated farmers from the government
  - State became the **"ultimate landlord"**; Ryotwari revenue as heavy as Zamindari rent
- **Current relevance:** AP Cabinet reclassified the residential part of **Pulladigunta** (**Guntur** district), long held as **'Thopu Poramboke'** → **201 families** got ownership after nearly a century (15 Jul 2026)

## Drain of wealth — meaning, components and effects | from: S1
# 1. Meaning
- **Meaning:** part of India's wealth sent to England **with no economic or material return**; began with the **Diwani of Bengal (1765)**
- **Export surplus:** India's large **export surplus** (and Indian revenue) brought no gold or silver - paid for British office expenses, war expenses (**war and military expenditure**), invisible imports
# 2. Components (home charges and more)
| **Component** | **What it covered** |
|---|---|
| **Home Charges** | Secretary of State's India Office costs in London, paid from Indian revenue (CDI) |
| **Interest** | On the public debt raised in Britain [GK] |
| **Salaries and pensions** | Of British civil and military officials (salaries, savings and **pensions**), remitted home (UPSC notes, CDI) |
| **Guaranteed railway returns** | Assured interest (**5%**[GK]) to British railway investors, whether lines earned or not |
| **Military expenditure** | India paid for the British Indian Army and imperial wars (Afghanistan, Burma) - the largest single budget head [GK]; war expenses |
| **Profits** | Of **British capitalists**, merchants and banks sent home |

- **Unequal trade:** also a component of the drain
- **Drain reason per Naoroji (APPSC-GS 2024):** **"profits taken by British capitalists from India"** (**Profits of British capitalists** = APPSC 2024 answer)
  - ⚠ **Trap:** NOT an excess of imports
# 3. Effects
- **Effects:**
  - Stifled capital formation and industrialisation
  - Shattered the myth of British benevolence
  - Moderates demanded lower land revenue, abolition of **salt tax**, **protective tariffs**, cuts in military spending
> Exam angle: "popularised by" = Naoroji (APPSC 2025); reason-for-drain options (APPSC 2024).

## Deindustrialisation — chronology, causes and results | from: S2
# 1. One-way free trade and chronology
- **One-way free trade:** After the **Charter Act 1813** ended the Company's monopoly
  - Machine-made British goods entered India at low duty (almost duty-free)
  - **Heavy duties** blocked Indian manufactures in Britain (England)
- **Chronology:**
  - Weavers coerced by Company agents (Bolt, 1772)
  - Lancashire cloth first offered in Bengal **1786**
  - By **1793** Indian calicoes and muslins displaced
  - By **1850** India imported **one-fourth** of all British cotton exports
# 2. Causes (B.D. Basu's list)
| **Cause** | **How it hit Indian industry** |
|---|---|
| **Patronage loss** | Loss of princely/royal patrons as native states fell |
| **Trade policy** | Free trade forced on India; flood of cheap machine goods; tariffs in Britain |
| **Raw materials** | Export of raw materials (raw cotton) |
| **Duties** | Internal **transit and customs duties** |
| **British privileges** | Special privileges for British traders; monopolies of the Company undercut Indian entrepreneurs |
| **Railways** | Carried raw materials in and British goods inland/out |
| **Other** | Artisans forced to divulge trade secrets; exhibitions |
# 3. Results
- **Collapse:** Town handicrafts suffered a "**sudden and complete collapse**"
  - Marx: the British "inundated the very mother country of cotton with cottons"
- **Overcrowding of agriculture:** Artisans pushed onto the land → disguised unemployment
  - The 1793 Act and famines pushed weavers into agriculture or wage labour

  - Economy **agrarian**
  - **Deindustrialisation** by British policy
  - India had a **strong industrial base** before British rule
  - Agriculture stagnant with low productivity; industry declined from loss of markets
# 2. Indicators (1947)
| **Indicator (1947)** | **Value (CDI)** |
|---|---|
| Per-capita income | **Rs 265** a year (~**$60**) |
| Life expectancy | **32 years** |
| Literacy | **under 16%** (female under 9%) |
| Workforce in agriculture | **over 75%**, giving only about **50% of GDP** |
# 3. Sectors
- **Agrarian base:** over 75% of the workforce in agriculture, yet only about 50% of GDP → disguised unemployment, low productivity
- **Industry:**
  - **Cotton mills** (Indian-owned) in **Maharashtra** and **Gujarat**
  - **Jute mills** (foreign-owned) in **Bengal**
  - Iron and steel from the early 20th century (**TISCO, 1907**)
  - **No capital-goods base**; basic/capital goods only ~**one-fourth** of industrial output
  - Public sector limited to railways, power, communications, ports
- **Trade:**
  - Exports: primary products (raw silk, cotton, wool, sugar, indigo, jute)
  - Imports: finished consumer and capital goods
  - **Suez Canal (1869)** tightened British control
- **British capital:** before **1914** about **97%** went to government projects, plantations and finance houses
# 4. Immediate challenges
- **Immediate challenges:**
  - Large foodgrain imports and inflation → **First Plan** gave top priority to **agriculture, irrigation, power**
  - Post-partition stabilisation
- ⚠ **Cotton mills** = Indian-owned; **jute mills** = foreign-owned

## Universal angles | from: S9 | universal
- **Significance:**
  - Drain theory = the Moderates' greatest contribution + base of Swadeshi

## Famine policy — relief, Temple wage and Famine Code 1883 | from: S1
# 1. Relief policy
- **Company period:** Government **never adopted a famine code**; minimal relief
- **Public works as relief:** Employment on roads and canals
  - Idea goes back to Kautilya's famine duties - seeds, food, tax exemption, public works
- **Temple wage:** Ration of about **1 lb of grain** a day on relief works
  - Criticised as a starvation wage
# 2. Famine Code, 1883 - provisions
- **Famine Code, 1883:** Framed on the **Strachey Commission (1880)** recommendations
- **Provisions:**
  - Stages of scarcity and famine
  - **Relief works** for the able-bodied
  - **Gratuitous relief** for the old and infirm
  - Suspension or remission of revenue
  - A **Famine Relief and Insurance Fund**
- ⚠ **Relief works** = able-bodied; **gratuitous relief** = old and infirm

## Wood's Despatch, 1854 — epithet and provisions | from: S2
- **Epithet:** **"Magna Carta of English Education in India"**
- **Provisions:**
  - Graded hierarchy: **vernacular primary schools** at the base → Anglo-vernacular high schools → affiliated colleges → universities
  - **Grants-in-aid** system to encourage private (largely missionary) enterprise
  - Universities in presidency towns on the London University model: **Calcutta, Bombay, Madras (1857)**
  - Government supervision of vernacular education; **training of teachers**
  - English for higher + vernacular for lower education
